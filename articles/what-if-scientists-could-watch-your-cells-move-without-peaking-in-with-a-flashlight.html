What if scientists could watch your cells move without peaking in with a flashlight?

2026-10-213 min

A new microscope at Stanford lets scientists watch living cells in real time without fluorescent labels -- the tiny tags that can change how cells behave. It works like scattering light off invisible LEGO bricks inside a moving toy house.

The flashlight that changes the cell

For decades, scientists who wanted to watch what was happening inside a living cell had only one option: stick a fluorescent label on it. Think of these labels like tiny glow-sticks glued to the parts you want to see. They make the cell light up, but they also weigh down the very structures they are meant to highlight.

That creates a problem. When you tag too many parts at once, the tags bump into each other and blur the picture. The glow also fades the longer you watch (scientists call this "photobleaching"), and some labels can make the cell behave differently than it normally would.

Watching cells the way they really act

A team at Stanford University has built a microscope that skips the glow-sticks entirely. They call it Interferometric Image Scanning Microscopy, or iISM for short, and it works a bit like the trick that makes the sky look blue.

A blue sky is blue because sunlight bounces off air molecules and tiny dust particles, and blue light scatters more strongly than red light. The Stanford microscope uses the same idea -- but on a much, much smaller scale. A laser shines on a living cell, and the tiny structures inside it (organelles, membranes, and so on) scatter some of that light. The microscope catches the scattered light and turns it into a sharp, real-time image.

Building a picture without touching

The key word is label-free. Because the scientists never have to stick anything onto the cell, the cell can move, change shape, and respond to its environment almost as if the microscope were not even there.

They can follow dozens of structures at once over long stretches of time -- watching a cell divide, a virus sneak in, or a drug take effect, all without disturbing the scene. At the same time, the technique needs far less light than other label-free methods, which means the cells stay healthier for longer.

Why this matters for medicine

So far this is a laboratory tool, but it points toward questions that have been hard to answer. How do drugs actually travel through a cell? What happens to a virus the moment it enters? By combining the detail of fluorescent tagging (for molecular identity) with the gentle, whole-picture view of iISM, scientists may soon watch infections and treatments unfold in living tissue like never before.

Try it yourself: Shine a flashlight across a glass of water in a dark room and sprinkle just a pinch of glitter into it. The glitter particles are like the structures inside a cell -- they catch and scatter the light. Tilt the glass slowly and watch how the scattered glitter shifts position as they move through the water. That shifting pattern is a simple model of what a label-free microscope captures (minus all the atoms).

Fun fact: The senior scientist behind the new microscope, W.E. Moerner, won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for making things glow in the first place -- he helped invent super-resolved fluorescence microscopy. Now his lab has built a microscope that does the opposite: seeing clearly while leaving the light off.
