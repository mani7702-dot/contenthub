How does exercise keep our muscles young?

2026-10-214 min

Muscles get weaker as we grow older — but exercise can turn back the clock. Inside every muscle cell, a tiny gene called DEAF1 goes into overdrive with age, clogging things up. Exercise helps clear the mess.

How does exercise keep our muscles young?

You have probably noticed it: when you are small, climbing a playground ladder feels effortless. As the years pile on, the same climb takes more of a push. Muscles do not stay strong on their own — they need exercise to stay young. But why does that happen? What is going on inside your muscles while you rest?

The answer lives inside your cells, where tiny genes act like little maintenance workers deciding what to build and what to clean up.

Muscles need a constant spring-clean

Every muscle in your body is made of millions of tiny living cells. Inside each cell, proteins are always being made — new, fresh ones to replace the ones that wear out. But the cells also need to clean up the old, damaged ones. If the cleanup slows down, the broken proteins pile up like clutter in a room nobody tidies.

Scientists call this balance the protein turnover. Too much building without enough clearing, and the cell gets clogged. That is one big reason muscles weaken with age.

The gene that goes into overdrive

Researchers found that a gene called DEAF1 is the troublemaker. In young, healthy muscles, DEAF1 behaves itself — it helps run things at a calm, steady pace. But as muscles grow older, DEAF1 levels shoot up. It is like a volume knob turned too high.

Too much DEAF1 pushes a control center called mTORC1 into overdrive. mTORC1 is the cell's main switch for building proteins. When it is cranked too high, the muscle spends all its energy making new proteins and forgets to clean up the old, broken ones. The waste piles up. The cell gets tired. The muscle gets weaker.

The body's built-in brake

Normally, a family of proteins called FOXOs keeps DEAF1 in check. Think of them as the brake pedals. When everything is running smoothly, the FOXOs hold DEAF1 back, and mTORC1 stays balanced — building and clearing in equal measure.

The problem? FOXO activity drops as we age. The brakes get weaker. Without them, DEAF1 races ahead, and mTORC1 goes too far. The cleanup system slows down.

How exercise hits the reset button

Here is the good news: exercise can fix it. The researchers discovered that physical activity turns on regulatory proteins that dial DEAF1 back down. It is like pressing the brake pedal again.

With DEAF1 quieted, mTORC1 returns to a healthier rhythm. Cells start clearing out the damaged proteins again while still building the fresh ones they need. The muscle remembers how to renew itself.

This is why even a single walk, a quick bike ride, or climbing the stairs at school can make your muscles feel stronger — your cells are doing their cleanup, right on cue.

Try this at home

Next time you climb stairs or ride your bike, think of each movement as a signal to your genes. Sit down right now and make a tight fist with one hand, hold it for five seconds, then relax. Do it ten times. You just told those tiny maintenance workers to get to work.

Fun Fact

Fruit flies and mice were the first to show this DEAF1 trick — but the same basic genes run almost every animal's cells, including yours. Scientists think the pathway works the same way in people, which is why staying active matters at every age.
