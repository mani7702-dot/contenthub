Why are birds more afraid of women than men — and what does it mean for city birds?

2026-10-223 min

In cities across Europe, urban birds consistently fly away sooner when a woman approaches than when a man does — and scientists have no idea why. A new study of 37 bird species and over 3,000 encounters reveals the same surprising pattern from the Czech Republic to Spain, leaving researchers puzzled about what the birds are noticing.

Why are city birds more afraid of women than men?

1. A surprising pattern in city parks

You have probably seen pigeons, sparrows, or robins in a city park. These urban birds get used to people — they often let us get fairly close before they fly away. But a new study found something strange: these same birds fly away sooner when approached by women than when approached by men.

Scientists in five European countries — Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain — walked toward birds in city parks and measured how close they could get before the bird took off. On average, birds gave women about one meter less space than they did men. In other words, the birds flew away sooner when a woman was approaching.

2. What the scientists checked

The researchers made sure their test was fair: they matched each woman with a man of the same height, wearing the same shoes and clothing style. They walked at the same speed and followed the same path. They even tested 37 different bird species — from great tits and house sparrows to magpies and pigeons — and the same pattern held every time.

They measured something scientists call flight initiation distance — the distance at which a bird decides it is time to fly away. The study was published in the journal People and Nature, run by the British Ecological Society.

3. Why can't scientists explain it?

This is the puzzling part: the researchers could not find a clear reason for the difference. Humans can look very different in height, clothing, movement, and voice — but the birds reacted differently even when men and women looked similar.

Professor Daniel Blumstein from the University of California, Los Angeles, said the results were real and repeatable, even though the team could not yet explain them. The birds seemed to be picking up on something — a movement, a scent, a shadow — that humans have not figured out yet.

This tells us that birds are paying attention to us in ways we do not fully understand. They may be reading cues from our faces, our walk, or even how we smell — details too subtle for humans to notice.

Why it matters

Understanding how birds react to people helps us protect them. City birds live alongside millions of humans every day, and small changes — like quieter footsteps or slower, gentler movements — can help birds feel safer in places we share.

Try this at home

Next time you see a city bird — a sparrow, pigeon, or robin — stand still and slowly raise one hand. The bird will probably hop or fly away when you are still several meters away. Now have an adult stand next to you and do the same. Can you notice if the bird reacts differently? Remember: birds are wild friends who need space. Watch quietly, then let them go about their day.

Fun Fact

Urban birds can remember hundreds of individual faces — both human faces and the faces of other birds. In one famous experiment, wild crows in Japan learned to recognize people who had caught and released them, scolding those same people years later. The crows even taught their babies to recognize the faces too!
