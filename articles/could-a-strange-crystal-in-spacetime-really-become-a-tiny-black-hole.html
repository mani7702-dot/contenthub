Could a Strange 'Crystal' in Spacetime Really Become a Tiny Black Hole?

2026-10-314 min

Black holes are usually pictured as giant cosmic monsters. Now two teams of physicists have worked out the exact maths behind something far stranger — a microscopic black hole that can be born from a repeating 'crystal' pattern hidden in space and time itself.

Could a Strange 'Crystal' in Spacetime Really Become a Tiny Black Hole?

When you hear "black hole," you probably picture a colossal vacuum cleaner of a thing, millions of times heavier than the Sun, quietly swallowing stars at the heart of a galaxy. But here is a secret physicists have known for a century: nothing in Albert Einstein's equations says a black hole has to be big. Under exactly the right conditions, one could be smaller than an atom.

The puzzle has always been how such a thing could form. Now researchers at Goethe University Frankfurt and TU Wien have taken a big step toward an answer. They have written down the first exact recipe for something called "critical collapse" — the razor's-edge moment when space and time either settle down or snap into a black hole.

The water-freezing trick

Think about water at exactly zero degrees Celsius. Nudge it the tiniest bit colder and something dramatic happens: the molecules suddenly lock together into the ordered pattern of ice. A tiny cause, a huge effect.

Physicists found that spacetime can behave in a surprisingly similar way. Under very specific conditions, the geometry of space and time stops being chaotic and organises itself into a repeating pattern — a "spacetime crystal." Give that pattern a whisper more energy, and according to the maths, it can collapse into a microscopic black hole. Give it a whisper less, and it simply dissolves.

Why the maths matters

Computer simulations had hinted this was possible for years, but a real formula explaining it had stayed out of reach. To crack it, the team used an unusual trick: they imagined gravity living in a world with an enormous number of dimensions, solved the problem there, and then translated the answer back into our familiar four. The result, published in Physical Review Letters, matches what the simulations showed all along.

A window onto the early universe

These tiny, "primordial" black holes might not just be a mathematical curiosity. Conditions in the first moments after the Big Bang were hot, dense and wildly chaotic — exactly the setting where a spacetime crystal could have formed and collapsed. Some scientists even wonder whether leftover primordial black holes could be hiding in plain sight today.

Fun Fact

If you could squeeze the entire Earth down to the size of a marble, it would become a black hole. A black hole is not really a "thing" — it is just a place where gravity has bent spacetime so steeply that nothing, not even light, can climb back out.
