What are the secret nurseries where baby stars are born in Orion?

2026-10-213 min

James Webb snapped a flash of new infrared light from a dusty corner of Orion the Hunter — a stellar nursery where baby stars are still gathering in glowing disks and shooting out high-speed jets.

A nursery that no eye can see

When stargazers point a small telescope at Orion the Hunter, the first thing that jumps out is the fuzzy middle of Orion's sword — the Orion Nebula. It glows because newborn stars bathe it in ultraviolet light.

But that bright cloud is just the front door. Behind it, shrouded in thick darkness, is a whole family of hidden nurseries. They are called the Orion Molecular Clouds, and one of the tiniest parts, OMC-2, was just shown to us by the James Webb Space Telescope.

James Webb used its superpower: it sees infrared light, which swooshes right through cold gas and dust like a flashlight through fog. The picture it revealed is 2.5 light-years wide and 1.5 light-years tall — a region so big that if you squinted, our whole Solar System would float inside it like a pebble.

Where do stars begin?

Inside OMC-2, gravity is winning a tug-of-war. A clump of cold gas begins to collapse under its own pull. As it spins faster and faster, the center gets denser and denser — that is the future protostar, still a baby and millions of years away from true stardom.

Around it, the leftover gas and dust flattens into a whirling accretion disk. Magnetic fields loop through the disk and, near the baby star, channel some of that material into narrow jets that shoot out along the star's rotation axis — like a cosmic firehose.

Those jets race through space at hundreds of thousands of miles per hour. When they ram into the slow, dusty gas around them, they crash, heat up, and glow. Webb's infrared eye caught that exact glow: sharp-edged streaks in red, white, and gold, some pointing toward us, others away.

Peeking into the Integral-Shaped Filament

This slice sits inside a larger, famous structure called the Integral-Shaped Filament — because at radio and infrared wavelengths it curves across the sky like a giant backwards "S", the same shape as the mathematical symbol for an integral: ∫.

The bright Orion Nebula sits almost in the middle of that S. Just behind it is OMC-1, and to the north rise OMC-2 and OMC-3, while OMC-4 lies to the south. OMC-2 is close enough — only 1,270 light-years away — that Webb can study each baby star individually.

Most of the youngest protostars there are still completely hidden inside thick dusty cocoons. Not even Webb can pierce those. But the older babies have already cleared a hollow around themselves, and their glowing jets light up the dark — a spectacular record of how our own Sun probably began, 4.6 billion years ago.

Fun fact: The four bright stars at the heart of the Orion Nebula form the Trapezium Cluster. Their fierce ultraviolet light is what makes the nebula itself shine.

Try this at home

On a crisp autumn night, learn to find Orion's belt — three bright stars in a short line. The fuzzy patch just below the belt is the Orion Nebula, visible even in twilight. With a simple pair of binoculars, you are looking straight into a neighborhood where dozens of baby stars are still being born.
