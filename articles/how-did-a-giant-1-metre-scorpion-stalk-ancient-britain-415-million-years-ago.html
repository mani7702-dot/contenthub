How did a giant 1-metre scorpion stalk ancient Britain 415 million years ago?

A fossil from the dinosaur age's distant, stranger ancestor

Long before dinosaurs roamed the Earth — even before the first trees had grown — a predator the size of a large dog stalked what is now England and Wales. This was no ordinary creature: it was a scorpion, but not the tiny desert kind you might picture. This one stretched over a metre from tail to pincers, with claws more than 16 centimetres long. Imagine a grown-up golden retriever, except covered in armour and carrying a stinger on its back!

Scientists have just confirmed that fossils sitting quietly in museum collections for more than 150 years belong to the largest scorpion we know of: Praearcturus gigas.

Rediscovering giants in old drawers

You might think finding a giant scorpion would be exciting enough the first time. But these fossils had been labelled and catalogued decades ago, then largely forgotten. It took modern science — sharper microscopes, 3D imaging, and fresh comparisons with newly discovered species — to realise what they had all along.

Dr. Richard J. Howard from the Natural History Museum in London explained that when people imagine giant bugs from the past, they usually picture Carboniferous rainforests full of enormous millipedes and dragonflies. But Praearcturus lived at least 50 million years earlier, when the land was still very new — no trees, no ferns, just simple plants and fungi.

"Confirming that this animal is a scorpion fundamentally changes our understanding of how and when these creatures evolved to such extraordinary sizes," Dr. Howard said.

Why grow so large, so early?

That's the big question. Later giant arthropods are often linked to high oxygen levels in the Carboniferous period. But Praearcturus lived long before those oxygen-rich forests. So what made it grow so big?

Dr. Russell Garwood from the University of Manchester suggested that the answer might be surprisingly simple: water. Life in the oceans was already much bigger than anything on land. If this giant scorpion spent time in shallow seas or muddy waters, it could tap into ecosystems where enormous creatures were already the norm.

By comparing Praearcturus with other animals alive at the time — tiny early fish, primitive millipedes, and the first adventurous worms and insects — the researchers found that the scorpion was a true heavyweight of its day. While most land creatures were still the size of a thumb, this armoured predator dwarfed them all.

What does this tell us?

This discovery pushes the story of giant arthropods much further back in time. It shows that evolution was already experimenting with "big" when the recipe was still being written — proving that sometimes, the most extraordinary chapters in Earth's story are the ones we almost miss.

Fun Fact

Praearcturus gigas lived during the Early Devonian period — the same geological age known as the "Age of Fishes" for the oceans, but when life on land was just barely beginning. It's older than the first fish that crawled onto land by nearly 50 million years.
