How did scientists reach the ultimate quantum limit right on a crystal surface?

2026-10-253 min

Scientists created an ultra-clean crystal surface so still that individual molecules sitting on it kept their quantum properties for the maximum time physics allows — the 'Fourier limit' — a first for molecules anchored to a surface.

1. How did scientists reach the ultimate quantum limit right on a crystal surface?

Why surfaces are so tricky for quantum tricks

Imagine you have a tiny marble that can glow in two colors at once — but only if nothing bumps it. That is basically what a quantum emitter is: a single molecule or atom that can hold multiple states simultaneously and glow with quantum light. These tiny systems are the building blocks of future quantum computers and unbreakable quantum messages.

The catch? These emitters are incredibly delicate. Just a single stray gas molecule, a little heat, or a speck of dust is enough to "wake them up" and destroy their quantum magic. Scientists call this loss of magic decoherence — like a spinning top that falls over the moment it hits a bump in the floor.

To study these emitters, scientists often trap them in a vacuum or embed them inside solids. But what if you could place them right on a surface? That would let researchers "touch" them with ultra-sharp tips and control them with incredible precision — the dream for future quantum devices.

The problem is that surfaces are never clean enough. Contaminants from the air stick to them, vibrations shake them, and heat jiggles the atoms — all of which destroy the fragile quantum states.

A crystal that cleans itself

The team at the Max Planck Institute found a clever trick using a special organic crystal. At room temperature, this crystal material does something magical: it slowly sublimes — it turns directly from a solid into a gas without becoming a liquid, like dry ice. As the top layers of the crystal evaporate, they sweep away any dirt and contaminants sitting on the surface.

Once the crystal was sparkling clean, the scientists dropped it into a cryostat — an ultra-cold chamber that brings the temperature to just a few degrees above absolute zero (the coldest temperature possible). At that extreme cold, the crystal stops moving. The atoms practically freeze in place, and the surface becomes as still as a pond on a windless night.

Then, they gently deposited individual molecules onto the frozen crystal — like placing a single drop of ink on a perfectly still lake.

Molecules that hit the quantum speed limit

With their pristine surface ready, the scientists watched single molecules sitting on the crystal, glowing and losing their quantum energy. They measured exactly how long each molecule could stay "quantum" before its coherence disappeared.

Every single molecule hit what scientists call the Fourier limit — the absolute maximum time physics allows. Think of it like a sprinter who breaks the world record by the tiniest possible margin. Beyond this limit, it is physically impossible to keep the quantum state any longer — it's a fundamental law, kind of like the universal speed limit for light.

In a noisy environment, coherence vanishes hundreds or even thousands of times faster than this limit. But on the crystal surface, the environment was so quiet and still that the molecules kept their quantum magic for exactly as long as the laws of physics would allow.

This is the first time anyone has seen molecules anchored to a surface reach this fundamental quantum limit. It is like tuning a radio to a perfectly clear station — no static, no interference, just pure signal.

What's next?

By combining their ultra-clean surface technique with powerful microscopes (STM and AFM), the researchers hope to manipulate individual quantum emitters with precision measured in billionths of a meter. Such control could one day lead to:

  • Quantum sensors so sensitive they can detect a single molecule of gas
  • Single-photon sources for ultra-secure quantum communication
  • Molecular quantum processors where individual atoms and molecules act as quantum bits

The future of quantum technology might not come from fancy vacuums or exotic materials — it might come from a crystal that cleans itself and a surface so still, it lets molecules stay quantum for exactly as long as physics allows.

Try this at home

You can't reach near-absolute zero at home, but you can see how a quiet environment matters for delicate things! Try this: Place a marble on a smooth kitchen counter and give it a gentle spin. Watch how long it keeps rolling. Now try the same spin on a towel full of bumps. Which surface lets the marble "dance" longer? The counter is like the ultra-clean crystal — quiet enough to preserve motion. The towel is like a dirty surface — too noisy for delicate things to survive.

Fun Fact

The Fourier limit is closely related to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle — one of the most famous ideas in quantum physics. It says you can't know a quantum system's energy and the exact time it will release that energy perfectly at the same time. The more precisely you try to pin down one, the fuzzier the other becomes. It's a built-in limit of the universe, written into the mathematical fabric of reality itself.

A team at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light (Erlangen, Germany), led by Prof. Vahid Sandoghdar, built an ultra-clean organic crystal surface inside a cryostat (near-absolute-zero, ~1–4 K). At room temperature the crystal slowly sublimates (passes directly from solid to gas), sweeping away surface contaminants. Once cooled near absolute zero, fresh molecules were gently evaporated onto the pristine surface. The researchers then monitored individual quantum emitters (fluorescent molecules) sitting on the surface and measured their coherence times — how long each molecule keeps its "quantumness." In a dirty or "noisy" environment, decoherence strikes within picoseconds to nanoseconds, collapsing quantum states hundreds to thousands of times faster than the Fourier limit. The team's crystal surface reached the Fourier limit for every emitter tested, proving the environment was quiet enough to preserve quantum coherence for the maximum physically permitted duration.
