Could Coconut Oil Power the Next Generation of Jet Fuel?

From Kitchen Shelf to Cockpit

Coconut oil is a familiar sight in kitchens around the world — perfect for frying, great for skin, and a staple in many healthy recipes. But could this tropical oil one day help power the passenger jets that zoom overhead?

Scientists at Osaka Metropolitan University in Japan have taken leftover coconut oil — the kind discarded after extracting the stuff we cook with — and transformed it into a sustainable aviation fuel. In groundbreaking experiments, they mixed their coconut-based fuel with conventional jet fuel and tested it in a real micro jet engine. The results were promising on several fronts.

The Clever Chemistry Behind the Fuel

The research team created two types of biofuel from coconut oil, each using a different alcohol. One used methanol and was called FAME; the other used ethanol and was called FAEE. Both were produced through a special co-solvent method that mixes coconut oil extracts with acetone and alcohol.

The advantage? Unlike traditional methods that require high pressure and intense heating, this new technique works at room temperature and uses only gentle heat. That means less energy consumed during production and a smaller carbon footprint from the start. Even better, the process also produces glycerin and biodiesel as useful byproducts, while the acetone and alcohol can be recovered and reused.

Engine Tests Show Promise

When the team blended their coconut biofuels with Jet A-1 (standard aviation fuel) and ran them through a small turbojet engine, the results were encouraging:

  • Thermal efficiency — how well the engine turns fuel into useful work — stayed comparable to pure jet fuel.
  • Unburned hydrocarbon emissions dropped by 5% to 40%, depending on the blend ratio. These are the "leftover" chemicals that contribute to air pollution, especially when planes are idling on runways.
  • CO2 and nitric oxide levels remained on par with conventional jet fuel.

The main trade-off? Fuel consumption edged up slightly, because coconut oil releases a bit less energy per unit than pure kerosene. But the team believes this can be improved with further research.

A Fuel for Coconut-Producing Regions

Coconut oil stands out because its natural fatty acid chains are already close in length to the hydrocarbons found in jet fuel. That means it needs less processing than many other plant oils before it can be blended into aviation fuel.

For countries that grow coconuts, this could be a win-win — turning agricultural waste into clean fuel while reducing reliance on fossil fuels.

The researchers stress that their coconut-based fuel is not ready to fully replace jet fuel just yet. The experiments used blends, not 100% biofuel. But the encouraging engine results suggest that coconuts might soon help push passenger planes toward a more sustainable future.

Fun Fact

Did you know? It takes about 14 to 15 coconuts to produce one liter of coconut oil — but a single coconut tree can produce up to 7,500 coconuts in its lifetime. That means there's a lot of potential fuel hidden in just one productive tree!
