What happened when a 160-meter asteroid hit the North Sea and made a 330-foot tsunami

2026-10-223 min

About 43 to 46 million years ago a 160-meter-wide asteroid slammed into the North Sea, blasting a crater 3 km wide and sending a tsunami over 100 meters high crashing across the waves. New evidence has finally settled a 20-year-old debate about the mysterious Silverpit Crater.

What happened when a giant asteroid hit the ocean and made a monster wave?

A crater hidden under 700 meters of seabed

About 43 to 46 million years ago, something from space slammed into the North Sea with unimaginable force. The object? A 160-meter-wide asteroid — roughly the length of a football field — traveling at 15 kilometers per second (that is about 33,000 miles per hour).

The collision carved out a crater 3 kilometers wide and sent a tsunami over 100 meters (330 feet) high crashing across the waves. And here is the wild part: that crater is still there today, buried 700 meters beneath the seabed, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. Scientists call it the Silverpit Crater.

For over 20 years, scientists have debated whether Silverpit was really made by an asteroid — or by slow-moving salt deep underground. Now, a team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson from Heriot-Watt University has found the smoking gun that settles the question once and for all.

The tiny crystals that tell the truth

The key evidence came from microscopic mineral crystals found in rock cuttings from an oil well drilled near the crater. These are shocked quartz and feldspar — minerals whose crystal lattices have been shattered by the kind of extreme pressure that only happens during a cosmic impact. Think of it like a snowflake that got crushed by a hammer: the pattern of cracks is unique and can only be made by a hypervelocity collision.

"The seismic data gave us an unprecedented look at the crater," says Dr. Nicholson. "But the shocked minerals found at the crater floor depth proved the impact beyond any doubt."

Rebuilding the ancient disaster

With the crater's origin confirmed, the scientists used computer simulations to replay the moment of impact. Their model shows that the asteroid struck the seabed at a shallow angle from the west, violently displacing rock and seawater.

The result? A towering 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that arced into the sky — that is nearly a mile high — before collapsing back into the ocean. The splash that followed generated a tsunami over 100 meters tall. To picture that, imagine a wave 30 times taller than a house.

Unlike ordinary wind waves, tsunamis move entire oceans. The Silverpit tsunami would have raced across the North Sea, its energy moving through the full depth of the water, far taller and more powerful than anything seaside communities experience today.

Why this matters

Earth has been hit by countless asteroids over its 4.5-billion-year history, but almost all traces have been erased. Wind, rain, and flowing rocks slowly wear down craters. Even more dramatically, Earth's tectonic plates — giant rocky slabs that slowly crawl like a conveyor belt — pull old seafloor apart and swallow it back into the planet's interior. That is why fewer than 33 ocean impact craters have been confirmed so far.

Silverpit is special because it was sealed and preserved in sediment layers, like a message in a bottle from the deep past. By studying it, scientists learn how asteroids shape worlds — and what might happen if another one ever came our way.

Try this at home

Fill a baking tray with sand and pour water on top. Now drop a small rock from a few feet up. Watch the crater that forms and the waves that radiate outward. The bigger you make the rock or the higher you drop it, the bigger the splash — just like the ancient asteroid that made Silverpit.

Fun Fact

The Silverpit Crater's central peak — the "bullseye" at its center — formed when the ground bounced back upward after the impact, like a trampoline snapping back after you jump on it. This same process created the moon's circular craters that you can see through a telescope!
