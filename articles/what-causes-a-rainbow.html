What causes a rainbow?

2026-10-194 min

No paint, no magic — just sunlight, a lot of falling raindrops, and light bending and bouncing inside each one. Here is how a rainbow is really made.

A rainbow looks too perfect to be real — a neat arch of color hung in the sky like a painted doorway. But it is not painted, and it is not an object sitting in one place. A rainbow is something your eyes build out of sunlight and falling water, and once you know the recipe, you will never look at one the same way again.

Start with white light that isn't white

The recipe starts with the Sun. The light streaming from the Sun looks white, but white light is actually all the colors of the rainbow mixed together, zipping along side by side. Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet — they are all in there. We just cannot see them as separate colors until something pulls them apart.

That "something" is a raindrop.

What you need to stand in

Before anything can happen, everything has to line up in a very particular order:

  • The Sun must be behind you.
  • The rain (or mist, or fog, or sea spray) must be in front of you.

That is why rainbows so often pop out right after a storm, when the Sun breaks through and there is still rain in the distance. You have your back to the Sun and your face to the wet part of the sky. If the Sun were in front of you instead, you would never see the bow — you would only see raindrops lit from the front.

What happens inside a single raindrop

Here is the surprising part: a whole rainbow is built out of millions of raindrops, each doing the same three-step dance with sunlight.

Step 1 — The light bends as it enters. When a ray of sunlight crosses from air into water, it slows down and bends. This bending is called refraction. (You have seen refraction if you ever slipped a straw into a glass of water and watched it look broken at the surface.)

Step 2 — The colors split apart. This is the magic step. Every color of light bends by a slightly different amount when it enters the water. Violet light bends the most, red the least. So a single beam of white sunlight goes into the drop together, and comes out fanned into a spectrum — its colors separated and spread out. The same thing happens with a glass prism, which is exactly why a prism makes that little rainbow stripe on the wall. A raindrop is just a very round, very tiny prism.

Step 3 — The light bounces off the back, then bends again on the way out. After splitting apart inside the drop, the light hits the far inside wall and reflects (bounces) back, then bends a second time — refracting again as it leaves the water and re-enters the air. That second bend spreads the colors even further apart.

The result is a beam of light coming back out of each raindrop with its colors laid side by side, red to violet. Collect that from trillions of raindrops at once, and your eyes read a rainbow.

Why it is an arch (and a red-outside one)

Not every ray comes back equally. Most of the light that reaches your eyes from the drops has bounced just once inside, and it comes back at a very specific angle — about 42 degrees from the direction opposite the Sun. Because that angle is measured all the way around in a circle, the rainbow shows up as a great arc across the sky. From the ground you usually only see the upper half, but from an airplane you can sometimes see the whole circle.

And there is a tidy rule about the color order: because red bends the least, it ends up along the top, outer edge of the bow, while violet — which bends the most — lands along the bottom, inner edge. So every rainbow has red on the outside and violet on the inside.

The faint second bow

Sometimes, if the sky is very clear, you can spot a second rainbow higher up and fainter, with its colors flipped — red on the inside this time. That extra bow is made by light that bounced twice inside the raindrops instead of once. That double bounce throws the colors into reverse order and sends them to your eyes at a wider angle, around 50 degrees, which is why the second bow floats outside the first one. Because so much light is lost in that second bounce, it always looks dimmer.

It is not really "there"

Here is the strangest truth of all: a rainbow does not exist in any one spot. There is no place in the sky you could fly to and touch the end of one. The bow depends entirely on where you are standing and where the Sun is shining. Move, and the rainbow moves with you — which is exactly why you can never reach its legendary pot of gold.

So the next time one appears after a rain, remember what you are looking at: a billion tiny glass prisms falling through the air, each one catching the sunlight, bending it, bouncing it, and handing your eyes the colors that were hidden inside white light the whole time.
