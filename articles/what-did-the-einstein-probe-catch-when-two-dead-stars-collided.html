What Did the Einstein Probe Catch When Two Dead Stars Collided?

2026-10-264 min

When two neutron stars crash into each other, they send a brief flash of gamma rays across the universe. But the Einstein Probe telescope spotted something surprising — a long, hidden afterglow of soft X-rays that older telescopes never saw before.

1. What did the Einstein Probe see when two dead stars crashed together?

The telescope that spotted a gamma-ray burst — and then kept watching for 10 minutes

Imagine two city-sized stellar corpses, each heavier than our Sun but crushed smaller than a city, spiraling around each other faster and faster. Then — crack — they smash together in the most violent explosion since the Big Bang itself. For a split second, a brilliant flash of gamma rays zips across the universe. To most telescopes, that flash is all they ever see.

But the Einstein Probe telescope saw something no one had ever caught before.

2. The half-second flash — and what happened next

The event, named EP250704a (or GRB 250704B to gamma-ray astronomers), happened on July 4, 2025. An Li, a PhD student at Beijing Normal University, was on duty when the telescope's soft X-ray camera caught a bright flash that lasted less than half a second. At the same time, gamma-ray satellites confirmed the brief burst.

Most telescopes would have moved on here. But the Einstein Probe's special wide-field camera kept watching. And what it saw next was a complete surprise:

Instead of fading away, the explosion kept glowing in soft X-rays for almost ten minutes — 1,200 times longer than the initial flash!

3. Why did no one see this before?

Here is the cosmic twist: that long afterglow was hiding in plain sight.

Previous telescopes — like NASA's Swift and Chandra — are built to catch bright, hard gamma-ray bursts. They depend on those initial gamma-ray flashes to point themselves in the right direction. Once the flash fades, the soft X-ray emission from the explosion fades too, becoming too faint for narrow-field telescopes to see.

The Einstein Probe is different. Its camera stares at a huge patch of sky all at once, so it keeps watching long after other telescopes look away. It was like having a camera that never blinks during a firework show — and catching details others miss.

"The prolonged emission was so soft that, for a burst at this typical distance, it would have stayed below the detection threshold of conventional gamma-ray instruments," explained Professor Bin-Bin Zhang from Nanjing University.

4. What powered this 10-minute glow?

Scientists think the answer lies in what is left behind after the crash. When two neutron stars merge, they can leave behind an ultradense, rapidly spinning, highly magnetized neutron star — a magnetar — that keeps pumping energy into space like a tiny cosmic lighthouse.

"This magnetar could explain why the system stayed active long after the brief gamma-ray flash ended," said Yi-Han Iris Yin, who led the analysis.

The discovery is a game-changer. Since 2017, when scientists first detected gravitational waves from a neutron star collision alongside its gamma-ray burst, they have been hunting for more. The Einstein Probe's soft X-ray vision may finally reveal what happens in those crucial minutes after the crash — a phase that has been invisible to all previous telescopes.

By revealing this hidden soft X-ray phase, the Einstein Probe opens an entirely new window for studying one of the universe's most dramatic events — and perhaps finally solving mysteries about what powers these cosmic smashups.

Now imagine they are made of super-dense stuff so heavy that a teaspoon of it would weigh as much as all of humanity! Keep rolling faster and faster... then CLAP your hands together once. Fun Fact

The Einstein Probe is named after Albert Einstein, whose theory of general relativity predicted that massive accelerating objects should send ripples through spacetime itself — but the telescope was launched more than 100 years after Einstein's death. It carries the world's largest soft X-ray telescope, scanning a patch of sky 3,600 times bigger than the full Moon!
