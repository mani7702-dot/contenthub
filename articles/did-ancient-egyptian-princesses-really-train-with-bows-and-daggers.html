Did Ancient Egyptian Princesses Really Train With Bows and Daggers?

2026-10-314 min

Bows, arrows, daggers and maces were found in the tombs of Egyptian princesses 4,000 years ago — but were they ceremonial gifts or tools of the trade? A fresh look at six royal mummies from Dahshur suggests these women actually trained with weapons.

Did Ancient Egyptian Princesses Really Train With Bows and Daggers?

1. A 4,000-Year-Old Puzzle Waiting in a Museum

In the 1890s, archaeologists digging at Dahshur — an ancient Egyptian burial complex south of Cairo filled with pyramids and deep shaft tombs — opened the tombs of several royal women. Inside, alongside jewellery and fine coffins, they found something unexpected: bows, arrows, daggers and maces, the kind of equipment usually buried with men.

For over a century, historians mostly assumed these were symbols — ceremonial objects placed in the grave to show status, not things the women had ever really used. Then the mummies slipped out of scholarly attention altogether, and it took a curatorial project at the Egyptian Museum in 2020 to track them down again.

That reunion set the stage for a brand-new study, published in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology, that asked a simple but powerful question: what do the bones themselves say?

2. Bones That Read Like a Training Diary

Led by Dr. Zeinab Hashesh, the team examined six royal mummies from Egypt's Late Middle Kingdom (roughly 1850–1700 BCE). Four of them were sisters and daughters of the pharaoh Amenemhat II.

When muscles are used hard and often, they leave marks on bone. And these women had them — pronounced ridges and robust attachment points on their upper limbs, exactly the pattern you'd expect from repetitive, high-intensity movements like drawing a bowstring or steadying a heavy weapon.

Princess Ita, who died between 28 and 34, showed particularly strong upper-body muscle attachments. Princess Khenmet had very robust ligament attachments. The evidence pointed to lives of real, habitual practice — hunting, archery and weapon training, not just posing.

3. Royal Sisters, Royal Lives

The skeletons told personal stories too. Princess Itaweret had survived broken ribs and foot fractures — injuries that healed well, hinting at skilled medical care. Several of the women also shared rare spinal abnormalities, a quiet clue that Egypt's royal families were closely related.

Dr. Hashesh put it plainly: these royals were "active participants in skilled, physically demanding activities such as archery and hunting."

4. What the Bones Couldn't Tell Us

Not every mystery is solved. The princesses' skulls were lost in the early 1900s, limiting some analyses, and the team says more work remains. Still, the study nudges one of Egypt's oldest debates — who got to be a warrior — a step closer to an answer.

Fun Fact

The weapons weren't the only surprise. Princess Ita's coffin also contained an especially ornate dagger — suggesting the finest craftsmanship of the day went into tools meant to be used, not merely admired.
