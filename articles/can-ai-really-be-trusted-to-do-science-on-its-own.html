Can AI really be trusted to do science on its own?

2026-10-283 min

AI is racing through maths, biology and crystal-hunting — but a growing number of scientists worry that speed comes at a cost: a crisis of trust. Can we tell real discovery from AI-generated fiction?

How do we know what AI 'discovers' is real?

Imagine a research assistant billions of times faster than any human — but one that sometimes makes up facts the way a daydreamer invents stories. That is the new reality facing science today.

1. A maths breakthrough — or a race with no finish line

In September 2026, a company called OpenAI announced it had tackled one of mathematics' grandest puzzles: the Navier–Stokes equations, a set of formulas that describe how fluids flow. Solving them is so hard that the Clay Mathematics Institute has offered a $1 million prize for a proof — and OpenAI's system reportedly did it using a swarm of roughly 10,000 AI agents.

But the celebration was short-lived. The solution resembled a spinning top tightening into a singularity — and it triggered a priority dispute. Two mathematicians, Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge of the rival firm Anthropic, had been working on closely related ideas. They raised questions about whether their drafts — kept inside OpenAI's own coding tool — may have influenced the final result.

OpenAI insisted nobody, human or AI, had seen the other team's work. It also said it would not claim the million-dollar prize, and the Clay Institute still lists the problem as "active."

2. Fake citations hiding in plain sight

Maths is only the beginning. Across biology, chemistry and medicine, researchers worry about a quieter threat: fake citations and phantom papers. One analysis found roughly 147,000 invented references sneaking into the scientific record last year alone — a tsunami of AI "slop" that could send future researchers chasing ghosts.

Peer review — science's quality-control system — is feeling the strain. Studies show up to 17% of referee reports now contain text substantially rewritten by language models. Journals are scrambling: some now use around 100 digital signals to flag suspicious submissions for human eyes.

3. The good, the bad, and the very uncertain

It is not all worry. The same AI boom that powers these debates also gave us AlphaFold, the tool that in 2020 cracked how proteins fold into their shapes — a feat so impressive that its creators won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Where traditional lab work would have taken a hundred million years, AlphaFold predicted 200 million protein folds in one year.

But even champions stumble. AI once announced over two million "new" crystal structures — enough to fill libraries. When independent chemists checked a sample, they found "yet to find any strikingly novel compounds." The lesson? AI can explore vast spaces, but humans must hold the leash.

Fun fact

The very word "hallucination," when applied to AI, is borrowed from psychology — where it means seeing something that is not there. In chatbots, it is confident but untrue talk.

Try this at home

Next time you ask a voice assistant a question, ask it something with a clear answer: "How many moons does Mars have?" (Phobos and Deimos — two.) Then ask something trickier. Notice how confident the reply sounds — even when it might be guessing. That is the AI equivalent of guessing on a test.
