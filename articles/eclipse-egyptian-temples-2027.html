5 Amazing Egyptian Temples That Will Sit in the Shadow of the 2027 'Eclipse of the Century'

When will a total solar eclipse turn day into night in ancient Egypt? On August 2, 2027, the Moon's shadow will sweep across a thin 160-mile (257 kilometer) path stretching right through the heart of Luxor — home to some of the world's most incredible ancient monuments. At its longest, the total eclipse will last 6 minutes and 23 seconds, making it the longest total solar eclipse visible on land anywhere in the 21st century until the year 2114!

What Is a Total Solar Eclipse?

A total solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes directly between Earth and the Sun, briefly blocking the Sun's bright face and turning day into twilight. This only happens along a narrow path on Earth — most of the time, the Moon's shadow misses our planet because the Moon's orbit is tilted. Everyone inside that special path sees totality; people just outside the path see a partial eclipse.

Fun fact: total solar eclipses occur somewhere on Earth every 18 months or so, but any single spot on Earth only gets to see one about once every 360 to 410 years!

1. Karnak Temple — The Hottest Eclipse Ticket

Karnak Temple on the east bank of Luxor is dedicated to Amun-Ra, a fusion of the hidden sun god Amun and the ancient sun god Ra. Built by ancient astronomers, the temple's design actually lines up with the sunrise — the first rays of the new day still reach the innermost sanctuary! During the 2027 eclipse, which begins at 1:02 p.m. Eastern European Summer Time, Karnak will sit almost directly in the path of totality for 6 minutes and 19 seconds. Historical cloud-cover data shows a 0% chance of clouds, so the view should be crystal clear.

2. Luxor Temple — The Obelisk Points to the Eclipse

Just across the Nile from Karnak, Luxor Temple is famous for its towering columns and intricate carvings. A 75-foot (23 meter) tall obelisk at the entrance will point almost straight at the eclipsed sun when totality peaks. The temple's missing twin obelisk now stands in Paris at the Place de la Concorde. Like Karnak, Luxor Temple will see 6 minutes and 19 seconds of totality at 1:02 p.m. EEST — and you can bet eclipse chasers from around the world will be fighting for a spot on the riverbank.

3. Valley of the Kings — Tombs in the Shadow

On the west bank of Luxor lies the Valley of the Kings, the ancient burial ground of the pharaohs. While you wouldn't want to be underground during the eclipse itself, the necropolis is home to spectacular tombs like KV17 (Seti I), KV62 (Tutankhamun), and KV9 (Ramesses V & VI). The valley will also experience 6 minutes and 19 seconds of totality starting at 1:01 p.m. EEST. Some tomb ceilings even have decorations that were designed to capture the stars and the sun — how fitting that they'll witness the sun disappear!

4. Mortuary Temple of Hatshepsut

The Mortuary Temple of Hatshepsut stands at the end of a long stone avenue beneath towering desert cliffs. Queen Hatshepsut built this magnificent temple over 1,400 years ago, and its three tiered terraces are carved directly into the limestone cliff. The temple will see 6 minutes and 19 seconds of totality at 1:01 p.m. EEST. With 0% cloud cover forecast, it's a prime eclipse-viewing spot — just bring shade and water for the August heat!

5. Temple of Horus at Edfu

South of Luxor in the city of Edfu, the Temple of Horus is one of the best-preserved ancient temples in Egypt. Built in the Ptolemaic era, it's dedicated to Horus, the falcon-headed god whose eye represents the sun. The temple gets a full 5 minutes and 20 seconds of totality at 1:04 p.m. EEST — just a bit less than Luxor, but still plenty of time to see the stars come out and the temperature drop as day turns to twilight.

What About the Pyramids of Giza?

Sorry, pyramid fans — the Great Pyramids of Giza sit just north of the eclipse path, so they'll only see a 95% partial eclipse (still impressive, but not total). The same goes for Abu Simbel, which will see 92% partial. If you want the full total eclipse experience in ancient Egypt, Luxor is your destination!

Try This at Home

Make your own eclipse viewer! Line up two pieces of cardboard — poke a small hole in one, hold it up to the sun (never look directly!), and project the sun's image onto the second sheet. During an eclipse, you'll see a crescent shape appear on the paper. Adults should supervise, and always wear certified eclipse glasses during the partial phases.

Fun Fact

The ancient Egyptians thought a solar eclipse was the sun god Ra being swallowed by the sky goddess Nut — and he had to fight his way back out again! Modern astronomers now know it's just the Moon doing its cosmic dance, but the mystery is still just as exciting.
