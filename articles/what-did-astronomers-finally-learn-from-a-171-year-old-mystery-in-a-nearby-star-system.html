What Did Astronomers Finally Learn from a 171-Year-Old Mystery in a Nearby Star System?

2026-10-30T17:50:00+05:304 min

For 171 years, astronomers have puzzled over a tiny wobble in the light from a star system just 16 light-years from Earth. Now, a team led by the University of Michigan has revealed what was really causing it — and what it means for the search for alien worlds.

What Did Astronomers Finally Learn from a 171-Year-Old Mystery in a Nearby Star System?

In October 1855, British astronomer William Stephen Jacob was studying a dim star called 70 Ophiuchi when he noticed something odd — the star seemed to wobble slightly in space, as if something invisible were tugging on it. He proposed that a hidden planet must be orbiting the star, making it dance.

That observation launched a 171-year mystery that has led astronomers through more than a century of telescopic observations, false alarms, and one surprising twist that rewrites the story of our cosmic neighborhood — and might even help us find life beyond Earth.

The Star System That Started It All

70 Ophiuchi (say it like “seventy O-fish-ee”) is a binary star system — two stars that orbit around each other like cosmic dance partners — located just 16.7 light-years from Earth, making it one of our closest stellar neighbors. To put that in perspective, our entire solar system's nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is only about 4 light-years away.

The system sits in the constellation Ophiuchus (the Serpent Bearer), and if you live far from city lights on a quiet night, you can actually spot it with your naked eye. It appears as a faint, slightly twinkling point of light.

What Caused the Century-Old Wobble?

For decades, astronomers kept coming back to 70 Ophiuchi because of that mysterious wobble. As the star moved toward and away from Earth, the shifts in its light could have been explained by an unseen planet’s gravitational pull — but it could also have been caused by something happening on the star itself.

Now, led by Yiting Li at the University of Michigan, an international team of researchers has solved the puzzle. By combining a century of data with fresh observations from two powerful telescopes — the 6.5-meter Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile and the 2.7-meter Harlan J. Smith Telescope in Texas — they confirmed what those wobbling signals were really telling us.

The answer? Stellar activity, not a planet.

What Does This Mean for Finding Alien Worlds?

While the study ruled out Jupiter-sized planets lurking near either star, it also opened new doors. Saturn-sized planets — or even smaller, rocky worlds — could still exist in the system's habitable zone, the sweet spot where liquid water could pool on a planet’s surface.

That makes 70 Ophiuchi AB a prime target for future space missions. NASA’s upcoming Habitable World Observatory and the SHERA mission are being designed to hunt for just these kinds of smaller worlds around nearby stars. And with the two stars' orbits now precisely mapped, astronomers know exactly where to look.

"Given how prevalent planets are in our universe," Li noted, "I would wager that we'll find there are other planets there."

The search continues, but for now, the 171-year-old mystery is solved: the wobble was a trick of starlight, not a sign of a giant planet — and maybe, just maybe, the real discovery is still waiting beneath the surface.

Fun Fact

If you could stand on one of 70 Ophiuchi's stars, you'd see the other star rise and set in the sky every 88 years — one complete orbit takes nearly nine decades to finish!
