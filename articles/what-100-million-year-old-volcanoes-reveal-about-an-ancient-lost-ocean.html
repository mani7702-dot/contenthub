What 100-Million-Year-Old Volcanoes Reveal About an Ancient Lost Ocean

2026-10-294 min

Scientists have uncovered a hidden volcanic system in Iran that formed 100 million years ago, when a long-vanished ocean called the Neo-Tethys shaped the ancient world.

What can 100-million-year-old volcanic rocks teach us about a lost ocean?

Imagine a time when the ground beneath what is now northwestern Iran was bubbling with volcanoes — not the quiet hills we see today, but active mountains spewing ash and lava into a sky few creatures understood. That fiery landscape existed 100 million years ago, and now scientists have found its hidden footprints in the rocks. Their discovery reveals a missing chapter in Earth's story, one that helps explain how entire oceans can quietly vanish.

A volcanic arc hidden beneath later layers

The volcanic system is called the Azerbaijan Continental Magmatic Arc. It was born when two tectonic plates — pieces of Earth's crust that slowly drift like puzzle pieces — met in a process called subduction. One plate plunged beneath the other, sending water and rock deep into the planet's hot interior. There, pressure and temperature cooked the mixture into magma — molten rock that rises toward the surface and creates volcanoes.

Scientists from Istanbul Technical University recognized the arc by carefully studying volcanic and plutonic rocks collected from sites along a major fault line (the Siah Cheshmeh-Khoy-Misho-Tabriz Fault). Using zircon dating, isotope analysis, and chemical fingerprinting, they confirmed that these rocks formed between 101 and 97 million years ago — during the Late Cretaceous, the final age of the dinosaurs.

Why was it hidden for so long?

The arc did not simply disappear. Over millions of years, tectonic forces twisted and folded the landscape. Erosion wore away the peaks. Sediment buried the valleys. Then, about 50 million years later, a second wave of volcanoes erupted right on top, smothering the older signs beneath fresh ash and lava. It is like building a sandcastle, only to have a child pile an even bigger one on top — the original shape is still there, just harder to see.

The Neo-Tethys connection

This hidden arc tells us about the Neo-Tethys Ocean, a vast body of water that once separated the ancient continents of Africa, Arabia, India, and Eurasia. As the African and Arabian plates pushed northward into Eurasia, the ocean began to close — its waters retreating as mountains rose. The volcanic arc records those dramatic collisions in stone.

When we look at a roadcut or a mountain road today, we walk across layers that hold entire vanished worlds. The next time you see a volcano on a map, remember: beneath your feet may lie the scars of older ones, waiting for patient scientists to read their stories.

Fun fact: The largest known impact crater on the Moon was likely made by a single asteroid, but on Earth, erosion and tectonics erase impact sites surprisingly quickly — some of the biggest ancient impacts are known only from buried geological signatures, much like this hidden volcanic arc.

Fun fact: If Earth's 4.5-billion-year history were scaled to a single 24-hour day, the Late Cretaceous volcanoes of Iran would have erupted just before 10:45 p.m. — with humans appearing in the final second before midnight!
