How scientists built a lightning-fast laser to peer inside living cells

2026-10-223 min

A new laser-powered microscope at UC Berkeley can reveal tiny cell structures that were once invisible, helping scientists watch the hidden machinery of life in stunning detail.

1. How scientists built a lightning-fast laser to peer inside living cells

A better flashlight for the tiniest world

Close your eyes and imagine shrinking — smaller than the dot at the end of this sentence, smaller than a single germ. As you go, the world changes: smooth things suddenly bristle with hills and valleys, and the walls around you are alive with moving parts. That hidden landscape is what scientists try to photograph every day, only it is very hard to see because the structures are so tiny.

Now a team at the University of California, Berkeley, has found a clever trick: they added a super-powered laser to one of the world's sharpest microscopes. The result is a new way to watch the hidden machinery inside living cells.

From light microscopes to electron microscopes

Almost a century ago, scientists invented phase contrast, a way to make faint, see-through parts of cells stand out by playing with the light. It was so useful that it earned a Nobel Prize in 1953. Around the same idea, today's most powerful microscopes shoot electrons (teeny particles) instead of light, and they can magnify objects about 10,000 times more than ordinary light microscopes.

But even electron microscopes have a problem: the smallest proteins are so tiny and so faint that they mostly disappear into blur and noise. That's where the new laser gadget steps in.

A laser that tweaks the electron beam

The Berkeley team built what they call a laser phase plate — a device that uses the world's most intense focused laser to gently nudge the electron beam. Instead of just lighting up a sample, the laser changes the beam's phase, which cranks up the image's contrast. Think of it like adjusting sunglasses so you can spot a single pebble on a bright beach.

With this boost, scientists can now clearly see proteins that are roughly one-third the size of the smallest ones they could image before. And because the method also helps 3D imaging, researchers can watch how molecules are arranged and move inside real, living cells — not just floating alone in a dish.

Why smaller proteins matter

Many of the proteins that keep our bodies running are small — so small they hid from old microscopes. Now, for the first time, scientists can study those tiny workers in their natural home inside a cell. The team says the new method could speed up discoveries about how cells work and how diseases begin.

Professor Holger Müller, who led the work, put it simply: imagine trying to solve a puzzle while the pieces keep changing shape. The laser phase plate is a fresh pair of eyes — one sharp enough to see the pieces clearly.

Try this at home

Shine a flashlight through a clear glass of water with a drop of milk in it. Notice how the cloudy water glows and you can finally see the light beam? That is contrast at work — your eye suddenly spots something that was invisible before. Scientists do the same thing with a laser, just on a far, far smaller scale.

Fun Fact

The very first phase-contrast microscopes had to be tuned by hand, like a radio dial. The new laser phase plate does the same job automatically, letting researchers focus on discovery instead of fiddling knobs.
