Why are red squirrels thriving on Jersey but vanishing from the rest of England?

2026-10-213 min

Red squirrels are vanishing across England, yet on one small island they run, leap, and chatter in the trees — and people are fighting to keep them safe.

Why are red squirrels thriving on Jersey but vanishing from the rest of England?

The red flash that used to fill British woods

Walk through an English wood 50 years ago and you might have seen them: bright red squirrels, flicking their bushy tails from branch to branch, stuffing their cheeks with acorns. Today, if you walk in most of England, you will see a different squirrel — bigger, grey, and much less flashy.

The red squirrel is vanishing across England. The grey squirrel is taking its place. But on one tiny island in the English Channel, red squirrels are not just surviving — they are thriving.

Why Jersey is a safe haven

Jersey is a small island that is part of the Channel Islands. It is also one of the few places in southern England where no grey squirrels live.

Grey squirrels came to the UK a long time ago from North America. They are strong, clever, and they eat almost anything. Red squirrels and grey squirrels both want the same food — nuts, seeds, and berries — but grey squirrels are bigger and grab the best spots first.

Worse, grey squirrels carry a secret danger: a virus called squirrelpox. Red squirrels have no protection against it. When a grey squirrel passes the virus to a red squirrel, the red squirrel usually gets very sick and dies.

Jersey has no grey squirrels, so no squirrelpox virus. That makes the island a safe refuge — a place where red squirrels can live without that deadly threat.

Numbers that tell the story

  • There are only about 120,000 to 160,000 red squirrels left in the wild across the whole of England and the UK.
  • About 75% of them live in Scotland, in the cold, quiet forests of the north.
  • Most of the rest are scattered in small pockets — on islands, in nature reserves, and in places like Jersey.

How islanders protect the red flash

Jersey's red squirrels were actually introduced in 1885. They are not native — but after more than 130 years, they feel like they belong. Locals call them "their" squirrels, and they have made them a priority.

Islanders help red squirrels in simple ways:

  • They put out squirrel feeders with nuts so the squirrels always have food.
  • They built rope bridges so squirrels can cross roads safely without getting hit.
  • The speed limits on island roads are low, which helps.
  • Red squirrels are legally protected — it is a criminal offence to harm them.

The Jersey Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (JSPCA) even hand-raises baby squirrels that fall from nests. They handled 50 red squirrels this year alone — including eight tiny kittens (baby squirrels are called kits) that needed round-the-clock care.

"You can see them almost anywhere," said a Jersey animal carer. "People love having them around."

What you can do

Red squirrels need help across the rest of England. Conservation groups build grey-squirrel-free zones on islands and in forest reserves. They plant nut trees and build feeders. And they ask people not to feed grey squirrels in parks — because that helps grey squirrels stay and compete with reds.

Try this at home

Next time you visit a park, look up and see which squirrels you can spot. The big, grey ones with no ear tufts are grey squirrels. The smaller, red ones with big tufted ears (like tiny winter hats) are red squirrels! Draw the two kinds in your notebook — can you tell them apart?

Remember: if you ever find a baby red squirrel that seems hurt or alone, tell an adult — do not pick it up. Wildlife carers know how to help.

Fun Fact

A red squirrel can rotate its ankles 180 degrees — that means it can climb down a tree headfirst! Grey squirrels can not do this. It is one of the tricks that helps red squirrels move through trees so fast.
