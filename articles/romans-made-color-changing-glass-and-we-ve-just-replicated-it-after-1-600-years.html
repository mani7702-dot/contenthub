Romans Made Color-Changing Glass—and We've Just Replicated It After 1,600 Years

2026-10-294 min

Ancient Roman craftsmen accidentally created the world's first nanotechnology by embedding invisible gold and silver nanoparticles into glass. Now, 1,600 years later, scientists have figured out their secret and recreated the legendary Lycurgus Cup that changes from green to red when light hits it.

Romans Made Color-Changing Glass—and We've Just Replicated It After 1,600 Years

1. The Cup That Changes Color

In the British Museum sits a drinking glass so magical that scientists only figured out its secret centuries later. The Lycurgus Cup, crafted by Roman artisans around 1,600 years ago, appears green when viewed from the front but glows fiery red when light shines through it from behind. It is, in essence, the world's first piece of nanotechnology—created long before anyone knew what nanoparticles were.

The cup is named after King Lycurgus of Thrace from Homer's Iliad, who tried to kill Ambrosia, a follower of the god Dionysus. The glass shows the dramatic scene of the king being tangled in the grapevine that transforms him, with Dionysus and his followers looking on. The intricate design is carved from a solid block of glass in what is called a "cage cup"—a feat of craftsmanship where the decorative cage is cut from a thick blank, leaving delicate figurework suspended in material.

2. The Nanoparticles Hidden in the Glass

For centuries, no one knew how the Romans achieved the color-changing effect. Modern analysis revealed the secret: the glass contains nanoparticles of gold and silver—so tiny that they are smaller than the wavelengths of visible light. Scientists estimate the original glass contained about 330 parts per million of silver and 40 parts per million of gold, with particles roughly 70 nanometers across.

These nanoparticles work through a phenomenon called surface plasmon resonance. When light hits the particles, they absorb certain wavelengths and scatter others. Looking at the cup from the front, the reflected light gives it a green hue. But when light travels through the glass, the nanoparticles filter out blue and green light, allowing reds and oranges to pass through—making the cup glow red.

It is likely the Romans discovered this by accident. Traces of gold and silver may have contaminated the glass melt from workshop tools, leftover gold leaf, or even small amounts of gold naturally present in the silver they used. The craftsmen may not have realized gold was involved at all—the quantities were so small.

3. Modern Scientists Crack the Ancient Code

Now, a team led by Lisa Hollands, a glass technical specialist at the University of Sheffield, has successfully replicated the Roman process using only materials and techniques available in the 4th century. The team first recreated Roman-era glassmaking by using period-appropriate raw materials, then spent considerable time experimenting with different oxygen levels, furnace temperatures, and glass compositions.

The results were surprising: the color proved extremely sensitive to the glass's position in the furnace, making it nearly impossible to control consistently—which likely explains why dichroic glass artifacts are so rare. Ian Freestone, an emeritus archaeological materials scholar at University College London, noted: "The fact that the color is so sensitive to the position in the furnace suggests that it would have been very difficult for the Romans to control the color. It makes me think that, with the Lycurgus glass, they hit the jackpot."

Once the glass was perfected, the team commissioned renowned glass artist Elliot Walker to craft a contemporary sculpture inspired by both the Lycurgus Cup and the ancient Roman statue of the Farnese Atlas—depicting Atlas holding the celestial sphere. Walker's creation replaces the world with a cup that shines with the same color-changing magic as the original.

4. Where to See the Modern Masterpiece

The recreated dichroic glass sculpture will have a permanent home at the University of Sheffield's Turner Museum of Glass. The achievement bridges two millennia of human ingenuity—Roman craftsmanship meets modern scientific understanding, proving that sometimes the most advanced technology of the ancient world was hiding in plain sight.

Fun Fact

The Lycurgus Cup is not the only ancient wonder made with colloidal gold. Medieval gold ruby glass, used in church windows and vessels, also relies on nanoparticles—but the Roman technique produces a far more dramatic color shift. NASA later recreated a similar dichroic effect in the 1960s, but the Roman version remains the most spectacular example of ancient nanotechnology.
