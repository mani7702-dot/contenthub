How big can a fungus get? China's 'King of Dian' stands taller than a dinner plate

2026-10-213 min

Deep in a Chinese forest, scientists found a fungus the size of a houseplant that turns out to be the heaviest and tallest fruiting body ever recorded among its entire branch of the tree of life — a 65-million-year-old survivor.

A forest giant that isn't a tree

When kids (and grown-ups) think of a "giant" in nature, an elephant or a redwood usually pops into mind. Fungi rarely get the giant treatment — even the biggest grocery-store mushrooms look small next to a person.

Yet tucked away in the misty, subtropical forests of Yunnan Province in southwest China, scientists met a fungus that stands about as tall as a standard dinner plate is wide. It is not the biggest organism on Earth (that title belongs to a honey-fungus colony the size of 1,665 football fields), but among its entire branch of the tree of life it now holds two records: the heaviest and the tallest single "fruiting body" ever measured.

King of Dian

The fungus wears a royal name. In science-speak it is Dianjunus rex. "Dian" is what the ancient Chinese called this region, and "rex" is Latin for king — so it is literally the "King of Dian."

What makes it a king is how it grows. Instead of a classic umbrella-shaped mushroom, D. rex sends up thick, upright stalks covered in tiny spore sacks. These "vertical fruiting bodies" rise straight up — sometimes 20 inches (50 cm) tall — and can weigh more than 5 pounds (2.2 kg). To put that in kid terms: that is roughly the weight of a full grocery bag of sugar held in a single stalk.

Born in the wake of the dinosaur extinction

Scientists used a molecular clock — a way of counting tiny DNA changes like tree rings — to estimate when this fungal family first appeared. The answer: about 65 million years ago, in the early Paleocene epoch.

That is strikingly specific timing. The Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction event happened 66 million years ago and wiped out about 75% of all plant and animal species, including the non-bird dinosaurs. With the giants gone, huge ecological vacancies opened up — especially for fungi, which are nature's master recyclers, breaking down dead wood and returning nutrients to the soil.

While the forests were busy regrowing, D. rex's ancestors appear to have evolved their giant form, taking advantage of all that newly available dead wood. It has stayed giant ever since, still growing today on the woody roots of Chinese cork oak trees, unchanged in basic plan for tens of millions of years.

Why stay so big?

Being enormous is expensive. A giant stalk takes serious energy to build. For D. rex to keep that strategy alive means the benefits finally outweigh the costs.

The leading idea: height helps. A fruiting body that towers above the leaf litter and competing vegetation gets a better ride on the wind. Fungi launch their spores like tiny seeds on air currents — a higher perch means spores travel farther before they drop and the next generation can germinate.

Try this at home

On a breezy day, find a dandelion clock (the puffball stage) or a dandelion gone to seed. Blow gently and watch the fluffy seeds spiral away. That is the same basic idea D. rex uses — except instead of tiny seeds, it is launching millions of microscopic spores from a 5-pound stalk.

Fun fact: Morels, truffles, brie, camembert, soy sauce, sake, yeast rolls, and the mold that makes blue cheese all belong to the same massive phylum (Ascomycota) as D. rex. The difference is that D. rex grew a skyscraper where they grew a button.
