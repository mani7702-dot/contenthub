Was the American 'Cheetah' Really a Cheetah After All?

2026-10-31T22:45:00+00:004

Ancient DNA shows the speedy Ice Age cat that once chased North America's pronghorn was actually a puma in disguise.

Was the American "Cheetah" Really a Cheetah After All?

For decades, scientists believed North America had its own kind of cheetah — a swift, long-legged hunter that chased the fleet-footed pronghorn across the Great Plains. But a surprising new study suggests that the famous "American cheetah" was never related to cheetahs at all. Ancient DNA reveals it was, in fact, a close cousin of the modern puma, wearing a cheetah costume that evolution crafted on its own.

The Cat That Fooled Everyone

The animal in question is Miracinonyx trumani, a predator that roamed North America during the Ice Age. Because its body looked nearly identical to the African cheetah — slim, tall-limbed, and built for speed — researchers long assumed it was the same genus. It even inspired the idea that pronghorns evolved their incredible speed as a defense against this supposed cheetah-like hunter.

But the new genomic study tells a different story. By sequencing high-coverage genomes from fossils between 23,000 and 31,000 years old, scientists confirmed that Miracinonyx belonged to the puma family tree, splitting away from modern pumas about 2.6 million years ago. Its cheetah-like shape came not from shared ancestry, but from a beautiful example of evolutionary convergence — two unrelated lineages arriving at the same sleek design because it works so well on open ground.

A Hunter With Two Very Different Lives

The findings paint a picture far more flexible than the old "desert sprinter" image. In temperate grasslands like those of Wyoming and Florida, Miracinonyx acted as a generalist predator. But in the far north — the Arctic Yukon — these cats appear to have switched diets, relying heavily on anadromous fish such as salmon.

"These cats were remarkably flexible, much like pumas are across their range today," explained lead author Molly Cassatt-Johnstone, a Ph.D. candidate at UC Santa Cruz. Genetic clues show that northern populations carried loss-of-function mutations in genes that tune circadian rhythms, helping them cope with the extreme light cycles of Arctic summers and winters. The study also uncovered that Miracinonyx shares with all other felines a missing gene for detecting sour tastes — the first recorded case in cats of this particular sensory loss.

Why They Disappeared When the Ice Retreated

Low genetic diversity — found in populations from both Wyoming and the Yukon — hints at why these cats eventually vanished near the end of the Pleistocene. Unlike some species that crashed suddenly from inbreeding, Miracinonyx declined gradually over hundreds of thousands of years. As the last ice age's climate shifted, that slow erosion may have left the cats too inflexible to adapt. "They persisted for a very long time without the signs of inbreeding we'd expect before a collapse," said senior author Beth Shapiro. "The decline was slow, not sudden — and that may be what left them unable to adjust."

Fun fact

The name Miracinonyx means "wonderful claw" in Latin, referring to the long, curved claws that once made scientists think the cat scaled cliffs like a modern cheetah. Today, those claws are recognized as another case of evolutionary mimicry — nature's way of testing the same winning design twice.
