Why Did Crocodiles Survive the Asteroid That Killed the Dinosaurs?

2026-10-314 min

A new study of more than 7,000 fossils suggests crocodiles and their relatives outlasted the dinosaurs by doing something simple and clever — they kept moving toward warm places, and the tropics stayed livable when much of the planet did not.

Why Did Crocodiles Survive the Asteroid That Killed the Dinosaurs?

When a giant asteroid struck Earth about 66 million years ago, it wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs. Yet crocodiles — and their many ancient relatives — lived on. For years, scientists wondered how. A new study led by researchers at University College London (UCL) offers a surprisingly simple answer: crocodiles followed the warmth.

1. A Front-Row Seat to Two Mass Extinctions

The crocodile lineage, known to scientists as pseudosuchians, first appeared around 250 million years ago — at roughly the same time as the dinosaurs. Since then, the group has survived two of Earth's five great mass extinctions. To understand why, the team combined a family tree of more than 400 fossil and living species, over 7,000 fossil records, and 130,000 observations of modern crocodilians, then used computer models to reconstruct Earth's climate and geography across 245 million years.

Their conclusion, published in the journal Science Advances, was striking: instead of evolving to cope with brand-new climates, crocodiles and their relatives simply moved to stay in the conditions they liked.

2. Following the Warmth, Not Fighting the Cold

When Earth grew hotter, the animals spread outward — at times reaching as far as Antarctica. When things cooled down, they retreated toward the tropics. Their preferred temperature stayed remarkably steady through it all: the group's ancient "comfort zone" averaged around 23 degrees Celsius (about 73 degrees Fahrenheit), while living crocodilians today sit between 26 and 30 degrees.

That habit paid off when the asteroid hit. The researchers' simulations showed that, in all but the most extreme scenarios, warm regions near the equator and in the subtropics stayed suitable for crocodile relatives — even as the habitats available to the dinosaurs shrank dramatically. Those tropical pockets became refuges where survivors could hold on and later spread out again.

3. A Family of Shapeshifters

Crocodiles are often called "living fossils," but their ancestors were wildly varied. The lineage once included heavily armoured plant-eaters, hunters that ran on land, bus-sized giants like Sarcosuchus (the "super croc," up to 12 metres long), and even small, pug-faced species. What stayed consistent, the study found, was not their shape — it was their relationship with climate.

Fun Fact

Long before today's familiar crocodiles, some relatives prowled entirely on land. One, called Postosuchus, could walk on either two legs or all four — a bit like switching between running and crawling when the mood struck.
