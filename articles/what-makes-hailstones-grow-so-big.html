What makes hailstones grow so big?

2026-10-224 min

A rare hailstorm dumped golf-ball-sized ice on Phoenix, Arizona. Here is the simple science behind how frozen raindrops grow into bouncing ice marbles.

What makes hailstones grow so big?

A rare storm over Phoenix, Arizona sent golf-ball-sized hail crashing onto rooftops and cars. The National Weather Service issued several warnings, and neighborhood streets were soon blanketed in ice. But how does plain rain turn into bouncing ice balls?

Updrafts keep hail floating

When a thunderstorm brews, warm air rises in a powerful column called an updraft. Tiny water droplets get sucked upward, where the air is colder. Some of those droplets freeze onto a small particle of dust, forming a pea-sized ice pellet. In a gentle shower, that pellet would fall as sleet. But in a strong storm the updraft is like an invisible escalator — it keeps pushing hailstones back up before they reach the ground.

The longer they ride, the bigger they grow

While a hailstone floats in the cloudy heights, it bumps into supercooled water droplets — liquid that stays unfrozen below zero degrees. Each droplet freezes on contact, adding a new icy layer like rings on a tree. The hailstone grows heavier with every lap of its climb-and-fall journey. When it finally becomes too heavy for the updraft to hold, it drops — sometimes as small peas, sometimes as large as golf balls or even tennis balls.

Why was Phoenix hail so rare?

Phoenix sits in the Sonoran Desert, where the air is normally dry and calm near the ground. That makes big hail unusual — most hailstorms happen in moister, mid-latitude regions. When the October sky over Arizona turned a deep green and the wind shifted, it signaled an unusually strong updraft had formed, letting hailstones grow much larger than the desert usually allows.

Try this at home

Fill a clear cup with ice cubes. Blow gently across the top — feel how the cubes tumble and bounce against each other, just like hailstones tumbling in a thundercloud.

Fun Fact

The largest hailstone ever officially recorded in the United States fell in Vivian, South Dakota in 2010. It was eight inches wide and weighed over one and a quarter pounds — about the size of a cantaloupe!
