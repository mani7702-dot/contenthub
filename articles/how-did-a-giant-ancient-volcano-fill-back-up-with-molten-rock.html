How did a giant ancient volcano fill back up with molten rock?

2026-10-233 min

Deep beneath Japan, one of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly rebuilding its underground magma reservoir — 7,300 years after one of the largest eruptions in history.

1. How did a giant ancient volcano fill back up with molten rock?

A sleeping giant under the sea

Off the southern coast of Japan, hidden beneath the ocean waves, sits Kikai Caldera — one of the strongest volcanoes on Earth. About 7,300 years ago, it erupted with a power almost impossible to imagine. Scientists rate it VEI 7 — the second-highest level on the volcano danger scale. That single blast made it one of the most powerful supervolcanoes in Earth's recent history.

But now, thousands of years later, something surprising is happening: the giant is slowly filling back up.

Watching magma from space

You might think studying a volcano under miles of ocean would be impossible. But the seafloor actually gives scientists a clear view. A team from Kobe University and the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC) used powerful airgun arrays to send sound waves through the seafloor. Special microphones resting on the seabed — called ocean-bottom seismometers — caught the echoes bouncing back.

By studying how those sound waves traveled, the scientists could see something amazing: a magma reservoir sitting 2.5 to 6 kilometers (1.5 to 3.7 miles) beneath the caldera. It is shaped like a trapezoid and is at least as wide as the caldera itself — which is roughly 10 kilometers across.

Fresh rock, not old leftovers

Here is the key question: is this just leftover magma from the ancient eruption, or is something new being added?

Chemical tests of recent lava domes — which have been growing quietly for the last 3,900 years — show a different composition than the material from the giant 7,300-year-old blast. That difference is a clue: new melt is being re-injected into the same underground reservoir from deep below.

Why this matters

Scientists do not know exactly when (or if) Kikai will erupt again. But understanding how supervolcanoes rebuild their magma stores helps us learn how all giant volcanoes work — including famous ones like Yellowstone in the U.S. and Toba in Indonesia. Every new discovery makes it a little easier to read the warning signs that Mother Earth writes in stone and seismic waves.

Try this at home

Fill a large bowl with water. Drop in a few drops of food coloring and watch how they swirl and sink at different speeds. That is a little like how magma moves through cracks deep underground — slow, steady, and invisible unless you know where to look.

Fun Fact

Kikai's 7,300-year-old eruption is the largest known volcanic eruption in the entire Holocene — the geological age that started about 11,700 years ago and includes our entire recorded history. Only eruptions from much earlier ice ages put more material in the air. Scientists still find traces of its ash layers in soil, tree rings, and even cave formations thousands of kilometers away.

for-grownups: The Kikai-Akahoya eruption ~7,300 years ago was a VEI 7 "supereruption" — the largest known in the Holocene epoch. New seismic-refraction surveys by Kobe University and JAMSTEC (Nagaya et al., Communications Earth & Environment, DOI 10.1038/s43247-026-03347-9) found a shallow magma reservoir 2.5–6 km beneath the caldera, shaped like a trapezoid up to 10 km wide with a melt fraction of 3–6% (max 10%). Chemical differences between the new melt and the ancient eruption layer suggest fresh magma is being re-injected into the same reservoir — a process that may help explain how supervolcanoes like Yellowstone and Toba rebuild between giant eruptions. This is long-term geological research, not an eruption forecast.
