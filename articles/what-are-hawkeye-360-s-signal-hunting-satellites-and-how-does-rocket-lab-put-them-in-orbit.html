What are HawkEye 360's signal-hunting satellites, and how does Rocket Lab put them in orbit?

2026-10-253 min

Rocket Lab launched three new Hawk satellites for HawkEye 360 on October 23, 2026 — special spacecraft that hunt invisible radio signals across Earth and help track ships, spot jamming, and map radio chatter from space.

1 — What are HawkEye 360's signal-hunting satellites, and how does Rocket Lab put them in orbit?

On the morning of October 23, 2026, an Electron rocket zoomed off a launch pad in New Zealand, carrying three brand-new satellites that are not looking at stars or taking pretty pictures. Instead, these spacecraft hunt something far more mysterious: invisible radio signals that zoom around our planet every day — from cell phone calls to ship radar to GPS pings on lost cargo boats.

The satellites belong to a company called HawkEye 360, and this was Rocket Lab's 98th launch ever — its 19th mission of the year. Rocket Lab gave the flight a fun name: "We're Not Hawking Around" (yes, a play on the word "hawking").

2 — What do signal-hunting satellites actually do?

Imagine you are at a crowded party where dozens of people are talking at once. Now imagine you could pick out one voice from across the room, follow it as it moves, and figure out exactly where that person is standing — all without anyone knowing you are listening. That is what HawkEye 360's satellites do, but with radio waves instead of voices.

Each Hawk satellite listens across a wide range of the radio frequency (RF) spectrum. By comparing signals picked up by multiple satellites flying in a cluster, they can triangulate — zero in on — the exact location of a radio source. This helps track ships at sea (even when they turn off their identification beacons), detect when someone is jamming GPS signals, monitor radar activity, and map radio communications across the globe.

Since 2018, HawkEye 360 has launched nearly 50 of these birds, building a constellation that can spot signals from orbit and turn them into clear data that governments and researchers use for everything from maritime security to disaster response.

3 — How does the Electron rocket make the trip?

The Electron rocket — a two-stage vehicle about as tall as a 15-story building — is one of the smallest rockets in service today. But small does not mean simple. Its Rutherford engines are the first in the world to use electric pumps instead of gas, a technology that makes the engine lighter and more efficient.

After lifting off at 3:11 a.m. EDT, the Electron climbed through the atmosphere, shed its first stage, and fired its upper stage to push the three Hawk satellites into low Earth orbit — the same neighborhood as the International Space Station, where satellites circle the planet every 90 minutes. From there, each Hawk satellite will spread out and join its flock, listening for the silent conversation of radio waves that connect our world.

Try this at home

Turn your living room into a signal detective! Get a cheap software-defined radio (SDR) dongle — they cost less than $20 — and plug it into an old laptop. Free apps like SDR# or GNU Radio let you tune across radio bands and see the invisible world of signals around you: baby monitors, Wi-Fi routers, Bluetooth headphones, even weather balloons. Try walking around your house with the antenna and notice how the signal strength changes — you are doing the same kind of triangulation that HawkEye 360's satellites do from space!

Fun Fact

Rocket Lab's Electron is the third most-launched small-lift rocket in history — that means it is one of the busiest little rockets on the planet. Its name comes from Richard Feynman's description of how scientists once thought all the energy in an atomic bomb came from a chain reaction that was "going to be very small." Rocket Lab took that idea of "small but powerful" and built a rocket that has now flown nearly 100 times.
