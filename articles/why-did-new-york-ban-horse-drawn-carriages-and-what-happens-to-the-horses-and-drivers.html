Why did New York ban horse-drawn carriages — and what happens to the horses and drivers?

2026-10-224 min

New York City Council voted to ban horse-drawn carriage rides in Central Park starting in June 2028, ending a 160-year-old tradition after a teen tourist's tragic fall and decades of safety concerns.

1. A 160-year-old tradition is coming to an end

Imagine riding through Central Park in a carriage pulled by a gentle horse, just like people did when the park first opened in 1858. For over 160 years, those horse-drawn carriages have been a picture-perfect New York experience — the kind of thing you see in movies and postcards.

But on October 16, 2026, New York City's lawmakers said that tradition is coming to an end. The City Council voted to ban horse-drawn carriage rides in Central Park, with the ban set to begin in June 2028.

2. Why did the rules change?

Two tragedies this year changed the conversation. In June 2026, an 18-year-old tourist named Romanch Mahajan was visiting New York from India. He was riding in a carriage with his family when the horse suddenly bolted. Mahajan fell out and was critically injured. He died days later in the hospital.

Then in the same year, a carriage horse named Deniz ate a toxic plant while waiting in the park and also died.

These incidents added to a long list of safety concerns. Since 1982, 40 horses working in Central Park have died, according to the Manhattan Borough President's office. The causes included overheating, collisions, and exhausted animals collapsing in the middle of busy streets.

3. What about the drivers and their horses?

Right now, about 200 carriage drivers work in Central Park. Many have spent years — even decades — caring for their horses, and they say they treat their animals with love. One driver, Alexus Lawson, told the BBC, "I spend more time with my horses than my wife and my kids."

But city leaders say too many close calls have happened. The Central Park Conservancy, which manages the park, reported seven horse-related incidents in just 13 months — including a spooked horse that caused a carriage to tip over, sending a driver to the hospital.

4. What comes next?

The good news for the drivers is that the city is planning a transition. The new law includes an "industry transition framework" that could offer job placement, training, and career counseling for those who lose their jobs.

For the horses, the law says they cannot be sold for slaughter — owners can keep them if they choose. But the Transport Workers Union, which represents the drivers, says the plan doesn't include enough guarantees or compensation.

Animal rights groups like PETA and the ASPCA — which was actually founded in 1866 after its creator witnessed a carriage horse being brutally beaten — have welcomed the ban. They say horses don't belong in a noisy city park.

Meanwhile, tourists who have always dreamed of a classic carriage ride through Central Park will need to find other ways to see the sights.

Try this at home

Want to see how a horse-drawn carriage moves? Tie a string to a toy wagon or wagon wheel, attach it to a stuffed animal or small toy, and pull it across the floor. Feel how the wheels turn and how the "horse" must work to move the load? That's what real carriage horses do every day — and imagine doing it on busy city streets!

Fun Fact

The ASPCA (American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals) was founded in New York City in 1866 — directly because people witnessed carriage horses being mistreated. Today, those same concerns are helping bring horse-drawn carriages to an end in Central Park over 150 years later!
