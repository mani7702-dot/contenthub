How Did Scientists Listen to 98 Million Bat Calls Across England's Woodlands?

2026-10-313 min

Researchers hung tiny microphones in 310 English woodlands and recorded bat calls for nearly 10,000 nights. The result is one of the biggest wildlife listening projects ever attempted — and it found some of Britain's rarest bats hiding in plain sound.

Most of us have never heard a bat. The animals call at frequencies far above what human ears can pick up, and they do it in the dark, among leaves, at speed. Yet a new project heard them — not once, but more than 98 million times.

A microphone in every wood

Between 2021 and 2024, scientists from the Bat Conservation Trust and Forest Research placed acoustic detectors on poles among the trees of 310 woodlands across England. The little recorders listened every night for 9,734 nights in total, producing about 90,000 hours of sound — more than ten years of non-stop listening.

Analysing all of that by hand was impossible. So the team worked with researchers at University College London and the University of Edinburgh to build tools that could sort through the enormous pile of recordings and pick out bat calls. Then 19 bat-call experts double-checked a sample of the results, mixing fast computers with careful human ears.

What the forest was saying

The survey turned up every bat species or species group found in Britain — except one. Common pipistrelles appeared in all 310 woodlands. Soprano pipistrelles turned up at 99% of sites, Myotis bats at 98%, and brown long-eared bats at 90%.

Broadleaved woodlands were the busiest, with more bat activity on average than other woodland types. That makes sense: old, varied woodlands offer food, shelter and safe routes to travel.

The rarest voices

Among the millions of calls were two species on the UK Red List of most threatened wildlife: the barbastelle and the grey long-eared bat. Barbastelle hotspots clustered across southern England, including parts of Cornwall, Devon and Dorset where they had barely been recorded before. Nathusius' pipistrelle and Leisler's bat, both near-threatened, also showed up.

The only British bat missed was the serotine, a less common species whose call is especially hard to tell apart from others on a recording.

Why this matters

Bats are excellent clues about the health of a place. They need plenty of insects and rich, connected habitat to feed, breed and travel. If bats are doing well somewhere, the landscape around them is usually doing well too.

The maps from this survey will help landowners, foresters and conservation groups decide how to create and care for woodlands in the years ahead. Recording continues until 2027 — and the findings are free for anyone to explore online.

Fun fact

A single common pipistrelle can eat around 3,000 tiny insects in one night — which is roughly a third of its own body weight. No wonder bats keep busy in the dark!
