How Did a Monkey's Foot Change the Story of Our Oldest Ancestors?

2026-11-013 min

Wild sooty mangabey monkeys in Ivory Coast can shinny up narrow tree trunks using a bend in the middle of their feet — and that small discovery is reshaping how scientists picture the ancestor we share with chimpanzees.

Humans and chimpanzees went their separate ways somewhere between 6 and 7 million years ago, splitting off from a shared ancestor we call the last common ancestor, or LCA. Nobody has ever found its bones. So scientists try to picture it by studying how its closest living relatives move — and the human-versus-chimp debate has been running for years.

Now a small monkey in West Africa has jumped into the argument, and it has some surprising things to say about feet.

The ankle that started the debate

Chimpanzees are superb tree-trunk climbers, partly thanks to springy ankles. During a climb, a chimp's ankle can flex upward by about 45 degrees. Most human ankles manage only around 20 degrees.

Monkeys were long assumed to be different. With stiffer feet, the thinking went, they relied on their arms to travel through the treetops — which suggested the LCA either had ape-like ankles, or was a full-time tree-crown dweller rather than a trunk climber.

Caught on camera in Ivory Coast

A team from The Ohio State University decided to test that assumption with a camera instead of a fossil. At the Taï Forest Monkey Project field station in Ivory Coast, first author Luke Fannin filmed wild sooty mangabeys — monkeys whose feet genuinely lack a chimpanzee's flexible ankle.

Watching them haul themselves up narrow vertical trunks in search of food and sleeping spots, the researchers measured something remarkable: the monkeys flex up to 46 degrees in the middle of the foot while climbing. Different joint, same result. As Fannin put it, there is a "functional equivalence" here — you cannot rule out vertical climbing just because a creature lacks a chimp-like foot.

Why it matters for our family tree

The discovery, published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, doesn't hand scientists a new ancestor. Instead it loosens a rule. An LCA with a monkey-style foot could still have been a capable trunk climber — so palaeontologists need more fossils, not just foot shapes, to settle what our earliest relative really looked like.

Senior author W. Scott McGraw summed up the twist nicely: the paper "adds clarity, but it also makes the broader picture more fuzzy."

Fun fact: Chimpanzees climb with their ankles and mangabeys climb with their midfeet — two totally different joints arriving at nearly the same 45-degree bend. Evolution often finds more than one road to the same destination.
