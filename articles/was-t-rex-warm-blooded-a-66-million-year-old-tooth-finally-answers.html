Was T. rex Warm-Blooded? A 66-Million-Year-Old Tooth Finally Answers

2026-10-314 min

For the first time, scientists have measured the body temperature of a living T. rex — by reading a chemical signature locked inside its fossil teeth. The answer is surprisingly close to our own.

Was T. rex Warm-Blooded? A 66-Million-Year-Old Tooth Finally Answers

1. Taking the Temperature of a Dinosaur

For more than a century, palaeontologists have pieced together the life of Tyrannosaurus rex from its bones, teeth and footprints. One question kept coming back: was the "king of the dinosaurs" a sluggish, sun-basking reptile, or an energetic, warm-blooded hunter like the birds it shared a family tree with?

Now a team at the University of California, Los Angeles has done something that once sounded impossible — they have actually taken T. rex's temperature. The reading, published in the journal Science Advances: about 36 °C (97 °F). That is remarkably close to a human's roughly 37 °C (98.6 °F), and far warmer than a modern lizard or snake.

2. The Secret Hidden in Tooth Enamel

The "thermometer" is not a device but a chemical clue. Inside tooth enamel, rare forms of carbon and oxygen can bond together, and how often those bonds form depends on temperature. Cooler conditions leave more of these bonds; warmer conditions leave fewer. The enamel of a warm-blooded animal should therefore hold fewer bonds than that of a cold-blooded one.

Tooth enamel is also the toughest part of a skeleton, so it resists chemical change over millions of years. By drilling a tiny sample of powdered enamel from fossil teeth, dissolving it in acid, and measuring the released gas with a mass spectrometer, the researchers could read an animal's ancient body heat.

3. Two Teeth, One Big Answer

Getting a museum to hand over a piece of a celebrity fossil is no small thing — the first version of this method needed too much precious material. But over a decade the UCLA team cut the amount required by about 90%, and the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County agreed to provide small portions of two teeth from Thomas the T. rex, one of the most complete tyrannosaur skeletons on display.

The team measured a nearby crocodilian fossil at about 30 °C (86 °F) — clearly cooler than the dinosaur. Because both animals were buried in the same place, that gap proves the readings reflect real biology, not the surrounding rock.

4. Why Warmth Was a Superpower

A self-heating body would have kept T. rex active while hunting, and may even have allowed tyrannosaurs to roam far north into ancient Alaska, where cold-blooded reptiles could not survive. Fossil hunters have found tyrannosaur bones there — but no lizard, turtle or crocodile remains.

The study offers the first hard measurement to sit alongside decades of clues that dinosaurs were warm-blooded. And it lines up neatly with the family tree: birds, T. rex's living descendants, run even hotter, with body temperatures of about 40–43 °C (104–109 °F).

Fun fact: The warm-blooded verdict came from just a few grams of dust. The team drilled powder from two fossil teeth that were not even on public display — the museum judged the tiny sacrifice well worth the knowledge gained.
