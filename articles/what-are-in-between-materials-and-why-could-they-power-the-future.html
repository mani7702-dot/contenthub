What Are \"In-Between\" Materials, and Why Could They Power the Future?

2026-11-014 min

When chemists heat ingredients to make a new material, they usually watch only the start and the finish. A team in the UK peeked at the messy middle — and stumbled onto a hidden material that could help make clean fuels and better batteries.

What Are "In-Between" Materials, and Why Could They Power the Future?

Picture baking a cake. You mix the batter, slide it into the oven, and pull out a finished sponge. Most of us never look at the gooey, half-set stage in the middle — it seems like just a pit stop on the way to the real thing. Chemists have long worked the same way: they focus on the starting ingredients and the finished material, and treat everything in between as forgettable.

But a team of researchers in the United Kingdom decided to watch that messy middle closely. What they found hiding there surprised them — brand-new materials that no one had ever seen before.

Watching the Steps Nobody Watches

The scientists, from the University of Warwick and the University of Birmingham, began with cleverly designed starting molecules. These "single-source precursors" already contain every element needed to build a final material. All the researchers had to do was heat them up and track what happened moment by moment.

As the temperature climbed, the molecules broke apart and re-formed into short-lived, unstable stages — the "in-between" phases. Normally these vanish in seconds and are easy to miss. Using tools such as solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance, X-ray diffraction, and pair distribution function analysis, the team captured them anyway.

One discovery stood out: an entirely new form of a material called bismuth vanadate, which the researchers nicknamed β-BiVO4. Its atoms are arranged differently from every known version, and it reacts to sunlight in a strikingly different way.

Why This "Hidden" Material Matters

Bismuth vanadate is a favourite among clean-energy scientists. It absorbs sunlight well and still carries enough energy to split water into hydrogen — a clean fuel. Because the new form has a larger "band gap," it could let engineers fine-tune materials for solar fuels, catalysts, and electronics.

The surprises did not stop there. Another hidden phase the team identified turned out to be excellent at storing lithium, hinting it could one day improve batteries. As Dr. Dominik Kubicki of Birmingham put it, these in-between materials "aren't just stepping stones — they can have useful properties in their own right."

The work, published in Nature Communications, suggests a simple but powerful idea: many useful materials may be hiding in the stages scientists usually skip past.

A New Way to Hunt for Materials

Dr. Sebastian Pike of Warwick explained the shift in thinking: "When materials are made by heating, scientists usually focus on the final product… But this study shows that there are many fascinating stages in between."

By controlling temperature, the starting molecules, and the reaction pathway, researchers believe they can deliberately create structures that are hard — or impossible — to make with ordinary heating. In other words, the middle of the recipe might become the destination.

Fun Fact

The word "vanadate" comes from Vanadís, a name for the Norse goddess Freyja. So bismuth vanadate is, quite literally, named after a goddess — and its newest form may help power a cleaner world.
