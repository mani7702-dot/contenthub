How a Laser 'Broom' Paints 3D Maps of Earth from Space — Without Any Moving Parts

2026-10-214 min

NASA's CASALS instrument uses rainbow light — not spinning mirrors — to sweep laser pulses across Earth, creating seamless 3D maps of forests and terrain in one pass.

How NASA's "Rainbow Laser" Maps the Whole Earth in 3D

Why satellites can't see everything

From orbit, satellites take pictures a bit like a flashlight sweeping across the ground — but the flashlight only lights up one thin line at a time. For laser instruments (called lidar), this is the problem: each pass of the satellite leaves blank stripes between the lines it measured. Scientists end up with a dotted map of Earth, not a full picture.

"Between those lines," explains the NASA team, "are enormous gaps — millions of acres of land scientists never directly measure."

No moving mirrors, just rainbow magic

Now a NASA team has built an instrument that fills in the blanks. It is called CASALS — the Concurrent Artificially-intelligent Spectrometry Lidar System — and it does something clever: instead of wiggling a mirror to aim its laser, it changes the color of the light.

Here is how it works. The instrument fires a laser centered at 1040 nanometers (invisible infrared light, just beyond what our eyes can see). But it does not fire just one color — it rapidly cycles through 256 slightly different wavelengths. Each wavelength bends at a different angle when it passes through a component etched with microscopic ridges, called a transmission grating. That is the same thing that splits light into a rainbow inside a soap bubble.

Thanks to diffraction — the physics of how light bends — each of the 256 laser pulses lands at a different spot on the ground. The result is a continuous 3D swath instead of dotted lines. The whole sweep happens with no moving parts whatsoever. No spinning mirrors, no motors, no fragile gears that can jam in space.

How good is the detail?

When CASALS's prototype flew over Arizona in November 2024 aboard a NASA aircraft flying at roughly 5 km up, it could distinguish individual tree branches and measure subtle changes in ground elevation with 22-centimeter (about 8 inch) precision.

That is sharp enough to see differences smaller than a dinner plate from an airplane window.

The instrument also wastes almost no light — it captures 80% of the photons that bounce back to the sensor, making it far more efficient than older systems. That means it can "see" through darker surfaces and thicker forests that previous instruments simply could not map.

What's next for CASALS?

The team is already planning the next steps. By summer 2027, they will fly CASALS again as part of NASA's ASCENT campaign — a project to map Earth's forests, coastlines, and terrain at unmatched detail. If it works in space, CASALS could become the first orbital lidar system that maps the entire planet as continuous 3D swaths rather than isolated tracks.

That would give scientists a dramatically more complete picture of Earth — how forests grow, how coasts erode, and how the ground shifts beneath our feet. All from a single, mirror-free, rainbow laser beam sweeping across the sky.

Try this at home

You can see diffraction (light bending) with a CD! Hold a shiny compact disc flat and tilt it toward a lamp or the sun. The tiny tracks on the CD act like the microscopic ridges in CASALS. Instead of a mirror, the ridges split light into a rainbow fan of colors. Tilt slowly — watch how the rainbow angles change. That is how CASALS steers each laser pulse without any moving mirrors.
