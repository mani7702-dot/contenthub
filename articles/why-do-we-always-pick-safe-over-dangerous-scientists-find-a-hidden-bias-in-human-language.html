Why do we always pick 'safe' over 'dangerous'? Scientists find a hidden bias in human language

2026-10-263 min

Scientists have discovered that the words we choose — whether in stories, tweets, or conversation — lean heavily toward safety over danger. This hidden bias, found by analyzing billions of words, could change how we study language and teach computers to understand us.

Why do we always pick "safe" over "dangerous"? Scientists find a hidden bias in human language

The words we choose reveal a survival instinct

Close your eyes and think about the words you used today. Did you say "safe" more often than "dangerous"? "Help" more than "hurt"? As it turns out, the words we pick — whether in stories we tell, tweets we post, or simple conversations — carry a hidden pattern that has been hiding in plain sight for decades.

Scientists at the University of Vermont made a discovery that challenges over 70 years of language research. By analyzing billions of word uses across books, news articles, social media, and spoken language, they found something remarkable: humans have a built-in preference for words about safety over words about danger.

Why "safe" beats "dangerous" every time

For more than 70 years, scientists believed word meaning came down to three emotional feelings: how positive or negative a word feels, how excited it makes you, and how much control it gives you. This is called the VAD model, named after its three parts (Valence, Arousal, Dominance).

But the Vermont team found something surprising. When they looked at millions of real conversations, the words people actually used didn't fit neatly into those three boxes. Instead, three new ideas worked much better:

1. Power — Is the word weak or strong?

2. Danger — Is the word safe or risky?

3. Structure — Is the word ordered or chaotic?

These three ideas explained over 90% of how words work — a big jump from the old model's 72%. And the biggest surprise: across every single type of writing they checked, words about safety showed up far more often than words about danger.

What this means for talking to computers

This discovery is changing how we teach computers to understand human language. When a chatbot reads your message, it tries to figure out if you sound happy, sad, or neutral. But the new research suggests computers should also ask: does this word feel safe or dangerous?

Imagine a computer that could tell the difference between "let's play" and "be careful" — not just by checking a dictionary, but by understanding the deeper feeling of safety behind the words. That's exactly what the researchers hope their discovery will help build.

Their tool — called an ousiometer (think "meaning-meter") — can scan huge amounts of text and track how the overall feeling of safety shifts as a story unfolds. They even tested it on Les Misérables, mapping how the meaning changed chapter by chapter, like watching a mood rainbow swirl across the page.

Try this at home

Grab a book or turn on the TV and listen for "safe" words: words like help, calm, strong, bright, home. Now count how often you hear "danger" words: scared, dark, crash, lost, alone. You'll likely find many more "safe" ones — and that's your brain doing exactly what scientists think all of us have been doing since language began.

You can also try this: ask a grown-up to help you type the word "cat" into Google search. Look at how many results suggest love and cuddles — and how few mention claws or danger! That's your safety bias in action.

Fun Fact

The tool that discovered this — the ousiometer — was built by scientists who already had a famous invention: the hedonometer. That's a "happiness meter" that has been scanning the internet to measure global mood since 2008. (Yes, computers have been measuring feelings in words for over 15 years!)

And here's a twist: the researchers say the old idea that people just like to be happy might not be the whole story. Instead, our love of positive language might really be a much deeper love of feeling safe — a survival instinct that shaped not just how we speak, but how our entire language is built.
