A Brief History of METI: When We Started Messaging the Stars

2026-10-264 min

For sixty years humans have searched the stars for signs of intelligence. But a smaller, bolder idea asks: should we also send messages first? Meet METI — the art and science of calling out into the cosmic dark.

How did we learn to call out to the stars — and who answers?

1. The question that started it all

Imagine sitting at a lunch table, looking up at the night sky, and asking a simple question: "Where is everybody?" That is exactly what physicist Enrico Fermi did in 1950 at Los Alamos National Laboratory. He was chatting with colleagues about flying saucers when he posed a challenge. If the universe is so big and old, shouldn't Earth have been visited already?

Fermi and his friends did quick math. The universe holds over 2 trillion galaxies, each with billions of stars. Our galaxy alone has hundreds of billions of planets. Yet we have seen no sign of visitors. That quiet gap between expectation and reality became known as Fermi's Paradox.

2. Listening first: Project Ozma

Before we could send messages, we had to learn how to listen. In 1960, astronomer Frank Drake pointed a radio telescope at a star called Tau Ceti, hoping to catch a signal. He listened for weeks. He heard nothing — but his experiment was the first official SEARCH for Extraterrestrial Intelligence, or SETI.

For decades, scientists focused on listening. They scanned millions of stars, built bigger telescopes, and waited for a reply that never came.

3. The Arecibo Message: our first cosmic postcard

Then, in 1974, something changed. A team led by Frank Drake and Carl Sagan decided to try sending a message instead. They beamed a 1,679-bit radio signal — just 210 bytes — toward a globular cluster called Messier 13, about 25,000 light-years away.

The message contained simple building blocks of life: numbers, DNA instructions, a diagram of a human, and a map of our solar system. It was meant as a demonstration of human technology, not a real conversation. The signal was so faint it would take 25,000 years just to reach its destination — and that is if anyone is even looking.

4. The birth of METI

For a long time, scientists made no distinction between sending and listening. But in 2005, the term "Active SETI" appeared. A year later, Russian scientist Alexander Zaitsev gave it a sharper name: METI — Messaging to Extra-Terrestrial Intelligence.

METI is different from SETI. SETI listens for messages from aliens. METI creates and sends messages to aliens. The question remains: is it wise to call out into the dark when we do not know who might answer?

for-grown-ups: SETI = searching for messages from aliens; METI = creating/transmitting messages to aliens. The distinction was formalized by Alexander Zaitsev in 2005–2006 ("Active SETI" then "METI"). Project Ozma (1960, Frank Drake, Tau Ceti) was the first SETI experiment. The Arecibo Message (1974) was a 1,679-bit pulse beamed at Messier 13, designed by Drake, Sagan, and colleagues — a demonstration of human technology, not a genuine conversation attempt. Fermi's Paradox ("Where is everybody?") arose from a 1950 Los Alamos lunch discussion about UFOs. Wikipedia confirms all these milestones.

Try this at home

Stand outside on a clear night. Count how many stars you can see with your eyes alone. Now imagine that each star might host a planet — and each planet might host someone looking back up at their own sky, wondering the same thing. The universe is vast enough that both thoughts can be true at once.

Fun Fact

The Arecibo Message travels at the speed of light, but the cluster it was aimed at moves through the galaxy. By the time the message arrives, M13 will no longer be in that exact spot — though it will still be close enough that the message reaches the right neighborhood.
