How Did Three Forgotten Assyrian Kings Vanish From History?

2026-10-314 min

For over 2,700 years, scholars have relied on a single list to understand the rulers of ancient Assyria. Now, new research suggests three of those rulers were quietly erased — and a solar eclipse may be the clue that finally brings them back.

How Did Three Forgotten Assyrian Kings Vanish From History?

For about a thousand years, the heart of the ancient Near East pulsed to the rhythm of the Tigris River — and to the ambitions of the Assyrian kings who ruled from its banks. Historians have long trusted a single document to map out the order of those rulers: the Assyrian King List, a ancient catalog that has been the gold standard for understanding who ruled, when, and for how long.

But now, two scholars are arguing that list is far from complete.

Three Faces in the Stone

Alexander Johannes Edmonds and Eckart Frahm, writing in the Journal of Cuneiform Studies, point to three rulers who never made the official roster — including a king named Tiglath-pileser who ruled from roughly 763 to 762 BCE. That's a name that rings familiar, but it belongs to a different king entirely. This forgotten Tiglath-pileser was a usurper, and he ruled at the same time as his nephew Aššur-dān III, the king the list officially recognizes for that period.

The evidence comes from a clay tablet now held by the British Museum, recording a royal grant issued during this forgotten king's short reign. Nearby, at a site called Tell 'Abta in Iraq, archaeologists uncovered a stone stela in 1894 bearing an overwritten name — a king whose identity was deliberately erased, then replaced with a later ruler's name.

The Eclipse That Changed Everything

The story takes a dramatic turn thanks to a celestial event. A powerful solar eclipse swept across the ancient Near East, leaving such a mark that scholars have long used it as a fixed point in Assyrian chronology. This forgotten king tried to use that moment — and its religious significance — to justify removing Aššur-dān III from power.

The eclipse would have been a source of great political tension. In the ancient world, a king's ability to command the favor of the gods was measured in part by their mastery over the heavens. A solar eclipse was a bad omen, and rulers who could not explain it risked losing their mandate to govern.

Rewriting the Record

The researchers argue that the Assyrian court later decided to smooth over this messy chapter. The forgotten king's name was scrubbed from monuments, and the official list was tidied up to present a cleaner — but incomplete — succession.

"This tells us that early Neo-Assyrian political history was much messier, but also more interesting, than has so far been recognized," the study notes.

Fun fact: The practice of erasing rulers from history wasn't unique to Assyria — ancient Egypt did it too, most famously with the name of Pharaoh Hatshepsut, whose monuments were defaced after her death.
