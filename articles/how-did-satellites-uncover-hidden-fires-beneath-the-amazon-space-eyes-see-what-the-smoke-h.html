How did satellites uncover hidden fires beneath the Amazon? Space eyes see what the smoke hides

2026-10-213 min

El Niño has dried the Amazon so much that fires are spreading under the tree canopy where normal satellites cannot see them — unless NASA's special sensors catch the heat rising through the smoke.

How did satellites spot fires hiding under the Amazon canopy?

The forest that should never burn

The Amazon rainforest is called the "lungs of the Earth" because it pumps out oxygen and soaks up carbon dioxide. You would not expect fires to spread here — the forest is usually so wet that trees grow dripping with moisture.

But every few years, a weather pattern called El Niño arrives. Winds change high in the sky, the usual rains hold back, and the forest floor turns dry as tinder. In 2026, rainfall around the city of Manaus was 95 percent below normal in August and 60 percent below normal in September.

Smoke that nobody saw — until the satellites looked closer

From space, scientists watch for fires every day. NASA's Aqua satellite carries a camera called MODIS that snaps pictures of Earth in visible light and infrared. When a fire burns, it glows hot in infrared — even through smoke.

But here is the catch: the Amazon has a thick tree canopy, and on the smokiest days the clouds and smoke block the view. NASA scientist Shane Coffield found that standard satellite data was missing about 40 percent of the fires around the city of Santarém alone. That is because the official system only flags the brightest, hottest fires. His team lowered the detection threshold to catch the quieter, smoldering fires spreading along the forest floor — fires that could easily hide in plain sight.

Satellites spotted 50 to 70 uncontrolled fires burning in the Santarém region, stretching from forests near Manaus and Santarém all the way to the mouth of the Amazon River.

Why the fires are so dangerous for the forest

Rainforest plants and animals are not built for fire. Unlike dry savannas or grasslands where plants expect to burn every few years, Amazon species evolved in a world of constant moisture. A single fire can wound trees that have stood for centuries.

Most of the blazes start small — farmers clearing shrubs or crops — but the dry season lets them escape into the understory, where they smolder for days or weeks beneath the leaves. The result is a blanket of smoke so thick that the city of Manaus reported some of the worst air quality on the planet in October 2026.

What space satellites can tell us

The good news is that NASA and other space agencies are getting better at spotting the hidden fires. The VIIRS instrument on the Suomi NPP satellite scans the whole planet every night, and new computer tools flag areas that heat up just a little — a sign that a fire may be starting.

Scientists hope that early warnings from space will help firefighters reach the worst spots before the flames climb into the canopy. Because once the canopy burns, the Amazon — the lung of our planet — struggles to breathe.

Fun Fact

The smoke from Amazon fires can travel thousands of miles. In October 2026, air-quality sensors in Manaus measured fine particles (PM2.5) above 135 micrograms per cubic meter — far above safe levels. Schools closed, outdoor sports were canceled, and the sky turned orange. All from fires that started far below the tree tops, invisible to the unaided eye.

Try this at home

Next time you see a photo of Earth from space, look for the bright dots. Many of those dots are wildfires caught by NASA's fire sensors. You can explore real-time fire maps at NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS) — the same system scientists use to track the Amazon.
