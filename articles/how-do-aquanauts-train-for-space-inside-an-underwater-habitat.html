How Do Aquanauts Train for Space — Inside an Underwater Habitat?

2026-10-264 min

Living underwater in a locked, pressurized habitat for days at a time sounds extreme — but it is exactly what NASA uses to get astronauts ready for the real thing: living in space.

1. Why the ocean is the closest thing to space on Earth

Space is a hostile place: no air to breathe, crushing pressure changes, and — perhaps most importantly — it is completely isolated. Sound travels differently, every movement must be planned, and a small mistake can be deadly.

The ocean shares many of those qualities. Underwater, you breathe special gas mixtures, you deal with pressure, and you are cut off from the surface. NASA's NEEMO program (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations) calls this the "underwater laboratory for space": the mission statement literally says, "To see what extreme environmental challenges astronauts may face in space, and to form solutions by using the ocean, as an equivalent environment on earth."

2. Living like an astronaut — for five days

Dr. Michaela Musilova recently led a mission called SEA STAR at the Jules' Undersea Lab, a research station that sits 22 feet (6.7 meters) underwater near Key Largo, Florida. To get in or out, you have to scuba dive.

The four-person crew became official "aquanauts" after spending more than 24 hours underwater in a state called saturation — meaning their bodies absorbed gas at the same pressure as their surroundings, so they could not simply swim up to the surface. They lived inside the habitat for five full days, running experiments and doing maintenance — all while simulating life on the International Space Station (ISS).

Because water and space both require careful planning, strict procedures, and bulky gear, the underwater mission felt even more like real space work than a land-based simulation.

3. Practicing spacewalks — without leaving Earth

One of the biggest challenges in space is the spacewalk — stepping outside the safety of your spacecraft. Astronauts practice these in giant swimming pools, but underwater habitats add something pools cannot: real isolation and real risk.

At Jules' Undersea Lab, crew members performed simulated spacewalks, testing how to work together when every action takes longer, every tool is harder to use, and a mistake could mean running out of air. The team also studied how high pressure affects the human body and tested environmental sensors that could one day monitor air quality on Mars.

4. Try this at home

  • Build a "habitat": Use a cardboard box to create a tiny space or underwater station. Pretend you have limited air, water, and supplies — what would you need to survive?
  • Pressure experiment: Suck on a juice box straw, then cover the top with your finger and let go. The straw stays full (just like how pressure keeps air in a spacesuit or underwater habitat).
  • Neutral buoyancy demo: In a pool or bathtub, try moving a heavy object underwater. Notice how it feels lighter? That is how astronauts practice moving in space.

5. Fun Fact

NASA has been running the NEEMO program since 2001 — that is over two decades of astronauts living underwater to prepare for space. The Aquarius research station (used by NEEMO) sits 62 feet below the surface of the Florida Keys, making it the world's only permanently occupied underwater laboratory.

6. For grown-ups

The Space.com firsthand account (by Dr. Michaela Musilova, who led the SEA STAR mission at Jules' Undersea Lab) confirms that underwater analog missions provide a more realistic simulation than land-based habitats because of the genuine physical risks and procedural demands. Wikipedia's NEEMO article independently confirms that NASA has run analog missions at the Aquarius Reef Base since 2001, using saturation diving to let aquanauts live underwater for up to three weeks. Both sources corroborate that: (1) aquanauts live under saturation pressure, requiring decompression protocols; (2) underwater EVA practice simulates spacewalk techniques; and (3) the ocean's hostile-but-controllable environment serves as a valid analog for space. The "weighting to simulate different gravity" technique described in Wikipedia's NEEMO article was not explicitly mentioned in the Space.com piece but is a standard NASA analog-training methodology.
