How Did a 245-Million-Year-Old Reptile Keep Its Stomach, Liver and Intestines?

2026-10-314 min

A nearly complete fossil from China has preserved the soft organs of a long-necked marine reptile — including a liver that is still reddish after 245 million years. It is the oldest largely complete digestive system ever found in a reptile.

How Did a 245-Million-Year-Old Reptile Keep Its Stomach, Liver and Intestines?

Most fossils are bones and nothing more. Soft things — stomachs, livers, guts — almost always rot away long before stone takes their place. So when a team of scientists in China lifted the lid on an almost complete fossil and found a reddish liver, a sac-like stomach and a long tube of intestines still inside, they knew they had something rare.

The creature is called Austronaga minuta, a small, long-necked reptile that swam in warm Triassic seas about 245 million years ago — millions of years before the first dinosaurs grew truly enormous.

1. A Little Reptile With a Big Surprise

Austronaga was only about 60 centimetres long — roughly the length of a school ruler and a half — yet it had an unusually stretched-out neck and tail. Its long tail pushed it through the water, while its fin-like front limbs steered and kept it steady. Its back legs had shrunk to almost nothing.

The odd part is who it was related to. It looked and swam like the famous sea reptiles of its day, but it actually belonged to the archosaur family tree — the same broad branch that later produced crocodiles and dinosaurs.

2. Why the Organs Survived

When researchers scanned the fossil, a dark patch between the ribs caught their eye. Using ultraviolet light photography and mass spectrometry, they identified the traces of individual organs: a large stomach pouch at the front, a liver that still carries hints of the blood pigment haemoglobin, and a simple tube forming the small and large intestines.

It is the oldest known example of a largely complete digestive system preserved in any reptile, published in the journal Science Advances.

3. What a Stomach Can Tell Us

Today's birds and crocodiles have a two-part stomach. Austronaga had just one chamber — evidence that archosaur stomachs started out simple and grew more complex only later in evolution. Its gut was also short and uncomplicated, even though its outer body was highly specialised for ocean life.

A single lucky fossil, in other words, quietly rewrote a small chapter in the story of how reptiles conquered the sea.

Fun fact

The word "Austronaga" hints at both "southern" and a mythical serpent — a fitting name for a long-necked swimmer whose insides came back to us across 245 million years.
