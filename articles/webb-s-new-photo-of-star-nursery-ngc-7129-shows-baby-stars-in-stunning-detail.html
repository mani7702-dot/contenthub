Webb's new photo of star nursery NGC 7129 shows baby stars in stunning detail

2026-10-233 min

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has captured a new infrared image of NGC 7129 — a stellar nursery 3,300 light-years from Earth where more than 130 baby stars younger than 1 million years old are shining through dusty gas clouds.

How Many Baby Stars Are Hidden in This Cosmic Cradle?

A glowing nursery 3,300 light-years away

In the constellation Cepheus, about 3,300 light-years from Earth, lies one of the most photogenic stellar nurseries in our cosmic neighborhood. It is called NGC 7129 — nicknamed the "Flower Bud Nebula" because its dusty clouds look like a tightly closed rose ready to bloom. Thanks to NASA's James Webb Space Telescope, we can now peer inside this dusty cradle and count the baby stars it is hiding.

Inside the infrared glow

On Earth, the night sky often looks dark. But NGC 7129 is shrouded in dust — leftover building blocks from which stars and planets form. Ordinary telescopes that see visible light cannot see through this cosmic fog. That is why Webb uses its powerful infrared eyes to peer through and reveal what is hidden.

In Webb's new image, astronomers spotted over 130 stars that are each less than 1 million years old — still infants compared to our 4.6-billion-year-old Sun. These are not fully grown stars; they are protostars and pre-main-sequence stars still gathering mass before they begin nuclear fusion at their cores.

At the heart of the nursery sits a more mature star called LkH(alpha) 234, which is about 5 to 8 times the mass of our Sun. Its intense light warms the surrounding dust and gas, lighting up the entire nebula like a cosmic lantern.

Spitzer vs. Webb — a clearer cosmic picture

NGC 7129 was first photographed by NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope, which also saw in infrared but with less detail. Comparing the two images lets astronomers watch how stars and their surroundings change over time.

Webb's sharper view reveals tall gas and dust filaments stretching across the scene like brushstrokes. Hot, atomic hydrogen gas glows golden, while cooler gas — shocked by powerful outflows from baby stars — appears red. Those outflows have carved a cavity on the left side of the image, compressing gas and triggering even more star birth.

Fun Fact

The name "LkH(alpha) 234" comes from astronomer Lynn H. Hunt and the fact that it was the 234th object in her catalog showing a bright hydrogen-alpha emission line — the same deep red glow you see in a Bunsen burner flame. This star is so energetic that it lights up the entire nebula around it.

for-grownups: The James Webb Space Telescope (Webb) is a joint NASA/ESA/CSA mission launched December 25, 2021. Its Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) captured the NGC 7129 image (ID 2611), resolving over 130 pre-main-sequence stars — including the 5–8 solar mass star LkHα 234 — less than 1 million years old in Cepheus. Wikipedia confirms NGC 7129 is also called the "Flower Bud Nebula," located 3,300 light-years away, near the older cluster NGC 7142, part of the Cepheus OB2 association.
