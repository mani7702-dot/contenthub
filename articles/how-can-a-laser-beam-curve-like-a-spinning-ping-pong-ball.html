How can a laser beam curve like a spinning ping-pong ball?

2026-11-013 min

A table-tennis spin makes a ball swerve in mid-air. Physicists have just found a matching twist inside a tightly focused laser — and it could matter for quantum computers.

Spin a table-tennis ball just right and it can swerve in mid-air, curving in a way the throw alone would never explain. That familiar piece of playground physics is called the Magnus effect, and it also bends the flight of soccer balls and baseballs. Now an international team has found its mirror image in an unlikely place: inside a tightly focused laser beam, aimed at a single atom.

A beam that isn't quite where you'd expect

Point a laser at an atom and common sense says the strongest interaction should happen right at the beam's brightest spot — dead centre. But squeeze light into a very tight focus and the shape of its electromagnetic field gets more complicated. The point where the laser grips the atom most strongly shifts sideways by a few hundred nanometres, a distance roughly a thousand times thinner than a human hair. That tiny sideways slide is the optical version of the ping-pong curve, and it had been predicted on paper but never measured until now.

To catch it, researchers at the Paul Scherrer Institute in Switzerland, working with colleagues at ETH Zurich and the University of Amsterdam, trapped a single calcium ion — an electrically charged atom — and slid it through different parts of a focused laser beam. "Our ion acts like a tiny sensor that we can use to feel out the structure of the laser light," explained first author Philip Leindecker.

Why an atom-size swerve matters

The experiment, reported in the journal Physical Review Letters, produced one surprise that delighted the team: the size of the shift depends only on the light's wavelength, not on how tightly the laser is focused. That makes the effect predictable and therefore easier to design around.

That matters because quantum computers often use precisely aimed laser pulses to nudge qubits — the quantum equivalent of bits — into the exact states they need. If the optical Magnus effect is ignored, it could nudge them slightly wrong and add errors. Handled cleverly, though, the same sideways force could be used as a tool to link qubits together for more complex calculations.

From the sports field to the quantum lab

The story is a neat reminder that physics loves a good analogy. The same idea that curves a spinning ball through the air also shows up when light and matter meet at the smallest scales. Researchers at the University of Amsterdam first sketched the prediction years ago; this is the first time anyone has watched it happen.

Fun fact: The optical shift here is only a few hundred nanometres — but if you could scale it up to the size of a table-tennis table, it would be like the ball leaving your paddle aimed at the net and instead curling neatly into the far corner.
