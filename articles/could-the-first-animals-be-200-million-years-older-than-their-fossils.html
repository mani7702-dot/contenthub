Could the First Animals Be 200 Million Years Older Than Their Fossils?

2026-10-314 min

A new Oxford-led study suggests animal life may have begun 800 to 700 million years ago — hundreds of millions of years before the earliest known animal fossils.

Could the First Animals Be 200 Million Years Older Than Their Fossils?

When did the first animals appear on Earth? Most of us picture the answer sitting in the fossil record — but that record may be hiding a secret. A new Oxford-led study suggests animals could have begun 200 million years earlier than their oldest fossils show, meaning the very first animals may have lived before, or even during, some of the coldest chapters in Earth's history.

A Puzzle Stretching Back 590 Million Years

The confusion centres on a famous fossil site in China called the Weng'an Biota, roughly 590 million years old. Its rocks preserve microscopic life in astonishing detail, yet none of it can be confidently called an animal. Scientists had argued that if animals had existed back then, such a well-preserved site should have captured them.

The new team put that idea to the test in Mongolia's Kheseen Biota. This deposit is over 40 million years younger — and animals are known to have existed by then. Researchers sifted through more than 140 samples under scanning electron microscopes, finding beautifully preserved new species, including tiny spiny organisms and embryo-like fossils. Yet still no animals.

That absence, the team argues, proves something important: even excellent fossil beds can miss animal life if the chemistry and habitat aren't right.

Winding the Clock Back

So the scientists tried a different approach. They looked at far older deposits, 850 to 730 million years old, and fed them into a method called molecular-clock analysis. This compares genetic differences between living species to estimate when shared ancestors lived.

Using older reference points instead of the younger Weng'an site, the estimated origin of animals jumped backwards by about 200 million years — to somewhere between 800 and 700 million years ago.

Life Before the Deep Freeze

If animals really did begin that early, they would have lived alongside — or just before — the Cryogenian Period, when glaciers crept across the planet in episodes nicknamed "Snowball Earth." The earliest animals would likely have been small and soft-bodied, with no shells or bones, making them extremely hard to fossilise.

Prof. Ross Anderson put it simply: until firmer evidence turns up, "the precise birth date of the animal kingdom remains uncertain."

Fun fact: Chemical traces in ancient rocks hint that sponges may have lived at least 650 million years ago — tens of millions of years before the first hard, unmistakable animal fossils.
