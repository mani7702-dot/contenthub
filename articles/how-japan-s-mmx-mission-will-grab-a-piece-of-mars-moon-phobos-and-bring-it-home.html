How Japan's MMX mission will grab a piece of Mars' moon Phobos and bring it home

2026-10-224 min

A Japanese spacecraft is heading to Mars' tiny moon Phobos to scoop up some dust and bring it back to Earth — the first mission ever to return samples from a Martian moon.

How will Japan grab a piece of Mars' moon and bring it home?

A tiny moon with a giant secret

Look up at the night sky and you can probably spot the red dot of Mars — our neighboring planet that looks like a bright, fiery eye. Orbiting around Mars, closer than you might think, are two tiny moons named Phobos and Deimos. They look like irregular, potato-shaped rocks, nothing like Earth's smooth, round Moon.

Scientists have been wondering: how did these tiny moons form? Are they escaped asteroids captured by Mars' gravity, or were they born from a giant crash long ago, when a space rock slammed into Mars and flung debris into orbit?

Japan has a bold plan to find out — send a robotic spacecraft to grab a piece of Phobos and bring it back to Earth.

The mission that crosses the solar system

The mission is called MMX — short for Martian Moons eXploration — and it belongs to JAXA, Japan's space agency. On October 19, 2026, MMX launched aboard an H3 rocket, beginning a journey of over 600 million kilometers (373 million miles).

That is a long trip! It takes about 10 months just to reach Mars. Once there, in August 2027, the spacecraft will swing into orbit around the Red Planet and study both moons from afar.

The robot visitor to a rubbery moon

Phobos is oddly shaped — it is actually shrinking as it slowly spirals closer to Mars, pulled by tidal forces. Some scientists think that in about 50 million years, Phobos will either crash into Mars or break apart and form a faint ring.

MMX will send down a small robot rover named Idefix (which means "stuck" in Japanese — a fitting name for something exploring a surface that might trap it!). Idefix rolls onto Phobos's bumpy, dusty ground in 2029 to map out the best spot where the main spacecraft can collect samples.

The big grab-and-go

In 2030, the main MMX spacecraft will make a soft landing on Phobos. A robotic arm — like a metal reacher-grabber — will extend down and use a device called a pneumatic sampler to drill into the surface and scoop up more than 10 grams of dust and rock (about the weight of two paperclips, but from outer space!).

The samples get sealed inside a special container, then the spacecraft fires its rockets to break free from Phobos. It begins the long journey home, aiming to drop a return capsule on Earth in 2031 — carrying the first pieces of a Martian moon that humanity has ever touched.

Try this at home

Make a "moon landing" game! Fill a shallow tray with flour or sand, scatter small pebbles and crumbs on top (that's the moon's dusty surface), then use a spoon or chopsticks to very gently "drill" and collect just a pinch. Notice how delicate the surface is — and imagine doing this in a place that is thousands of times farther away than the Moon!

Fun Fact

Phobos orbits Mars so closely that it rises in the west and sets in the east — the opposite of our Moon — and it circles the planet in just 7 hours and 39 minutes. From Phobos's surface, Mars fills nearly one-third of the sky and looks three times bigger than a full Moon does from Earth.
