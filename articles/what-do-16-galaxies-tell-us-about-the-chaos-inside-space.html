What do 16 galaxies tell us about the chaos inside space?

2026-10-203 min

NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory teamed up with Hubble, JWST, and other telescopes to release a gallery of 16 galaxies — each one a different color, shape, and story of cosmic violence, star birth, and black-hole power.

Galaxies are like cosmic gemstones — some glitter with sweeping spiral arms, others are stretched and smashed by crashes with their neighbors. NASA has just put together a new gallery of 16 galaxies, each one photographed by some of the most powerful telescopes ever sent into space. Together, the pictures show that the universe is far from quiet: it is a place where black holes belch, stars explode, and whole galaxies tangle together in slow-motion dances.

Three families of galaxies

Astronomers sort galaxies into three big groups, kind of like sorting jewelry by shape:

  • Spiral galaxies — like our own Milky Way — have pinwheel arms where new stars are born. Messier 33 and NGC 3938 are face-on spirals that let astronomers see every arm in sharp detail.
  • Elliptical galaxies are rounder and hold older stars, often formed when two galaxies smash into each other and merge.
  • Irregular galaxies come in wild, unpredictable shapes, churning out stars in bursts.

Each galaxy in the gallery was chosen because it shows something special — not just beauty, but a process that astronomers can study up close.

Black holes that shape whole galaxies

At the center of most big galaxies lurks a supermassive black hole — millions or billions of times heavier than our Sun. When it is active, it can blow out a jet of energy that shoots across thousands of light-years. In the gallery, Centaurus A (the closest active galaxy to us) shows a jet so bright it stretches 25,000 light-years into space — powered by its central black hole gulping down gas.

Messier 106 also lights up the page with a pair of jets that twist and spin, lit up here by Chandra's X-ray vision seeing the million-degree gas they heat as they travel.

When galaxies crash into each other

Some of the most dramatic pictures come from galaxies in the middle of colliding. Arp 143 shows two galaxies in the act of smashing — it looks like a bullseye made of stars. NGC 3256 and the dusty starburst II Zw 096 are also caught in the chaos of merger, where gravity pulls stars and gas into new arrangements and triggers bursts of baby star formation.

These collisions are not quick. They take hundreds of millions of years — slower than a human lifetime, but a blink compared to the age of the universe.

The Whale and the Cigar

Two galaxies stand out for the huge halos of gas around them. NGC 4631, nicknamed the Whale Galaxy, sprawls on its side with a stream of million-degree gas heated by exploding stars. Messier 82, the Cigar Galaxy, shoots out powerful winds that carry material thousands of light-years into space — spreading the chemical elements (the stuff of our bodies) far beyond their home galaxy.

By combining X-rays (Chandra) with light from other parts of the rainbow (Hubble, Webb, and more), astronomers can see both the hot gas and the cold dust, getting the full story of how each galaxy lives, breathes, and changes.

Try it yourself: look up on a clear night and find the brightest blob you can see. That is light from billions of stars bundled into one small fuzzy patch — and every patch you find is a whole galaxy like the ones in this gallery, just much farther away. The Andromeda Galaxy is the closest big spiral you can see without a telescope, and it is on its way toward us for a slow-motion collision in about 4.5 billion years.

For the grown-ups

The 16-image gallery was released by NASA's Chandra X-Ray Observatory (Oct 2026) and combines data from Chandra, JWST, Hubble, IXPE (Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer), Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, NuSTAR, and ground-based observatories. Each image isolates X-ray emissions from gas at temperatures of millions of degrees, revealing energy from black-hole jets, supernova remnants, and stellar winds that optical light alone would miss. The gallery is publicly available at the Chandra Photo Album (chandra.harvard.edu/photo/2026/galaxies/).
