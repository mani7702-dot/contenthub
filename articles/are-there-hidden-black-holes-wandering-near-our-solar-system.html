Are there hidden black holes wandering near our Solar System?

2026-10-214 min

New Gaia satellite data hints that lonely stellar-mass black holes may be drifting through the nearby interstellar clouds, but the search so far has come up empty.

The galaxy next door

Look around the night sky and you can see thousands of stars — but the things we cannot see may be just as interesting. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is thought to contain anywhere from 100 million to 1 billion tiny “stellar-mass” black holes. These are the collapsed cores of massive stars that exploded long ago. They are invisible to our telescopes unless they happen to be eating material from a nearby star. But what about the loners — the black holes drifting through space with nothing to feed on? Should we expect to find some of them drifting right through our cosmic neighbourhood?

Black holes that nobody sees

Most black holes we know about are part of a pair: the black hole tugs at a companion star and steals its gas, glowing brightly before devouring it. But models say the majority of stellar-mass black holes are isolated — living alone with no star to feed on. Left in the dark, they would give off almost no light at all, drifting silently between the stars. They are the universe’s most invisible neighbours.

So why not just point Gaia at them? The European Space Agency’s Gaia telescope maps the positions and motions of over a billion stars with incredible precision. Astronomers led by Abdurakhmon Nosirov (Fudan University) combed through Gaia’s third data release, looking within 50 light-years (15 parsecs) of our Solar System for objects that might be lone black holes.

The search comes up short

The team found five promising candidates sitting close to the plane of the Milky Way. To check if they were really accreting gas, they looked for infrared light in the CatWISE catalogue and radio waves in the RACS and VLASS surveys. If the black holes were quietly eating gas from the interstellar clouds around them, they should have shown up there. They did not. The team concludes these sources are most likely not black holes at all — instead, the dense star fields near the Galactic plane tricked Gaia’s measurements.

What makes a cloud “liveable” for a black hole?

Here is the interesting part — the researchers say only inside what are called Local Interstellar Clouds would a quiet black hole stand a chance of being spotted. The Solar System is currently sailing near the edge of the Local Interstellar Cloud, a puff of gas that surrounds us. In these slightly denser regions, a black hole could pull in a thin soup of interstellar gas and spark faint, detectable infrared or radio signals. Far from these clouds, the gas is too thin — and the black hole becomes forever invisible.

So the hunt continues. Future telescopes that combine views across radio, optical, and X-ray bands may finally catch a cold, silent black hole in the act of drifting through one of our cloudy cosmic corridors.

Try this at home

Make an invisible neighbour! Fill a clear bowl with water and sprinkle in a few drops of milk to make the water cloudy. Drop a single drop of food colouring near the rim — watch how it slowly spirals into the cloud. The coloured drop is the black hole: you can only tell it is there because it moves the “clouds” around it with its invisible pull.

Fun Fact

A single teaspoon of a black hole’s material would weigh billions of tons on Earth — but a stellar-mass black hole is only about 10 kilometres (6 miles) across. The whole Milky Way contains hundreds of millions of them.

for-grownups:

A research team led by Abdurakhmon Nosirov (Fudan University, Shanghai) used Gaia Data Release 3 to hunt for isolated stellar-mass black holes within 15 parsecs (~50 light-years) of the Solar System. Theoretical models predict 10^8 to 10^9 such objects formed by full gravitational collapse in the Milky Way, most of them starved of a companion star to feed on. The team identified six astrometric candidates close to the Galactic plane; after removing a brown dwarf mimic, five remained. However, infrared catalog CatWISE and radio catalogs RACS and VLASS show no detecting flux, suggesting these sources are likely spurious astrometric solutions from crowded background fields rather than accreting black holes. Published in The Astrophysical Journal, Vol 1004, Issue 1 (2026), DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/ae6805.
