Why is the sea salty?

2026-10-193 min

If you leave a glass of sea water in the sun, it does not dry up the way a puddle does. That is a clue to where the salt comes from.

If you filled a glass with sea water and left it on a sunny windowsill, the water would slowly disappear and a crust of salt would be left behind.

That little experiment is the whole story of the salty sea, just faster.

Rain eats rock

Rain is not plain water. As it falls it picks up a gas from the air, which makes it very slightly sour. Over time, that faint sourness can dissolve tiny bits of rock — the same way a little vinegar can loosen a coin.

The rain then runs downhill. Streams join into rivers, and rivers carry that dissolved rock, in invisible form, all the way to the sea.

Every river on Earth does this, all the time. The Ganges, the Amazon, the Mississippi, and thousands of quieter streams are all delivering a slow, tiny load of minerals to the ocean.

The water leaves, the salt stays

Once the water reaches the sea, the Sun warms it and some of it turns back into vapour and rises into the sky. Later it falls as rain and goes around again.

The salt cannot rise with it. It stays in the sea.

So the sea loses water but keeps salt, while the rivers keep bringing more. Over a very, very long time — millions of years — the salt slowly piled up.

A salty enough sea?

Seawater contains about thirty-five handfuls of salt for every thousand handfuls of water. It is not as salty as the Dead Sea, which is so concentrated you can float without trying, but it is far too salty to drink.

That is why being stranded at sea means being thirsty even while surrounded by water. Your body needs the water, but drinking seawater makes you thirstier, because your kidneys need fresh water to get rid of the extra salt.

Try this: if you are near the sea this weekend, taste a drop of water on your finger. Then rinse your hands in a fresh stream. Same water cycle, two very different tastes.
