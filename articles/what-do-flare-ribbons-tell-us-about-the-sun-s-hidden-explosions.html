What Do Flare Ribbons Tell Us About the Sun's Hidden Explosions?

2026-10-243 min

Solar flares are among the Sun's most powerful explosions, but they happen behind the Sun's bright glare. Scientists are now using flare ribbons — glowing streaks that act like a flare's footprints — to see what these explosions really look like.

What Are Those Glowing Streaks on the Sun?

Imagine the Sun is a giant ball of hot, glowing gas. Every once in a while, it burps out a burst of energy called a solar flare — the strongest explosion in our Solar System. These flares happen when the Sun's invisible magnetic fields get twisted and tangled like rubber bands, then suddenly snap and reconnect. But because the Sun is so bright, scientists can't see the flare itself. Instead, they watch for something called a flare ribbon.

The Sun's Fireworks Explained

Flares come in five strength levels — A, B, C, M, and X — with each letter ten times more powerful than the one before. When the magnetic fields reconnect, they release huge amounts of energy all at once. The flare ribbon traces where that energy hits the Sun's surface, like footprints showing where a flare ran.

These ribbons follow the Sun's magnetic field lines into the chromosphere — the Sun's colorful atmospheric layer. When high-energy particles slam in, the ribbons light up. NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory watches these ribbons grow and change, helping scientists understand how the Sun's invisible magnetic dance creates visible explosions.

Peeling Back the Sun's Secrets

A new study in The Astrophysical Journal zooms in on the tiny pieces inside flare ribbons. The lead author, Marcel Corchado Albelo from the U.S. National Science Foundation, works with the Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope (DKIST) in Hawaii. By tracking how the ribbons' complexity evolves, the team maps how energy pours out during a flare. The ribbons break apart and reform like a zipper opening and closing — each tiny flash is a clue to how the Sun stores and releases magnetic energy.

Try this at home

Grab a bar magnet from the fridge and sprinkle iron filings on a piece of paper. Drag the magnet underneath, and watch the filings line up in curved patterns — those are magnetic field lines, just like the ones on the Sun. The tighter you bend the magnet first, the messier the pattern, showing how twisted fields store energy before they snap.

Fun Fact

The strongest solar flare ever recorded — an X28-class flare in September 2003 — was so powerful it briefly knocked out GPS and radio signals across half the planet. Astronomers sometimes call it part of the famous "Halloween Solar Storms."

<div class="for-grownups">

For grown-ups: Flare ribbons are emission features in the Sun's transition region and chromosphere that form during solar flares as conjugate footpoints of magnetic reconnection. The study "Evolution of Spatial Complexity in Flare Ribbon Substructure and Its Relationship to Magnetic Reconnection Dynamics" (The Astrophysical Journal) uses high-resolution tracking of ribbon substructure to understand how magnetic energy is released during flare reconnection. NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) carries the Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) and Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI) for continuous full-disk solar imaging. The Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope (DKIST), a 4-meter solar telescope at Haleakala Observatory, is managed by the National Solar Observatory and funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation. Solar flares release energy equivalent to billions of hydrogen bombs and can produce coronal mass ejections that disrupt satellites and power grids.
