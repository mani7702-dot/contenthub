Is Mars still alive? The giant canyon that proves our red neighbor is still changing today

2026-10-244 min

Scientists have found that Valles Marineris — the Solar System's largest canyon, stretching nearly 4,000 km across Mars — is still actively reshaping the Red Planet today, with fault lines deep beneath the canyon slipping and triggering marsquakes.

Is Mars still alive? The giant canyon that proves our red neighbor is still changing today

A scar too big to ignore

When the Sun rises over Mars, it casts shadows across a wound in the planet's surface so vast it can be seen from space: Valles Marineris. This isn't just a canyon — it's a chasm system stretching nearly 4,000 kilometers along the Martian equator. To put that in perspective, it's nine times longer, twenty times wider, and five times deeper than Earth's Grand Canyon. If you could stand on its rim, you would be looking into a gash over seven kilometers deep — more than a dozen Empire State Buildings stacked end to end.

For decades, scientists assumed this was a fossil landscape, carved by processes that ended billions of years ago. Mars has no tectonic plates to scrape and grind, no moving crust to build mountains or tear them down. It seemed dead.

But the ground is still moving.

The quake that spoke from the depths

On August 25, 2021, an invisible messenger traveled through the body of Mars. It was a marsquake — designated S0976a — detected by NASA's InSight lander, which sat on the floor of the Elysium Planitia plain with a seismometer pressed into the dust. What made this event special was where it came from: the opposite side of the planet, beneath the western edge of Valles Marineris.

At first, scientists couldn't tell what had caused it. On Mars, seismic waves can be triggered by meteoroid impacts, landslides, or faulting. The team — led by Dr. Jung-Hun Song at Imperial College London — had to test every possibility against the data.

They compared the seismic readings and satellite images taken before and after the event. No fresh impact crater. No new landslide scars. The only explanation that fit all the evidence was a strike-slip earthquake: two blocks of crust sliding horizontally past each other along a steep fault line, roughly 20 to 40 kilometers deep beneath the canyon floor.

Ancient scars, modern stress

Here is what makes this discovery profound: the fault that slipped was not a new crack. It was a fracture formed billions of years ago, during the violent geological upheaval that created Valles Marineris itself. The canyon began when the Tharsis region to the west grew so massive from volcanic eruptions that the crust cracked — a process similar to Earth's East African Rift, but on a scale that dwarfs anything we see on our planet.

As Mars slowly cooled over eons, the whole planet contracted. The ancient faults, already weakened by time, began to slip again — not from the canyon-forming forces of old, but from the gentle, relentless stresses of a shrinking world.

"The orientation of the faulting matches what we'd expect if ancient, pre-existing structures were slipping under modern stresses," explains Dr. Doyeon Kim of Imperial, senior author on the study. "It shows that structures created billions of years ago can remain important and still control where the crust breaks today."

Three places where Mars still trembles

Valles Marineris is now the third confirmed site of active faulting on present-day Mars. The other two — Cerberus Fossae and the southern plains of Elysium Planitia — were also discovered through InSight data. What this means is that Mars is not the inert rock we once thought. It is a planet that still breathes, still shifts, still quietly reshapes itself one small quake at a time.

For future astronauts, these findings are more than curiosity — they are a map of where the ground might move beneath their feet.

Try this at home

Fill a baking dish with flour, then gently sprinkle cinnamon on top so it forms a thin, even layer. Push your finger straight down through the flour until your knuckles press against the cinnamon. Now slide your finger sideways — the cinnamon layer will crack and shift, just like the fault beneath Valles Marineris. The flour represents Mars' crust, the cinnamon is the ancient fault plane, and your finger pushing sideways is the strike-slip motion that triggered the marsquake.

Fun Fact

Valles Marineris is so enormous that if it were on Earth, you could fit the entire United Kingdom inside it. Its length — nearly 4,000 kilometers — spans almost exactly 10% of the distance from London to Tokyo. The canyon is also deep enough to hide Mount Everest with more than a kilometer to spare.
