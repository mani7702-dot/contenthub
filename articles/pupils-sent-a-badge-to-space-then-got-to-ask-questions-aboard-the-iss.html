Pupils sent a badge to space — then got to ask questions aboard the ISS

2026-10-213 min

A school in Wales designed a badge that flew to the International Space Station, where it floated in zero gravity — then the students got to video-chat with NASA astronaut Frank Rubio, who holds the record for the longest single spaceflight.

How did a tiny badge from a Welsh school end up floating in the International Space Station?

A badge that earned its wings

Most school art projects stay pinned to a noticeboard. But a class of pupils at Ysgol Cemaes, a primary school in the coastal village of Cemaes on Anglesey, Wales, made something a little out of this world — literally.

As part of a project led by Dr Melanie Coathup, a former pupil of the school who now works closely with astronauts, the children designed their very own space badge. In early 2026, that badge was packed up, cleared for launch, and sent into orbit aboard a cargo spacecraft to the International Space Station.

Once there, the badge did what only objects do in space — it floated. The students later saw photos of their creation drifting in zero gravity, tumbling gently past the station's windows, with the curved limb of Earth glowing behind it.

A video call with a record-holder

Meeting the people who make space travel possible is the stuff of dreams. For these pupils, that dream came true on a Wednesday when they were given a live video call with NASA astronaut Frank Rubio.

Rubio is no ordinary space traveler. He holds the American record for the longest single spaceflight: he spent 371 days aboard the ISS after a problem with his Soyuz return ship meant he and his crewmates had to wait for a replacement. During that year in orbit, he conducted dozens of science experiments, took breathtaking photos of Earth, and even grew space-grown peppers.

He is now set to fly again — this time as part of Artemis III, humanity's next planned trip to the surface of the Moon.

What does a badge feel like in space?

If you have ever wondered what happens to a paper badge when gravity turns off, the answer is: it drifts. Without "down", it simply hovers and spins wherever the air currents take it, catching the light from the station's windows.

That's exactly what happened to the Welsh pupils' badge. Floating there, half a world away from the playground where it was made, it carried with it the names and drawings of every child in the class — a tiny piece of Earth, orbiting Earth, brought back by photos and stories.

The pupils had spent weeks learning about life in space: how astronauts brush their teeth, how they sleep strapped to a wall, and how even a drop of water becomes a floating sphere. Then came their questions:

"How do you know which way is up?"
"Do the stars look different from there?"
"Did our badge float nicely?"

Rubio answered each one with a grin, holding the badge up to the camera and letting it spin slowly in front of the window — a small, spinning souvenir from home, 400 kilometres above the planet.

Try this at home

Make a badge (or a simple paper cut-out), tape it to a balloon or lightweight string, and hold it in front of a fan on low — that's your poor-person's zero gravity. Watch it tilt, drift, and catch the air the way the ISS badge did.

Then ask yourself: if my badge could visit another world, what would I want it to bring back a photo of? Draw it, label it, and pin it somewhere you'll see it every day — a tiny reminder that even the smallest creations can travel farther than we imagine.

Fun fact: The International Space Station travels at about 28,000 km/h (17,500 mph) — that's roughly seven-and-a-half times faster than a bullet — and it has to dodge space junk the size of a marble by steering its robotic arm like a giant space broom.
