How did giant stick insects hide their true identity for decades — and what DNA revealed?

2026-10-243 min

Scientists thought one common Australian stick insect was just two species. A close re-examination of museum specimens turned that into five — including two brand-new species with strange \'lidded\' eggs no one had ever seen.

How did giant stick insects hide their true identity for decades — and what DNA revealed?

The case of the mistaken stick bug

Imagine you are a detective, but instead of fingerprints you only have leaves and twigs. That is exactly the job facing scientists who study stick insects — bugs so good at looking like twigs that they have fooled researchers for decades.

A team from the University of Sydney decided to take a second look at one group of Australian stick insects. They pulled out old specimens from museums, measured every bump and ridge, and even sequenced their DNA. The result? What everyone thought was two species turned out to be five.

The mystery of the lidded egg

Among the surprises was a brand-new species with the strangest eggs anyone has ever seen in this group. Instead of smooth or ridged shells, these eggs come with a tiny lid — like a miniature bottle cap that snaps shut. No stick insect in the world was known to make such eggs before.

Scientists named it Anchiale mabiensis, after the rainforest where it was found. They also brought back a name — Anchiale robusta — that had been forgotten. This big, chunky stick insect had been hiding in plain sight, lumped together with two other species for decades. Only when the researchers compared DNA and studied the shapes of their bodies did it become clear: it was its own species all along.

Why being a phantom is hard to study

Stick insects belong to an ancient order called Phasmatodea — from a Greek word that means “phantom.” That is their superpower: they vanish. Their bodies match twigs, their legs bend like branches, and some even rock back and forth like a leaf in the wind.

But being a good phantom makes you hard to study. Two stick insects that look almost identical might actually be completely different species that just evolved to look alike. That is why the research team used clues from both the museum and the lab — comparing old specimens and reading their DNA — to finally tell them apart.

Try this at home

Go on a “twig hunt” in your house or garden. Look at pencils, small branches, and even broom handles. Can you spot anything that looks less like a snack and more like something alive? That is the trick stick insects use every day. Now imagine trying to pick out real twigs from fake ones when they could be anywhere. That is how the scientists felt!

Fun Fact

Australia has 130 known stick insect species — that is roughly one for every 200,000 people in the country! Around the world there are over 3,600 species, making Phasmatodea one of the most diverse groups of plant-eating insects.

The reclassification shows how much insect biodiversity still goes unnoticed. Museum specimens and citizen-science photos on iNaturalist and the Atlas of Living Australia provided key data. The team estimates that many more Australian stick insect species await formal description, especially in tropical regions where few specimens have been studied.
