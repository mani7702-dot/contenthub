Did a giant space rock smash into Earth to make our Moon?

The collision that changed everything

Imagine a space rock so big it was almost as large as Mars. Now imagine it slamming into the early Earth at such a colossal speed that the entire planet glowed white-hot and molten. That is exactly what scientists believe happened about 4.5 billion years ago — and the result is something we see in our sky every single night: the Moon.

The space rock was called Theia (pronounced THEE-uh), named after a Greek goddess. It was roughly the size of Mars, orbiting the Sun in step with Earth at a stable gravitational point called the L4 Lagrange point — a cosmic parking spot where Earth's gravity and the Sun's gravity balance perfectly.

A slow dance gone wrong

For millions of years, Theia danced around Earth in this stable rhythm. But gravitational tugs from other planets — especially Venus and Jupiter — nudged its orbit. Eventually, Theia's path crossed Earth's, and the two bodies collided at an angle of about 45 degrees.

The impact was unlike anything we can picture. Theia struck Earth at speeds of over 9 kilometers per second (that is about 32,000 kilometers per hour). The force was so great that the entire planet became a searing ball of molten rock. Theia's iron core sank down into Earth's core, but most of the lighter rock and debris from both Theia and Earth was blasted sky-high — some of it flying faster than Earth's escape velocity, while the rest settled into orbit.

How debris became our Moon

The debris that stayed in orbit around Earth did something amazing: it began to stick together. Scientists think this process started quickly — within just a few years, the innermost material clumped together, and within a few hundred years, the Moon had fully formed.

This is why the Moon's composition matches Earth's so closely. The Moon rocks that Apollo astronauts brought back contain the same oxygen isotopes as rocks from Earth's mantle — a chemical fingerprint that tells us they shared the same origin.

Try this at home

Fill a shallow baking pan with flour. Sprinkle a thin layer of cocoa powder on top. Now drop a marble from about 30 centimeters above the surface. Watch how the cocoa dust explodes outward, with some of it landing outside the impact crater. The flour represents Earth's crust, the cocoa layer is Theia's outer rock, and the marble is the impact. Just like the real collision — the material that flew outward eventually settled in a ring, just like the debris that formed our Moon.

Fun Fact

The Moon is actually moving away from us. Because of the gravitational dance between Earth and Moon, the Moon drifts about 3.8 centimeters farther away each year. That means when the Moon first formed, it was so close to Earth that it took up about half the sky — twice as big as it looks today.
