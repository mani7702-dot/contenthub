5 reasons smoky peat fires turn the sky over Borneo a rusty orange

2026-10-214 min

Each year in Southeast Asia, dry-season fires light up forests and swamps across Indonesian Borneo. The flames are mostly hidden underground, burning through layers of ancient peat, and the smoke they make travels thousands of miles across the ocean.

5 reasons smoky peat fires turn the sky over Borneo a rusty orange

If you have ever seen photos from Indonesian Borneo in the dry season, you probably thought someone had spilled a giant pot of rust-colored paint across the sky. That is not paint. It is smoke — and the reason it looks so strange is because the fire is mostly underground.

1. The real fire lives beneath your feet

Most forest fires lick up into the trees and go out when it rains. But across Borneo, the trees are standing on a layer of peat — a thick carpet of half-rotted plants that built up over thousands of years, like a soggy sponge made of swamp. When the dry season arrives, the water table drops, and that sponge catches light. The flames sink down into the peat itself, burning a little underground river of carbon all the way across the island.

2. Peat is a giant below-ground bonfire

Peat can be several metres thick. Once it starts smoldering, the fire creeps sideways and downward, where it is cool, damp, and invisible from above. That is why buckets of water from the sky often barely touch it. The deeper the peat, the longer the fire burns — and the more smoke pours out.

3. The smoke rides the wind for thousands of miles

Indonesia sits right in the path of the northeast monsoon. From May through October, that seasonal wind sweeps west-to-east across the equator, picking up smoke from Borneo and carrying it over the South China Sea. By the time it reaches Singapore or the Philippines, what was once a local blaze has become a regional blanket.

4. Tiny particles are what make the sky glow

The rusty orange is not one big blob of color — it is billions of microscopic soot particles, each about a tenth the width of a human hair. When sunlight hits them, the blue wavelengths scatter away and the longer oranges and reds dominate. That is why the sun looks like a blurry coin seen through old glass.

5. Every breath counts — even far from the flames

When the haze rolls in, hospitals see more people with coughs and tight chests. That is because the finest particles sink deep into the lungs. But here is the hopeful part: the fires themselves only burn a few months each year, and when the rainy season returns, the sky clears.

Fun Fact: A single hectare of tropical peat can store over 5,000 tonnes of carbon — more than a hundred times what a tree of the same size holds. That is why peat fires don't just make smoky sunsets; they puff out far more CO₂ than the trees around them.

Try this at home

Light a beeswax candle in a darkened room, then blow it out and watch the smoke drift upward in a slow, steady ribbon. The way the smoke spreads and thins shows you how invisible particles move through the air — just the same particles that travel across an ocean to turn a whole sky orange.
