How Did a 147-Year-Old Physics Rule Suddenly Grow a New Direction?

More than a century ago, a young American physicist named Edwin Hall noticed something odd. When he sent electricity through a thin strip of metal and held a magnet so its field pushed straight down through the strip, the flowing charges veered off to one side. That sideways swerve, called the Hall effect, has been a workhorse of physics and engineering ever since.

Now a team at Carnegie Mellon University has shown the old rule has more room in it than anyone thought.

A sideways nudge that reveals hidden secrets

Here is the everyday version. Imagine a river of tiny charged particles flowing down a channel. Hold a magnet above it, and the moving charges get pushed toward one bank. That builds up a small voltage you can measure.

The size and direction of that voltage tells scientists a lot: whether the current is carried by positive or negative charges, how many carriers there are, and how easily they move. It is the principle behind magnetic sensors in cars, keyboards, and medical imaging machines.

For nearly 147 years, the rule seemed simple. The field had to point perpendicular to the material — straight through it — for the effect to show up.

What the team actually changed

The researchers, led by physicist Simranjeet Singh in the Lab for Investigating Quantum Materials, Interfaces and Devices, worked with a crystal called tantalum iridium telluride. They shaved it down to just a few atomic layers and laid it against a thin magnetic layer.

That close pairing let the magnetism seep into the normally non-magnetic crystal. When they cooled it and measured carefully, they found two signals at once: the familiar Hall response, plus a second one tied to magnetism lying flat within the material's plane.

"We've shown that that's not true — you can also get a response when the field is in-plane," Singh said. The results were published in Nature Materials.

Why it matters

A single ultrathin device can now detect magnetic fields along more than one direction at the same time. Before, you needed two separate sensors to measure two directions. The work points toward simpler, more compact magnetic sensing for electronics, transportation, and medical devices.

Fun fact

You have almost certainly used the Hall effect today without knowing it. The tiny sensor that tells your phone which way it is turned, or that helps a car's wheel-speed system count rotations, often relies on this exact sideways-swerve trick — first spotted with little more than a strip of metal and a magnet.
