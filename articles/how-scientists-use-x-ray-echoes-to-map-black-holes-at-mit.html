How Scientists Use X-ray Echoes to Map Black Holes at MIT

How Scientists Use X-ray Echoes to Map Black Holes at MIT

Listening to the echoes of black holes

Imagine you are standing in a canyon and you shout — you hear the echo come bouncing back. Now imagine doing the same thing, but the "canyon" is a supermassive black hole millions of times heavier than our Sun, and the "shout" is a burst of X-ray light. At MIT's Kavli Institute for Astrophysics and Space Research, Professor Erin Kara is teaching us how to listen to those echoes.

Professor Kara's team studies what happens when matter swirls toward a black hole. As it spirals inward, it heats up and blazes with X-ray light — so bright that some of it shoots out and strikes the inner edge of the disk, creating a reflected "echo" that arrives a fraction of a second later. By measuring that tiny delay, scientists can map the region around the black hole, which is otherwise invisible.

A PhD built on ancient X-ray data

The story began in 2009, when Professor Kara was a Ph.D. student at Cambridge. She was reviewing old X-ray data from Chandra and XMM-Newton — two veteran space telescopes that have been watching the sky for decades. Amid that archive, she spotted something exciting: time delays between direct X-rays and their reflected echoes, coming from the region just outside the black hole's event horizon.

"It felt very discovery-driven," she later said. Every time delay was like a new piece of a puzzle, slowly revealing how black holes feed and grow.

Modern tools, ancient mysteries

Today, Professor Kara works with data from cutting-edge missions. The XRISM space telescope (launched in September 2023 by Japan's JAXA with NASA and ESA) captures X-rays with incredible precision. Meanwhile, NASA's NICER instrument on the International Space Station watches for rapid changes in X-ray brightness.

These tools have shown that black holes are far more dynamic than scientists once thought. They can "turn on and off" at rates that are much faster than expected, and matter gets pulled in faster than previously believed.

Professor Kara's team also looks ahead to missions like ULTRASAT and LISA, which will scan the sky continuously for new X-ray sources and gravitational waves. With every new echo, we learn more about how the most extreme objects in the universe behave.

Try it yourself: On a quiet evening, go outside and look up at the night sky. Pick a bright star and imagine it as a sun with planets of its own. Now imagine a black hole passing in front of it — the star would flicker and brighten as the black hole's gravity bends the light, a phenomenon called gravitational lensing. You can explore this on free astronomy websites like Stellarium (stellarium.org), where you can zoom across the galaxy from your computer.

Fun Fact

The smallest time delay ever measured between direct X-rays and their echo from a black hole is less than a millisecond — that means light travels the distance of just a few hundred kilometers in that blink of an eye. For a supermassive black hole, that is comparable to the distance from Earth to the Moon.

For the grown-ups

Professor Erin Kara at MIT's Kavli Institute for Astrophysics and Space Research studies X-ray reverberation mapping — measuring tiny time delays between direct X-ray emissions and their echoes bouncing off the inner regions of black hole accretion disks — to map the extreme physics around supermassive black holes. This technique, first detected in 2009 using archival data from XMM-Newton and Chandra, relies on instruments like the XRISM space telescope (launched September 2023) and NICER on the ISS to capture the rapid X-ray variability. The time delays reveal the size and structure of the corona and inner disk, constraining how matter behaves in the strongest gravitational fields known.
