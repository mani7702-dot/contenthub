Why does a tiny crystal defect lose energy so fast — and what does it mean for quantum computers?

The secret inside every diamond

If you hold a diamond up to the light, you see sparkle. But deep inside its crystal lattice — the neat grid of carbon atoms — there are tiny mistakes. A missing atom here, a stray nitrogen there. These imperfections are called spin defects, and even though they are smaller than anything you can see, they might hold a big key to the future of computing.

When light turns a defect 'on'

Shine a flashlight on a spin defect and it wakes up. Electrons get pushed into an excited, higher-energy state. But like a ball that rolls down a hill, the defect soon wants to go back to its calm, low-energy self. It can do this in three ways: flash a tiny photon of light, flip its internal spin, or shake the surrounding atoms like a jitterbug.

The energy path scientists missed

For years, researchers thought the third option — called internal conversion — was a minor player. But a team at the University of Chicago built a new computer model that finally includes all three paths at once. Their surprise? In materials like silicon carbide, internal conversion is actually the main way defects lose energy — far more than anyone had guessed.

This matters because spin defects are being tested as the 'bits' (qubits) in quantum computers and ultra-precise sensors. If engineers build devices thinking energy leaks away slowly, but it actually leaks fast, the qubits might stop working before you finish a calculation.

What comes next?

Now that the missing piece is in the puzzle, scientists can predict how these tiny defects will behave — and design better materials for tomorrow's quantum gadgets. The tool they built is open source, so researchers worldwide can use it to hunt for even more hidden rules inside the crystals that could power the future.

Try this at home

You can see energy loss in action with just your hands. Clap them together sharply once — the heat you feel is sound energy turning into thermal energy. Spin defects do something similar: light energy goes in, but when it comes out it spreads out as tiny vibrations (heat) in the crystal. That spreading is the part scientists just learned to measure correctly.

Fun Fact

A single strand of human hair is about a million times thicker than the distance between atoms in a diamond crystal. The spin defects that quantum computers rely on are even smaller than that tiny gap — each one is just a missing or swapped atom in a sea of billions.
