How one American inventor built a secret underwater attack boat out of wood and iron

How one American inventor built a secret underwater attack boat out of wood and iron

Imagine trying to sneak a tiny wooden boat under a giant warship, drill a hole in its hull, and attach an exploding bomb — all without being seen. That is exactly what David Bushnell tried to do in 1776, using a homemade submarine called the Turtle that looked like a turtle.

It was the world's first combat submarine, built during the American Revolution to fight the most powerful navy on Earth.

The inventor who thought outside the box

David Bushnell was just a student at Yale University in 1775 when he came up with a wild idea: use a submarine to attack British ships without facing them in open battle. The Royal Navy ruled the seas, and fighting it head-on was suicide.

Working with his brother Ezra, local clockmaker Isaac Doolittle, and other Yale mentors, Bushnell built a tiny, acorn-shaped submersible. The Turtle held just one person and was made of oak and iron hoops — like a turtle's shell wrapped in wood.

How the Turtle worked

The pilot climbed inside and filled a chamber with water to dive down (water is heavier than air, so adding it makes things sink). To come back up, they used hand pumps to push the water back out.

For moving, the pilot kicked a pedal-operated front propeller and turned a hand crank. To see in the dark, the compass and gauges were coated in foxfire — a glowing fungus that lit up when warm. (Unfortunately, it only worked above a certain temperature, so winter attacks were out.)

The secret weapon? A timed bomb screwed onto the bottom of the submarine, set to explode "at any proposed length of time under 12 hours." The pilot would steer right up to the enemy ship, drill a hole with a hand-cranked auger, and attach the charge to the hull. Then they had to swim away fast.

The attack on the HMS Eagle

On the night of September 6-7, 1776, the Turtle was transported hidden under a small boat across New York Harbor. Pilot Ezra Lee (who replaced Bushnell's brother after the original pilot got sick) paddled into position under the HMS Eagle, the flagship of the British fleet.

Lee tried to drill into the Eagle's wooden hull — but the copper sheathing that protected British ships was too tough for his hand tool. He could not get a grip. The bomb was never attached, and the Turtle quietly paddled away, empty.

A bold idea that changed everything

Even though the mission failed, the Turtle's design was brilliant. George Washington himself later wrote to Thomas Jefferson in 1785, saying "It is no easy matter to get a person hardy enough to encounter the variety of dangers to which he must be exposed." He then organized the Corps of Sappers and Miners — the country's first combat engineers — inspired in part by Bushnell's underwater war machine.

Today, a full-size model of the Turtle sits in the U.S. Navy Submarine Force Library and Museum in Connecticut. And the Navy even named a submarine tender after Bushnell — the USS Bushnell — proving that one young inventor's wild idea became a lasting legacy.

Try this at home

Build your own "submarine" using a small plastic bottle with a hole in the cap. Put it in a sink or bathtub. Push it down — what happens when you fill the hole? Can you make it rise again? Try using a straw to blow air in or a funnel to let water out. Just like the Turtle, you are controlling how much water is inside to make it sink or float.

Quick recap

1. In 1775, Yale student David Bushnell designed a wooden submarine to fight the British

2. The Turtle was one-person, acorn-shaped, and used water ballast to dive

3. Foxfire fungus lit the inside gauges — but only in warm weather

4. On Sept 6-7, 1776, pilot Ezra Lee tried to drill into HMS Eagle but the copper hull was too tough

5. George Washington praised the idea and built on it with the Corps of Sappers and Miners

Fun fact

The Turtle was so effective in design that the U.S. Navy named an entire ship after David Bushnell 140 years later — the USS Bushnell (AS-2). It helped during World War I and even survived the Pearl Harbor attack before being decommissioned in 1946.
