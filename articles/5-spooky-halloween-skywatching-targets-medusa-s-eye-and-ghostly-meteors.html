5 Spooky Halloween Skywatching Targets: Medusa's Eye and Ghostly Meteors

2026-10-244 min

As Halloween approaches, five spooky but kid-safe skywatching targets light up the October night sky — the winking Demon Star, the ghostly Pleiades, the sea monster Cetus, spiderweb moon craters, and the Halloween fireballs from comet Encke's debris trail.

What spooky Halloween skywatching targets can you see this October?

The Demon Star That Winks

High in the constellation Perseus shines a star that ancient sky-watchers called the "Demon Star." This is Algol — and unlike most stars that shine steadily, Algol winks on purpose!

About every 2 days, 20 hours, and 49 minutes, a hidden companion star slides in front of Algol, blocking some of its light. For about 10 hours, Algol dims dramatically — becoming 3 times fainter for a brief moment. You can even watch this happen with your naked eye if you check on Algol over several nights. It's like the star is winking at you from across the universe!

Algol is part of a three-star system. The total system is about 100 light-years away, and the two main stars are locked in a gravitational dance that astronomers have been studying for centuries.

Try it yourself: On a clear October evening, find the Great Square of Pegasus, then follow the "hourglass" pattern of stars upward to the fainter stars of Perseus. Look for a bright, steady blue-white star. Observe it nightly for a week, and you might catch it dimming!

The Ghostly Pleiades

Look above and slightly to the left of the Demon Star, and you'll find a tight cluster of tiny, twinkling stars called the Pleiades (also known as the Seven Sisters or M45).

To the naked eye, you can usually spot six or seven bright stars, but binoculars reveal dozens more — over a thousand stars in total! These are all sisters, born together about 100 million years ago from the same cosmic cloud of gas and dust.

The Pleiades have been part of Halloween traditions for thousands of years. Ancient Celtic druids timed their harvest festival — Samhain, the precursor to Halloween — to when the Pleiades reached their highest point at midnight. They believed the boundary between the living and the dead was thinnest at this time of year.

Fun fact: The Pleiades are so bright and beautiful that they inspired one of the very first names ever given to a telescopic nebula — Charles Messier catalogued them as "M45" in 1769!

The Sea Monster Cetus

Far to the southeast, near the horizon, glides a constellation shaped like a giant sea creature. This is Cetus (pronounced SEE-tus), the whale or sea monster from Greek mythology.

According to legend, Cetus was a monstrous beast sent to devour the queen of Ethiopia. The hero Perseus saved the day by using the severed head of the Gorgon Medusa — her terrible visage turned the monster to stone!

You can find Cetus by following the chain of stars from the "W" shape of Cassiopeia down toward the brightest patch of the Milky Way. Look for a curved line of faint stars forming the monster's body, with the bright star Deneb Kaitos marking its head.

Spiderweb Moon Craters

On the nights surrounding the full moon on October 26th, turn your gaze to our closest neighbor: the Moon itself. Near the terminator (the line between day and night on the Moon), you'll see something resembling a spider's web.

The star-shaped patterns of bright rays radiate outward from the Tycho crater, one of the Moon's youngest impact sites. It was formed about 108 million years ago when a chunk of space rock slammed into the Moon at tremendous speed. The debris flew outward in long streaks, creating the spiderweb effect.

When the full moon lights up these craters, the sharp shadows make every ray and ridge stand out. It looks as if someone spilled glitter across the Moon's surface!

Halloween Fireballs from Comet Dust

The final spooky target requires patience and luck: the Taurid fireballs, often called "Halloween fireballs."

These aren't regular meteors — they're bright, slow-moving fireballs that streak across the sky like shooting stars on steroids. They happen because Earth is passing through debris left behind by comet Encke, which orbits the Sun every 3.3 years and is only 3 miles (4.8 km) wide.

The Northern and Southern Taurids are active from mid-October through early November. While you can't predict exactly when or where a fireball will appear, the best viewing is after the constellation Taurus rises — usually after sunset. Look toward the southeast, keep your eyes dark-adapted, and just watch. These fireballs are a bonus, not a scheduled show!

Try it yourself: On a clear night after October 20th, dress warmly, lie flat on the ground with a blanket, and gaze toward the east-southeast about an hour after sunset. No equipment needed — just patience!
