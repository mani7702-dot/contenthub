Europe's ExoMars Reaches Mars: The Orbiter That Survived and the Lander That Fell

2026-10-253 min

On October 19, 2016, the European Space Agency's ExoMars mission touched down at the Red Planet. The Trace Gas Orbiter went into orbit, while the Schiaparelli lander's attempt to land ended in a dramatic crash.

1. A Dramatic Arrival at the Red Planet

On October 19, 2016, the European Space Agency's ExoMars spacecraft reached Mars after a seven-month journey from Earth. The mission carried two travelers: a sleek research satellite called the Trace Gas Orbiter, and a small landing craft named Schiaparelli. One would go into orbit and thrive. The other's story ended in a crash — but its sacrifice taught us how to land better next time.

2. The Separating Path

On October 16, three days before arrival, the two halves of ExoMars went their separate ways. The Trace Gas Orbiter fired its engine and settled into Mars orbit, where it began mapping the thin atmosphere for hints of methane, water vapor, and other gases that could signal past or present life.

Schiaparelli, a squat, saucer-shaped lander built in Italy, aimed for the flat plains of Meridiani Planum. Its job was to test new landing technologies — a heat shield, a supersonic parachute, and retro-rockets — that would one day carry a full rover to the Martian surface. But during the final minute of descent, something went wrong.

A spinning sensor that should have told Schiaparelli its orientation had been giving confusing data. The result: the lander thought it was still high above the ground when it was already just metres above the surface. It fired its landing thrusters too early, then cut them while still moving — and slammed into Mars at speed. A crater formed, and Schiaparelli fell silent.

3. The Orbiter That Kept Going

While the world mourned the lost lander, the Trace Gas Orbiter survived. Today — nearly a decade later — it continues to circle Mars, sniffing the atmosphere for methane plumes that could hint at geological or even biological activity. It also serves as a communications relay, passing data from rovers and landers on the surface back to Earth.

Images from NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter later revealed Schiaparelli's crash site — a smudge on the rust-red ground, with its parachute nearby. The European Space Agency called the mission 96% successful, because the orbiter achieved everything it was designed to, and the landing test produced data that improved future Mars missions.

4. Lessons for the Future

Schiaparelli's brief fall was not in vain. The engineers who studied its final minutes learned exactly how to fix the faulty sensor readings. Their work will guide the next ExoMars rover — Rosalind Franklin, scheduled to launch in 2028 — as it searches for signs of ancient life beneath the Martian soil.

The same spirit of learning-from-failure drives space science forward. Every mission, whether it lands gently or not, adds a piece to the puzzle of how we explore other worlds.

Try this at home

You can build a simple model of a landing craft! Cut a small paper circle to be your "lander" and a larger paper cup to be the "heat shield." Drop them from shoulder height and watch how they fall. Try adding a paper parachute or crumpled tissue "retro-rockets" to slow the fall. Ask a grown-up to help you look up NASA's Mars landing videos online — see how real missions use heat shields, parachutes, and sky-cranes to bring spacecraft safely down!

Fun Fact

Schiaparelli is named after Giovanni Schiaparelli, an Italian astronomer who, in 1877, mapped the surface features of Mars and noticed linear markings he called "canali" — which some interpreted as artificial canals. We now know the markings were just an optical illusion, but the name lives on in NASA's future. Meanwhile, the real Schiaparelli lander's crash site was given the formal name "Schiaparelli LM" by planetary scientists who studied its final moments.
