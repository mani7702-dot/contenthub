How Scientists Are Protecting Wheat, Rice, and Barley From a Hidden Fungus

2026-10-263 min

Scientists in the UK and Japan have discovered how to strengthen the natural immune defenses of wheat, rice, and barley — three of the world's most important food crops — so they can fight back against a destructive fungus that threatens global food security.

1. What threat hides in plain sight on our dinner tables?

Deep in farms across Asia, Africa, and beyond, a tiny but powerful fungus is quietly attacking three of the world's most important foods: wheat, rice, and barley. It is called Magnaporthe oryzae — and it causes a disease known as rice blast.

This might sound far away, but rice feeds more than half the world's people. If the blast fungus spreads unchecked, it could threaten the meals on millions of plates. Right now, it destroys enough rice every single year to feed about 60 million people — that is like wiping out the food supply of South Korea!

2. How does the fungus sneak into plants?

The fungus is sneaky. It sends out special molecular disguises — tiny proteins it calls effectors — that slip inside plant cells. Once inside, these effectors trick the plant's own machinery to serve the fungus, letting it take hold and spread.

But plants are not defenseless. They have built-in immune receptors — like tiny smoke detectors — that can spot these foreign invaders. When a receptor finds an effector, it sends an alarm that tells the plant to reinforce its walls and, if needed, shut down the infected cells to protect the rest.

3. How are scientists turning the tables?

A team of researchers from the John Innes Centre in the United Kingdom and Kobe University in Japan has found a promising new tool. They focused on a group of immune proteins called tandem kinase proteins (TKPs) — some of which carry a special tag called an HMA domain.

Using a technique called crystallography (which builds a 3D map of molecules), the scientists watched how HMA domains grab onto the fungus's effectors. They then redesigned the receptors so they could recognize two different fungal threats at once — a skill called "dual specificity" that most natural receptors lack.

This means the plants could be engineered to detect and block multiple forms of the blast fungus, making it much harder for the pathogen to evolve around the defense. The findings appeared in the journal Science Advances.

4. What could this mean for the future?

The research is still in its early stages — tests so far have been done on individual plant cells in labs. The next step is to see whether the engineered defenses work in whole plants, and eventually on real farms.

If it succeeds, farmers may one day grow super-resistant wheat, rice, and barley — crops that can protect themselves against the blast fungus, keeping our food supply safe even as the climate changes and new fungi spread.

Until then, scientists are working hard to unlock the secrets of these tiny defenders in the race to protect the world's biggest meals.

Fun Fact

The blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae is so well-studied that it is often called the "lab rat of plant pathology" — scientists have been researching it for over a century because understanding how it infects rice teaches us about thousands of other plant diseases too!
