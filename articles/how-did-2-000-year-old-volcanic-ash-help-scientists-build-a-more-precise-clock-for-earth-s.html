How did 2,000-year-old volcanic ash help scientists build a more precise clock for Earth's history?

2026-10-314 min

Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD and buried Pompeii. That same 2,000-year-old catastrophe is now serving as a calibration point for argon-argon dating, the clock geologists use to time volcanoes, extinctions and asteroid impacts — making it up to twice as precise.

How did 2,000-year-old volcanic ash help scientists build a more precise clock for Earth's history?

1. Why this eruption is special

When Mount Vesuvius erupted in the year 79 AD, it buried the Roman towns of Pompeii, Herculaneum and Stabiae under meters of ash and pumice. That disaster is famous as a frozen moment in human history — but to scientists who study deep time, its real value is that it happened within living memory.

Pliny the Younger wrote an eyewitness letter describing the eruption, and historians later pinned it to August 24 of that year. That unusually well-known date makes Vesuvius the perfect checkpoint: a volcanic event whose true age is known independently, so it can be used to test and refine the methods geologists rely on for everything from dating dinosaur-extinction asteroids to timing ancient climate shifts.

2. The clock inside volcanic rock

Geologists date ancient eruptions with a technique called argon-argon dating. It works on a simple principle: a mineral called potassium-40 slowly turns into argon-40 at a steady, measurable rate, and argon is a gas that only gets trapped once the rock cools. By measuring the ratio of argon-40 to argon-39 (a lab-made helper isotope), scientists can calculate how long ago the rock solidified.

But like any clock, it only works if you know how fast the hands move — in this case, the half-life of the potassium-to-argon decay. Until now, that number carried enough uncertainty to blur events that happened only thousands or tens of thousands of years apart, exactly the scale where scientists try to link, say, an asteroid impact to a mass extinction.

3. Rewinding 2,000 years of ash

A team led by Paul Renne at the Berkeley Geochronology Center, working with UC Berkeley and the University of Padua, used sanidine — a potassium-rich crystal — taken from pumice of the 79 AD eruption. By dating samples whose true age is constrained by history, they sharpened the potassium-40 half-life to 12.044 billion years, give or take 88 million.

That refinement brought the method's precision to 0.7% — matching a target the same group predicted was possible back in 1997. When they ran the calibrated technique on the Vesuvius samples, they dated the eruption to 1,938 years before the minerals were analyzed in 2025, which lines up cleanly with the historically accepted age once the calendar offset is applied.

4. What this fixes for the future

With a more precise clock, geologists can finally tell apart events that sit only thousands of years apart in the rock record. That matters when testing whether an asteroid impact, a burst of volcanic activity, or a climate shift really did cause a mass extinction — or whether they simply happened to land around the same time. The advance also strengthens comparisons across different dating systems, including radiocarbon methods used by archaeologists, and should help forecast timelines for active volcanoes near cities like Naples, Mexico City and Yogyakarta.

Fun Fact

About a decade ago, Renne's group used argon-argon dating to pin the dinosaur-extinction asteroid impact, massive Indian volcanic eruptions and the end-Cretaceous extinction to within just a few tens of thousands of years of one another — strengthening the case that the two events reinforced each other. This new calibration makes those fine-grained connections far easier to spot.
