How Did a Road Crew in Brazil Uncover a 20-Meter Dinosaur?

2026-10-313 min

Workers laying a rail terminal in northeastern Brazil dug up bones 8 meters down — and palaeontologists soon realized they belonged to a brand-new species of giant dinosaur, one with surprising family ties to Europe.

Sometimes the biggest discoveries begin with a construction crew simply digging a hole. In the northeastern Brazilian state of Maranhão, workers building a road-and-rail terminal at Davinópolis uncovered something far older than any road: the fossil bones of a giant dinosaur that is new to science.

A giant hiding underground

The animal has been named Dasosaurus tocantinensis. Its name blends "Daso," a word for the region's dense forests, with a nod to the nearby Tocantins River.

The fossils sat about 8 meters (26 feet) below the surface. Archaeologists monitoring the building site first spotted them and thought they might belong to a large prehistoric mammal. But when palaeontologist Elver Mayer took a closer look, the depth told him a different story — these rocks were around 120 million years old, laid down during the Early Cretaceous Period.

The team measured a massive 1.5-meter (59-inch) thigh bone, or femur. From that single clue, they estimated the creature stretched about 20 meters long — roughly the length of two city buses. Tail vertebrae, ribs, and arm and leg bones made it a relatively complete specimen, with more fossils possibly still buried at the site.

A family reunion across an ocean

The most surprising part is who Dasosaurus is related to. Its closest known relative is a dinosaur called Garumbatitan morellensis, found in what is now Spain. That means the two share a lineage that once spread across lands now separated by the Atlantic Ocean.

Researchers think the ancestors of Dasosaurus travelled into South America by way of North Africa around 130 million years ago. Back then, these continents were still joined together inside the ancient supercontinent Gondwana, so the journey was a walk, not a swim.

What the bones reveal

Scientists also studied the dinosaur's bone tissue under a microscope. The growth patterns mix features seen in older sauropods with traits found in titanosaurs, the long-necked plant-eaters that later became some of the largest animals ever to walk the Earth. That mix hints that the secret to growing truly enormous may have appeared earlier in dinosaur history than anyone thought.

The specimen now lives at a natural-history research centre in São Luís, the state capital.

Fun fact

In Brazil, many fossil sites are found not in remote wilderness but along highways and quarries. Dense tropical vegetation hides the rocks, so palaeontologists often rely on road crews and builders to expose them — making every new construction project a potential window into the deep past.
