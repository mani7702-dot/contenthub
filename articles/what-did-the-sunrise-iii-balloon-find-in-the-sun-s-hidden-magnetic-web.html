What did the Sunrise-III balloon find in the Sun's hidden magnetic web?

2026-10-244 min

A balloon-borne telescope called Sunrise-III floated 35 km above Earth in 2024 and revealed that even the Sun's 'quiet' regions are laced with a surprisingly intricate web of magnetic threads — not the smooth, simple canopy scientists expected.

What did the Sunrise-III balloon find in the Sun's hidden magnetic web?

When we think of the Sun, we picture sunspots and explosive solar flares. But most of our star's surface is actually "quiet" — no sunspots, no eruptions, just a peaceful disk of boiling gas. That quiet cover makes up the vast majority of the Sun. So how magnetic is it really?

A telescope above the atmosphere

Ground-based telescopes have a problem: Earth's air blurs and wobbles starlight like a stone tossed in a pond. To see the Sun's finest details, scientists launched the Sunrise-III observatory — a 1-meter telescope carried by a high-altitude balloon that floated 35 kilometers (22 miles) above Earth in 2024. That's where the atmosphere thin out, giving the telescope a crystal-clear view.

The gondola carried three instruments built by different countries. The key one was SCIP — the Sunrise Chromospheric Infrared spectroPolarimeter. SCIP can measure magnetic fields continuously from the Sun's visible surface (photosphere) all the way up through the chromosphere, a pinkish layer above the surface.

The magnetic canopy is not so smooth

Scientists expected the quiet regions to have a simple, smooth magnetic canopy — like an umbrella arching up from the surface. But Sunrise-III saw something far more intricate.

The canopy is full of thin, thread-like magnetic structures, woven together like a fishing net. Rather than one smooth arch, the magnetic fields spread outward and upward in many narrow strands. Computer simulations showed that swirling motions on the Sun's surface twist these field lines, creating the surprising patterns.

In separate observations of a quiet region near the Sun's edge, Sunrise-III also mapped magnetic fields inside spicules — narrow jets of solar material shooting upward from the surface. These readings reveal how the magnetic field changes strength and direction with height.

Why this matters

The Sun's quiet regions cover about half the solar surface and dominate its total magnetic output. Understanding their magnetic structure helps explain how energy generated by the Sun's churning surface travels upward and heats the corona — the Sun's outer atmosphere, which blazes at over a million degrees despite being farther from the heat source.

The Sunrise-III team found that even the Sun's calmest neighborhoods are surprisingly magnetic, with far more structure than textbook diagrams suggest.

Try this at home

Hold a magnet under a piece of paper and sprinkle iron filings on top. Watch how the filings form invisible field lines into loops and spirals. That's a tiny taste of how the Sun's magnetic field looks — if it had threads instead of just lines!

Fun Fact

The balloon carrying Sunrise-III floated at an altitude of 35 km — that's 10 times higher than a commercial jet flies. It stayed aloft long enough to capture some of the sharpest magnetic measurements of the Sun ever taken from near-space.
