How Did Scientists Discover a Pac-Man Enzyme That Gobbles Plastic?

2026-10-263 min

Scientists have found a special enzyme — nicknamed the 'Pac-Man enzyme' — that bacteria use to chew through plastic. The discovery shows how nature is already fighting pollution, one tiny bite at a time.

1. The Plastic That Won't Go Away

Every year, humans make over 350 million tons of plastic. Most of it is designed to last forever — and that is literally the problem. Conventional plastics like the plastic in water bottles and shopping bags can take hundreds of years to break down. Instead of disappearing, they slowly crumble into tiny pieces called microplastics that end up in our oceans, our soil, and even our bodies.

But nature is fighting back. Deep in forest soil, in the leaf litter and decaying wood, there are tiny workers — bacteria — that have been practicing a very useful skill: eating plastic.

2. The Pac-Man Enzyme

A team of scientists at the University of Konstanz in Germany wanted to understand how certain microbes can digest biodegradable plastics. They buried thin films of a special bioplastic called LCAO (long-chain aliphatic polyester) in the forest soil of their university garden, then waited.

After about a year, they dug up the samples. The biodegradable plastic films were almost completely gone — broken down by the bacteria living in the soil. But when they tested conventional plastic (like the plastic in grocery bags), nothing happened. It was as tough as ever.

Under an electron microscope, the scientists saw something amazing: the biodegradable plastic was riddled with tiny holes — each one the exact size and shape of a single bacterial cell. It looked as if the bacteria had burrowed straight into the material.

3. What Makes the Enzyme So Special

The bacteria do their work using a special tool: an enzyme they call the "Pac-Man enzyme". The name comes from its shape — it has an unusually wide opening, like Pac-Man's mouth, that grabs and breaks apart plastic molecules.

But there is a twist. This enzyme looks a lot like another kind of enzyme that bacteria use to fight antibiotics like penicillin. That means the same family of tools that helps bacteria survive our medicines may also be helping some of them learn how to digest plastic. It is a powerful reminder that evolution is always at work — finding new ways to solve new problems.

4. Good News and Big Limits

The good news is clear: bacteria can break down certain biodegradable plastics in about nine to eleven months. Cellulose — a natural material found in plants — breaks down even faster, in just two or three months.

But here is the catch: the enzyme only works on biodegradable plastics, not on the billions of tons of conventional plastic already polluting our planet. The researchers say the real solution is to make more products from bioplastics that contain the right chemical bonds — the ones that nature already knows how to unlock.

Try this at home

Start a mini compost bin! Fill a clear jar with layers of damp soil, leaves, and small strips of paper. Check on it weekly — you will see the paper getting smaller as tiny organisms break it down. Talk about what happens to things that are "made to be broken" vs. things that stick around forever.

Fun Fact

Some seabirds and tiny water fleas have been found with microplastics in their stomachs — scientists have even discovered plastic in the deepest parts of the ocean, farther down than sunlight can reach. But now we know that nature's toolkit already includes the beginnings of a solution: enzymes that can learn to eat what we throw away.
