Can the Human Heart Really Handle the Trip to Mars? A New Study Says Yes

2026-10-313 min

Thirteen astronauts spent six months in space, and researchers found their hearts adapted well enough to handle the weaker gravity of Mars. Regular exercise, it turns out, may be the secret.

Can the Human Heart Really Handle the Trip to Mars? A New Study Says Yes

For years, one of the biggest worries about sending people to Mars wasn't the rocket or the fuel — it was the heart. Could a human heart survive months of floating in space and then handle a planet with only one-third of Earth's gravity? A new study offers a surprisingly cheerful answer: yes, especially if you keep exercising.

A Small Crew and a Long Journey

Researchers from Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston tracked 13 astronauts — nine men and four women — who each spent about six months aboard the International Space Station. Using special ultrasound equipment, the astronauts scanned their own hearts up to five times during their missions, watching how the organ changed as it floated in weightlessness.

On Earth, your heart works hard all day, pushing blood upward against gravity. In orbit, that job gets much easier. Blood drifts toward the head, and the heart can quietly shrink because it simply has less to do. That sounds harmless, but a smaller heart can struggle later — causing dizziness, low stamina, or fainting when a person stands up again.

What Happened Back on Solid Ground

When the astronauts returned to Earth, the team tested their hearts under simulated Martian gravity. The result was encouraging: there were no major signs of cardiovascular strain. Instead of being worn out, the hearts had adapted — and the researchers think daily exercise deserves much of the credit.

As part of NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program, the crew worked out for up to two hours a day. That included 30 to 45 minutes of endurance training for the heart, plus 60 to 75 minutes of resistance training to protect muscle. "With our findings, there is now reason to think that we would be able to traverse to Mars, get there and actually work on the surface of Mars," said senior author Dr. James Thomas of Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine.

Why This Matters

This is the first study to look at how the heart responds to simulated Martian gravity after a long stay in space. Mars missions would take months or years — far longer than a typical space station stay — so scientists still need to confirm the heart stays strong over much longer trips. But the early picture is brighter than many expected.

Fun Fact

The medical term for that dizzy, see-stars feeling when you stand up too quickly is orthostatic hypotension — the same thing gravity does to your blood. Astronauts exercise hard so it won't spoil their very first steps on a new world.
