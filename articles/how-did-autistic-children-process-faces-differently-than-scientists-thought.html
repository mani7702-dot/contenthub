How Did Autistic Children Process Faces Differently Than Scientists Thought?

2026-10-293 min

A new Yale study using brain scans from 128 sensors reveals that autistic children's brains respond to faces in a different way than previously understood — and those differences start earlier than scientists had realized.

time: 08:28 IST

How Did Autistic Children Process Faces Differently Than Scientists Thought?

1. Reading faces the way brains do it

When you meet someone new, your brain fires off a lightning-fast pattern of electrical signals so you can recognize a friend, guess whether they're smiling, or tell if they're surprised. For many autistic people, that same pattern unfolds a little differently — and now, a sweeping new study is showing exactly how.

A team at Yale School of Medicine studied the brain activity of about 400 children using a device called electroencephalography, or EEG. That machine records tiny electrical pulses from the scalp — but instead of placing just a handful of sensors, the researchers spread 128 electrodes all across each child's head. This gave them a full, city-wide map of what was happening inside.

2. Two paths that grow apart with age

The children looked at photos of faces and everyday objects while the 128 sensors recorded their brain's response. Then the team used machine learning — a type of computer pattern-finding — to try to guess what each child was looking at based only on the brain signals.

For neurotypical children, the computer got better at this guess as the years passed. Their face-processing signals became sharper, more distinct, like a radio dial that slowly tunes in to a clear station. But for autistic children, that sharpening did not happen. Their brain signals stayed blurrier, making it harder for the computer to tell whether they were looking at a face or an object.

"This is the first time we've leveraged the full volume of very rich information provided by an EEG recording to understand face perception and autism," said James McPartland, the Yale professor who led the study.

3. The differences start earlier

Scientists had long focused on one particular brain wave called the N170 — a spike of activity that usually appears about 170 milliseconds after someone sees a face. In autistic individuals, this spike tends to arrive later. But by scanning the brain's activity over a wider window of time, the Yale team found that the differences begin even before that 170-millisecond moment — at some of the very earliest stages of how the eyes send pictures to the brain.

That's an important clue. It suggests the differences aren't just about one delayed signal; they're baked into how the whole system develops.

4. What this could mean

Because the differences appear during development — not just from life experience — the findings hint that autism-related patterns in face processing are linked to how brains grow from childhood, not just to the years a person accumulates. That in turn could one day help researchers look for earlier, more objective ways to understand autism — and to find supports that fit each person's unique brain wiring.

The study was published in Nature Mental Health.

Fun Fact

Every human face you recognize — from your mom's smile to a stranger's wave — involves a cascade of electrical signals that travel from your eyes to your brain in less than a fifth of a second. The word "N170" comes from the fact that this particular brain spike shows up about 170 milliseconds after a face appears. Some scientists think this system evolved so we could quickly spot friends in a crowd — or tell a friendly face from a threatening one.
