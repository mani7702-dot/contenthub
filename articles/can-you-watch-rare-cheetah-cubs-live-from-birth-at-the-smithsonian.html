Can You Watch Rare Cheetah Cubs Live From Birth at the Smithsonian?

2026-10-293 min

A live webcam at the Smithsonian Conservation Institute in Virginia lets anyone watch Zura the cheetah care for her five newborn cubs around the clock — a rare glimpse into the lives of one of the world's most endangered big cats.

Can You Watch Rare Cheetah Cubs Live From Birth at the Smithsonian?

On October 5th, a cheetah named Zura gave birth to five tiny cubs at the Smithsonian's Conservation Institute in Virginia — and thanks to a live webcam, you can watch every adorable moment, day or night.

What Makes These Cubs So Special?

Cheetahs are the fastest land mammals on Earth, but they are also one of the most vulnerable. Today, only about 7,000 cheetahs remain in the wild across Africa. Each cub born in a conservation breeding program is a hopeful sign for the species' future.

Unlike zoo habitats where cubs might have frequent human contact, these cubs were born at the Institute's off-campus research facility in Front Royal, Virginia. There, the keepers can observe the family remotely through the cam without disturbing their natural rhythms. The cubs' mama — Zura — gets space to nurse, groom, and teach her babies how to be cheetahs, while researchers quietly gather data that will help protect cheetahs in the wild.

The Sounds of a Cheetah Nursery

Press play on the livestream and you might hear soft chirps, gentle purrs, and even tiny meows. Cheetahs don't roar like lions — instead, they chirp. Scientists believe these high-pitched calls are how cheetah mothers communicate with their cubs in the tall grass, calling them to follow during a hunt.

The cam captures everything: nursing sessions, the cubs' first wobbly steps, and those heart-melting moments when Zura curls around her babies like a living blanket. The faint sounds of contented purring are a good sign — it means the cubs are warm, fed, and feeling safe.

Why the Cam Matters

This isn't just about cute animals — it's about survival. Cheetahs face a double threat. In the wild, their numbers have dropped because of habitat loss and human-wildlife conflict. In captivity, scientists are working to keep genetic diversity strong so that future generations can someday return to the African savanna.

The Smithsonian team has been monitoring Zura and her cubs closely through the cam, checking that all five are eating well and growing steadily. Viewers can also donate through the livestream page to support ongoing cheetah conservation work.

Fun Fact

A cheetah cub's smoky-gray coat isn't just adorable — it's camouflage! The distinctive "mantle" of long fur along their back helps them blend into the dry grass of the African savanna, hiding them from predators until they grow strong enough to run at 70 miles per hour. Cheetah cubs stay with their mother for about 18 months, learning how to hunt by practicing pounces on sticks and leaves.
