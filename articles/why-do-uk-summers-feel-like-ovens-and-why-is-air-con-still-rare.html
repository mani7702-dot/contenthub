Why do UK summers feel like ovens, and why is air-con still rare?

2026-10-224 min

Older UK homes were built to hold heat in, not push it out. During heatwaves the solid brick walls act like thermal batteries, storing the day's heat and radiating it back all night — turning a sunny afternoon into a sleepless, sweaty ordeal and pushing cities several degrees hotter than the countryside.

Imagine your house is a giant sponge soaking up the sun's heat all day long. That is exactly what is happening across the UK as summer temperatures climb higher and higher.

The thermal battery effect

Most homes in the UK were built decades — even centuries — ago, when the goal was to keep the cold out, not the heat in. The secret is in the walls: solid brick. These thick walls are excellent at soaking up heat, kind of like how a hot water bottle stays warm for hours after you fill it. During a heatwave, the sun beats down on those brick walls all afternoon, and the buildings drink in the warmth like a thirsty plant.

But here is the catch: brick is just as good at holding onto that heat as it is at soaking it up. By evening, when the sun goes down, the walls are still glowing hot — radiating warmth back into every room. It is like living inside a radiator that never switches off.

Cities bake hotter

This problem gets even worse in cities, thanks to something scientists call the urban heat island effect. In towns and cities, there is far less grass and trees than in the countryside. Instead, there are roads, rooftops, and pavements made of dark materials that absorb even more heat. Add to that the way tall buildings create "urban canyons" that trap sunlight and block winds, and the whole city can end up several degrees hotter than the surrounding fields and forests.

Night-time danger

This is not just uncomfortable — it can be dangerous. The urban heat island effect is strongest at night, when our bodies really need cool air to rest and recover. When night-time temperatures stay high, people who are elderly or have breathing problems can struggle, reaching for inhalers or fans just to get through the night.

Cooling down

So what can we do? Planting more trees and green roofs, painting roofs white to reflect sunlight, and designing buildings with better ventilation all help. And slowly, more UK homes are getting air-conditioning — something that was almost unheard of just a few years ago.

But the simplest trick is free: on a hot day, close your curtains during the afternoon to keep the sun's rays out, then open your windows at night when the air outside finally cools down. Let the house breathe, and let the walls slowly give back their stored warmth to the cooler night air.

Try it yourself: Build your own mini heat island! Take two jars, fill them with equal water, and wrap one with a dark rubber band while you leave the other plain. Put both in the sun for an hour and feel the difference. Or spend an evening noticing how the brick walls and sidewalks around your neighbourhood still feel warm long after the sun sets — and how a park or a street tree feels noticeably cooler.

Fun Fact: The urban heat island effect doesn't just make cities hotter — it also makes the air dirtier. Extra heat speeds up the chemical reactions that create ground-level ozone (smog), which is why big cities often have worse air quality on the hottest days of summer.
