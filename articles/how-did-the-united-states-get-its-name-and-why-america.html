How did the United States get its name? And why 'America'?

2026-10-224 min

Before 'United States of America,' there was 'United Colonies,' 'Columbia,' and even 'United States of the Earth.' Here is how the name that fit on a map became the name on every passport.

How did the United States get its name? And why "America"?

What is a country called before anyone has decided what to call it? For the thirteen colonies that became the United States, the answer was: a lot of different things — and one of them sounds like it came from a fantasy novel.

The original name on everyone's lips

In the early 1770s, nobody said "United States of America." The colonies as a group were usually called the United Colonies — that is what King George's governors had named them back in 1687, and the habit stuck. On July 4, 1776 the Continental Congress declared the colonies "free and independent states," but even then the big question was: what do we call the whole country?

Representative Richard Henry Lee put it plainly in a June 7, 1776 resolution: these "United Colonies are, and of right, ought to be, free and independent." Congress approved the resolution, but the name of the new nation was still up for debate.

The name that won in a letter

The very first known use of the phrase "United States of America" did not come from a grand ceremony or a law. It came from a letter.

On January 2, 1776, cavalry officer Stephen Moylan wrote to Joseph Reed, George Washington's aide-de-camp, asking for a commission in Reed's unit "with the view of my attaching myself to the fortitude and enterprise of the United States of America." Moylan was writing in his personal capacity — the formal name had not been adopted yet — but his wording was picked up and passed around.

When the Articles of Confederation were being drafted in Philadelphia, a committee led by John Dickinson settled the question. Their June 1776 draft declared: "The name of this confederation shall be the United States of America." The Articles were sent to the states in 1777 and finally ratified in 1781.

The names that almost were

While Congress was deciding, several alternatives floated around:

  • "United States of North America" — used in the 1778 Treaty of Alliance with France, because no one yet knew how far west the country would reach.
  • "Columbia" — a poetic name that had already appeared in songs and poems (think "Hail, Columbia"), borrowing from Christopher Columbus. It was popular enough that the capital was briefly going to be called the District of Columbia.
  • "United States of the Earth" — a congressman seriously proposed this in 1810, but it did not catch.
  • "The United States" — still used today when formality is not needed.

Some states wanted to keep their old identities: John Adams worried the name "United States" sounded too grand, and others proposed "The United States of the British Colonies" or "The United States of the New World."

Why "America" stuck

The word "America" was already over 250 years old when the country picked it. In 1507, German cartographer Martin Waldseemüller printed a world map and labeled the newly discovered land "America" — in honor of Amerigo Vespucci, the Italian explorer who first suggested the lands Columbus reached were a new continent, not just a shortcut to Asia.

For a long time, "America" was used mainly on maps. As the historian Daniel Immerwahr has shown, American presidents barely used the word in speeches until the 1890s. Before that, they said "the United States" or "the American Union." The name officially covers the whole country and most of the continents — even though most other countries on the Americas call themselves "American" too.

A name changes, the questions stay the same

Today, the full name on passports is still "United States of America." But it took a letter, a draft, a poem, and a 1507 map all to land on a single name — and some people still argue whether it should be "America" or "the United States," just like they did 250 years ago.

Try this at home

Ask an adult to show you a map. Trace the difference between North America, South America, and the United States with different colored pens. Then look up: what do Spanish speakers call the United States? (Hint: "Estados Unidos.") Try saying "United States of America" out loud — it is the only country name that literally says "united states."

Fun Fact

The word "America" is printed on the oldest surviving map of the whole New World: Martin Waldseemüller's 1507 world chart, kept in the Library of Congress. It is the only known map Waldseemüller ever made, and he based the name on Amerigo Vespucci's letter describing his voyages. The Smithsonian calls it the single most important map in American history — because it gave the entire continent its name.
