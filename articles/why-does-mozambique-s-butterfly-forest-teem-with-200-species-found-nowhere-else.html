Why does Mozambique's 'butterfly forest' teem with 200 species found nowhere else?

2026-10-212 min

A government decree set aside 23,000 acres around Mount Mabu as a community conservation area, protecting one of southern Africa's wettest forests and its hundreds of butterflies that exist nowhere else on Earth.

On a misty mountain in northern Mozambique, a forest earned a magical nickname: the butterfly forest.

It isn't just pretty words. Stand on the slopes of Mount Mabu and you'll watch hundreds of colorful wings flit between the trees — butterflies so special that many exist nowhere else on Earth.

A forest full of unique neighbors

Mount Mabu sits in one of the wettest corners of southern Africa, where clouds drop soft rain almost daily. That makes it a perfect home for life that has nowhere else to go.

Scientists who have trekked these slopes over the past twenty years discovered the forest is packed with species found only here, including:

  • A bush viper named Atheris mabuensis — no, really, scientists named it after this very mountain.
  • A tiny chameleon called Rhampholeon maspictus, no bigger than your thumb.
  • Over 200 kinds of butterflies, each one a living paintbrush of orange, blue, and black.

Because the mountain is somewhat cut off from the wider world, life here evolved in its own quiet corner — and that means surprises keep turning up.

How Sir David gave it a name

In 2013, Sir David Attenborough visited with the BBC and walked among the swirling wings. He looked up and called it the "butterfly forest", and the name stuck like a favorite song.

The forest is also a sacred place for local communities, who have long protected its trees even before the government wrote it into law.

Now it is locked in protection

On August 24th, Mozambique's Ministry of Agriculture, Environment, and Fisheries signed a decree setting aside 23,000 acres around the mountain as the Mount Mabu Community Conservation Area.

That is an area bigger than 17,000 football fields — all kept safe for the butterflies, the vipers, the chameleons, and the birds that call it home.

The plan says the land is "dedicated to the protection, conservation and sustainable use of natural resources by local communities" — so people and wildlife both get to thrive together.

Try this at home

Make your own butterfly corner! Plant a small patch of flowers that bloom in different colors (try marigolds, zinnias, or native wildflowers if you can find them). Sit quietly and watch which insects visit — and remember, real butterflies taste with their feet, so a garden that smells and feels right brings them back every year.

Fun fact

The name "Mabu" comes from a local language, and the mountain was virtually unknown to science until 2005, when a team of researchers from Mulanje Mountain became the first to properly visit and measure it. Before that, the "butterfly forest" existed in stories — not in science books.
