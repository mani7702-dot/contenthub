How 30-Year-Old Tree Thinning Surprised Scientists and Gave Forests a Carbon Boost

2026-10-273 min

>-

The experiment that nobody remembered

In the mid-1980s, forest scientists in Victoria, Australia launched a big experiment to settle a question that had been argued for decades: What is the best way to look after a forest?

Do you protect it completely, letting every tree grow wild? Or do you carefully remove some trees so the others have more room, light, and food?

The scientists set up their experiment in two places — Tanjil Bren in Victoria's Central Highlands and Cabbage Tree Creek in East Gippsland. Between 1987 and 1989, they tried out different ways of managing the forest. Some areas were clear-cut (where all the trees in a patch are removed), some had small patches cut, and some were thinned — meaning about half the trees were carefully taken out, leaving the strongest behind.

Then something interesting happened: the experiment was finished, the results were published, the signs were taken down, the roads grew over with ferns, and everyone forgot about it.

But the forest kept growing.

The surprise after 30 years

Thirty years later, in 2014, a group of University of Melbourne scientists walked back to the old Tanjil Bren site. What they found made their jaws drop.

In the thinned areas — where about half the trees had been removed decades earlier — the trees that were left behind had grown enormous. These trees were only about 80 years old, but the biggest ones were over a meter thick at chest height, and some were even bigger — 1.5 meters across!

The thinned trees were 20% bigger around than the trees growing in patches that had been left completely untouched.

And here is the best part: the thinned forests had stored just as much carbon in their wood as the untouched forests. That means 30 years of careful thinning did not lose any carbon — it let the remaining trees grow so big that they caught up to, and matched, the carbon stored by forests that were never touched.

A helping hand for an endangered possum

The mountain ash forests of Victoria are home to one of the world's most vulnerable creatures: the Leadbeater's possum. This tiny, furry marsupial (about as big as your hand) is officially listed as critically endangered.

Leadbeater's possums need something very special: hollow trees where they can sleep safely during the day. These hollows only form in big, old, mature trees. They also need a dense mid-story of acacia trees to climb through and find food.

Here is where the thinning experiment paid off. Camera traps placed across the different forest sections found Leadbeater's possums in every single thinned area — but in none of the untouched areas. The acacia trees that grew back after thinning created exactly the kind of dense, protective mid-story that these possums need to survive.

The big lesson for growing forests

The forgotten experiment taught us three important things:

1. Long-term experiments matter. Some of the most important discoveries only show themselves after decades. If we had not revisited Tanjil Bren, we would never have learned what thinning could really do.

2. Thinning is not a trade-off. Removing some trees carefully can give you wood for people to use, store more carbon in the trees that remain, and create better homes for wildlife. It is a win-win-win situation that breaks the false idea that forests are either "saved" or "used."

3. There is no magic fix. Different forests are different, and no single method works everywhere. What worked for mountain ash in Victoria might be totally different for pine forests in Sweden or rainforests in the Amazon.

So the next time you walk through a forest, remember: sometimes the smartest thing a forest manager can do is leave fewer — but better — trees behind.

Try this at home

Plant a native tree or shrub in your neighborhood! Even a small acacia or eucalyptus in a pot on a balcony can provide food and shelter for local birds and insects. You can also visit your nearest forest or park with a grown-up and look for hollow trees — those are homes for possums, owls, and woodpeckers!

Fun Fact

The mountain ash trees in Victoria's Central Highlands are the tallest flowering plants on Earth — some reach over 100 meters (330 feet) tall, taller than a 30-story building. Leadbeater's possums use these giants like a skyscraper apartment, leaping from branch to branch dozens of meters up in the air!

For grown-ups

The 2014 rediscovery of the mid-1980s Silvicultural Systems Project (SSP) at Tanjil Bren revealed that 30-year-old thinning of mountain ash (Eucalyptus regnans) forests produced trees 20% larger in diameter than unharvested controls, with equivalent or greater carbon storage in wood, and supported Leadbeater's possum (Gymnobelideus leadbeateri) occupancy in every thinned plot (none in controls). The study (DOI: 10.1007/s44391-026-00076-6) concludes that selective thinning delivers a "win-win-win": timber production, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity conservation — reframing the conservation-versus-production false dichotomy for wet sclerophyll forests. Leadbeater's possum has declined from an estimated 7,500 in the early 1980s to ~1,500–2,000 today, with clearfell logging and Black Saturday bushfires (2009, which destroyed 43% of habitat) as primary threats.
