How did sharks learn to eavesdrop on the ocean? Their hearing reaches 250 feet underwater

2026-10-233 min

Scientists discovered that blacktip sharks can detect low-frequency sounds from up to 250 feet away — and figure out where the sound is coming from.

How did sharks learn to eavesdrop on the ocean? Their hearing reaches 250 feet underwater

The ocean is never silent. Far below the surface, sounds travel for miles — the crack of a whale call, the splash of a fish jumping, the rumble of a boat engine. For sharks, all of that noise is a conversation. And now scientists have measured exactly how far those conversations can reach.

1. How far can a shark hear?

Sharks do not have ears like we do. Instead, they depend on their inner ear, a set of tiny sensors that pick up motion and vibrations in the water. That makes them excellent listeners in their own special way.

In a new study off the coast of Florida, researchers played low-frequency sounds — between 100 and 800 Hertz — through an underwater speaker and watched what nearby blacktip sharks would do. A drone hovered 40 to 50 feet above the water, filming each reaction. Calibrated microphones in the water measured exactly how loud the sound was at every spot.

The result: sharks reacted from up to 243 feet (about 74 meters) away. That is roughly two-thirds of a swimming pool length — and it is much farther than scientists had measured before in freely swimming sharks.

2. Can they tell where it is coming from?

Yes — and that is the surprising part. When the sharks heard the sound, they did not just twitch. They made sharp turns and swam away from the speaker. That shows they were not simply startled by a noise; they could tell where the sound was coming from.

More than 70% of all responses happened in what scientists call the acoustic far field — the zone where sound behaves differently than it does right next to the source. Detecting direction from that distance is a skill that had never been proven in wild sharks before.

Why does turning away matter? In the ocean, low-frequency sounds often come from danger — a predator, a boat, or a storm. A shark that can hear from far away and figure out the direction is one step better at staying safe.

3. Listening without a swim bladder

Most bony fish have a gas-filled organ called a swim bladder that helps them sense sound pressure. Sharks do not have one at all.

Instead, sharks depend on their inner ear, which includes a structure called the macula neglecta. This sensor picks up the tiny movements of water particles created as sound waves pass through the sea — letting the shark "feel" the direction the sound traveled. The study's authors believe this is what lets them hear so well from so far away.

Try this at home

Make a mini ocean-sound test: Fill a large bowl or bathtub with water. Drop a single drop of food coloring near the edge and tap the surface gently with a spoon at one side. Watch how the waves and ripples spread outward. That is the same way sound moves through water — in all directions — until a shark's inner ear picks up the motion.

Sound direction game: Sit with a friend in a quiet room. Have them whisper your name from somewhere in the room. Can you tell which direction it came from? You use your outer ear the way a shark uses its inner ear — both are tuned to find where sound begins.

Fun Fact

A single low-frequency sound can travel hundreds of miles through the ocean. That means a shark hearing a distant call may be reacting to something that happened far beyond the horizon — the ocean's own internet of invisible messages.
