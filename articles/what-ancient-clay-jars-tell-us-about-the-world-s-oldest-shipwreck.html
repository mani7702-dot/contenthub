What Ancient Clay Jars Tell Us About the World's Oldest Shipwreck

2026-10-304 min

Off the Turkish coast near Kekova, divers have pulled pottery from a ship that sank around 4,500 years ago, making it the oldest known shipwreck and a time capsule of long-vanished Mediterranean trade.

What Ancient Clay Jars Tell Us About the World's Oldest Shipwreck?

1. A Timeless Cargo Washes Ashore

Imagine a wooden ship setting off from the rocky coast of what is now southern Turkey, its sail full of wind, its hold packed with the kind of cargo that connected the ancient world. That voyage never reached home. The ship is gone — only a memory in the waves — but its clay jars still rest on the seabed, and each one is a tiny time machine.

Archaeologists now believe a shipwreck off the coast near the village of Kekova is the oldest one ever found. Working from clues left behind in the pottery, researchers tentatively date the sinking to around 2700–2400 BCE, making it roughly 4,500 years old. That makes it older than the legendary pyramids of Giza and older than the famed shipwreck at Dokos, which previously held the record at about 4,200 years.

2. Reading the Clues in Broken Pottery

The secret to the wreck's age is written not in stone but in clay. The cargo included large, ovoid storage jars — amphorae — each with two handles and a raised horizontal band of clay around its middle. To the untrained eye they are just broken pots, but to archaeologists they are fingerprints of the past.

Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University, Antalya, has combed this coastline for ancient wrecks for decades. Acting on tips from local seafarers, his team located the site in shallow waters off Kekova in 2024. Over the course of several months they carefully pried the pottery free from barnacles that had clung to the rocky slope, cataloging each sherd and fragment.

3. Footprints of an Early Trade Route

The pottery tells a story bigger than one lost ship. The jars once held goods that moved between regions of the eastern Mediterranean during the Early Bronze Age. That era coincides with the age of the great pyramids — a time when long-distance sea trade was just beginning to stitch distant communities together.

What makes the find special is how little wood survives underwater for so long. The ship's timbers vanished centuries ago, leaving the cargo as the sole witness. Yet humble pottery, as durable as it is, persists. The shapes, the clay, the fingerprints of ancient potters — all survive to whisper the story of who sailed where and traded what.

4. A Work Still in Progress

The researchers caution that the dating is still being refined. A full study has yet to appear in a peer-reviewed journal, and the wreck site is still yielding new details. But even a tentative age of 4,500 years reshapes how we think about ancient Mediterranean life. It suggests that sophisticated seafaring and organized trade networks were already up and running in an era most of us can barely imagine.

Fun Fact

The oldest shipwreck in the world sat undiscovered in the shallows off Turkey for millennia — its secrets preserved by the very calm, rocky waters that hid it in plain sight.
