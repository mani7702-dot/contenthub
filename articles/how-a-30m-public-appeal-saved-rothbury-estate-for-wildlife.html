How a £30m Public Appeal Saved Rothbury Estate for Wildlife

The numbers, the names, and the next chapter.

An area of rolling hills, ancient woodland, and patchwork farmland the size of

Athens — 3,800 hectares, nearly 15 square miles — has passed from private

hands into a new future. On October 30, 2026, the Wildlife Trusts announced

that a £30 million public appeal had reached its goal, securing the Rothbury

Estate in Northumberland and launching one of England's most ambitious

nature-recovery rewilding projects.

1. What Was Raised — and Why It Matters

More than 25,000 people donated £17 million of the total £30 million, with the

National Lottery Heritage Fund contributing £5 million and the government's

Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) adding £1.94m. Sir

David Attenborough, whose backing of the appeal helped rally a surge of

international donations, called it "an opportunity to create something very

special for generations to come."

The estate had been owned by the Percy family — the youngest son of the Duke of

Northumberland — for about 700 years. It was the largest area of land in single

ownership to come up for sale in England in more than three decades, and

campaigners described the purchase as a "once-in-a-generation opportunity."

2. What Lives There Already

The Simonside Hills rise from the estate's centre, and red squirrels — whose

numbers have dwindled nationally — can already be seen in the woods. Curlews

wheel over the moorland, and mountain bumblebees hum among the heather. River

Coquet runs through the valley, where salmon and the vulnerable European eel

have been spotted. Harebell, wild thyme, and mountain pansy flower colourfully

in summer.

3. What Might Return

The Trusts' vision extends far beyond protecting what is already there. They

hope to restore and extend habitats so that additional species move back in:

perhaps water voles along the riverbanks, perhaps pine martens in the deeper

woodland. The long-term dream is a 40-mile "wild corridor" stretching from

Druridge Bay on the North Sea coast to the Scottish border at Kielder, linking

fragments of habitat so that wildlife can move, adapt, and thrive.

Eco-acoustic monitors are already being placed across the estate — volunteers

walk for miles through bracken as high as their shoulders to install and

retrieve them — recording the sound of small mammals, bats, and birds so

scientists can track how the landscape changes over time.

4. Not Everyone Agrees — Yet

Some tenant farmers have voiced concerns, calling the project "a folly" and

questioning whether farming businesses can remain profitable under new

management. The trust's response is to demonstrate that wildlife and working

farms can coexist: "We want to show that you can have lots of wildlife and

still have a productive business model," said Mike Pratt, Northumberland

Wildlife Trust chief executive.

Fun Fact

The estate sits near Hadrian's Wall country, and the Simonside Hills contain

some of England's largest clusters of ancient "cup and ring" rock carvings —

mysterious spirals and grids carved by hands some 4,000 years ago.
