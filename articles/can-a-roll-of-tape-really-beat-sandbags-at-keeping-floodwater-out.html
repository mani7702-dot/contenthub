Can a Roll of Tape Really Beat Sandbags at Keeping Floodwater Out?

2026-10-303 min

A UK experiment showed that ordinary rolls of tape can block up to 99% of floodwater sneaking past a door, easily beating the 70% kept out by traditional sandbags — a handy trick for families when a storm surge hits with little warning.

Can a roll of tape really beat sandbags at keeping floodwater out?

1. Why tapes beat sandbags in a head-to-head test

When flood warnings flash on the evening news, most of us picture stacking heavy, gritty sandbags at the door. But what if the best defense is already living in your toolbox? At the University of Hull's Property Flood Resilience lab, researchers flooded a life-size front door sitting inside a giant water tank and then tried sealing it with tape instead of sandbags.

They tested four everyday products: flexible synthetic rubber tape (the kind used for plumbing and roofing), good-old duct tape, aluminum foil tape, and yellow plastic sheeting tape. Every tape was pressed firmly around the edges of a standard door, then the tank was filled with water up to two feet (about 60 cm) deep — the kind of low-level flooding that often catches homeowners off guard.

The results surprised everyone. The strongest performer, synthetic rubber tape, cut water leakage by 99% compared with an unprotected door. Duct tape held back 97 to 98%. Even the weakest tape still stopped 80% of the seepage. By contrast, sandbags managed only about 70% under the same conditions — a gap that means several extra buckets of water slipping inside to soak your floors and walls.

2. The hidden superpower of door direction

You might expect tape and sandbags to be the only difference, but the scientists noticed something else, too: the way a door opens matters. When the test door swung outward, it leaked far less than when it opened inward — even before any tape or sandbags were added. That is because outward-opening doors press tighter against the frame when water pushes against them, like a lid sealing a pot. So if you live somewhere flood-prone, knowing how your door is hung could be a small detail that saves a big puddle.

The testing was intentionally modest: water no deeper than two feet, doors of six common styles, and the realistic race against time when an alert comes and there are only minutes to act. That is exactly the situation the project was built to study. Flood Re — a joint effort between the UK government and home insurers — commissioned the work so families would know which quick fixes actually work when the forecast turns grim.

3. Tape is a sidekick, not a superhero cape

Let's keep this honest: Professor Stuart McLelland, who leads the Hull team, is clear that tape should never replace proper, permanent flood defenses that a licensed installer fits once and forgets. Think of tape as a Plan B — something you can grab in a hurry to buy time and protection when a storm surge hits faster than you can haul sandbags.

Still, the practical perks add up fast. A roll of tape costs a few dollars, weighs almost nothing, stores in a kitchen drawer, and does not rot, mildew, or smell after it dries. Sandbags, by contrast, are heavy, messy, and usually end up abandoned in a garage until next year's mildew bloom. Tape also loses no strength once it is wet, so it keeps doing its job for the entire length of a flood — not just the first hour.

A 2007 flood in Hull itself showed why speed matters: roughly a month's worth of rain fell in a single day, swamping about 10,000 homes. The university team recreated that 600 mm (two-foot) water level in the lab precisely to mirror what real families faced then and could face again.

Fun Fact

The 99% figure belongs to a specialty tape called butyl rubber membrane, which is so sticky it was originally designed to seal spacecraft hulls! Duct tape, a household staple since World War II, came in a strong second place — proving that some old friends are still good in a crisis.
