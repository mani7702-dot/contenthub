Can we really un-break plastic? A new way to recycle acrylic glass

2026-10-223 min

Researchers in Switzerland found a clever trick to take old acrylic plastic — the kind in car lights and display cases — apart into its original building blocks, so it can be made into new plastic again without the huge heat old methods needed.

Some plastic is famous for being un-recyclable. Think of a sturdy, clear plastic sheet — the kind used for car tail lights, display cases, or the little stand that holds your school menu. You can bend it, drill it, scratch it… but you cannot melt it back into its original soup.

That is about to change. A team of researchers at ETH Zurich in Switzerland has found a way to take this plastic apart again — cleanly, cheaply, and with about half the heat that older methods demanded.

What is acrylic glass, anyway?

The material has a brand name you may have heard: Plexiglas (or Perspex, or Lucite). Chemically it is called PMMA — short for poly(methyl methacrylate).

Here is the clever part. PMMA is made by taking thousands of tiny identical molecules, called monomers, and hooking them together end to end into one very long chain. Once hooked, they are extremely hard to unhook. Traditional recycling had two bad options: melt the plastic down and hope for the best (which dulls it and shortens the chains), or cook it at 400 °C or hotter to tear the chains apart. That second option is like trying to un-bake a cake by setting it on fire — you get the ingredients back, but you burn a lot of energy doing it.

The new trick: a chemical pair of scissors

The ETH Zurich team's idea was pleasantly simple. They added one cheap, off-the-shelf chemical — a member of a family called N-hydroxyimides — directly to the plastic, with no solvent and no special equipment.

Think of it like giving the long chain a pair of scissors that knows exactly where to cut. Once the reagent is added, the reaction starts on its own and the chain falls apart, releasing the monomers that were hiding inside. Because the plastic was never dissolved, and because the reaction begins at just over 200 °C instead of 400 °C, the energy bill shrinks dramatically.

In the best experiments the team pulled back up to 98% of the material as pure monomer — and they say a second pass over the leftovers pushes the total to about 99%.

What about the messy stuff?

Real waste is never clean. Old taillights are dyed, scratched, and mixed with a bit of this and that. The good news: the recovered monomer came out pure enough to build new products that match the quality of brand-new plastic.

The researchers have filed a patent and are looking for industry partners to scale the method up. They also hope the same basic idea might one day work on other common plastics, such as polystyrene.

Meanwhile, a team at the University of Bath in the UK reported a different low-temperature route for the same plastic at the same time: using ultraviolet light in an oxygen-free chamber at 120–180 °C, they turned more than 95% of real consumer acrylic into usable monomer. Two separate teams, two separate mechanisms, one shared goal — making the recycling of acrylic actually pay for itself.

Try it yourself: find a plastic item at home — a clear storage box lid, a CD jewel case, a phone stand — and check its underside for a tiny triangle with a number inside. That number (7 for PMMA) is the resin identification code, and it tells recyclers exactly what material they are holding. Sorting by that little triangle is the first, simplest step toward recycling working.

For the grown-ups

PMMA's monomers are chemically locked together by ester bonds. Typical bulk depolymerisation demands 350–400 °C, which is energy-hungry and tends to char the material and produce side products. The ETH Zurich approach (Whitfield, Lohmann, Agrachev et al., Nature Chemistry, 2026) uses a weak-link motif: a cheap N-hydroxyimide reagent cleaves the methacrylate chains directly, releasing methyl methacrylate monomer at just over 200 °C with no solvent and no catalyst, in bulk. The resulting monomer was re-polymerised back into material with properties comparable to virgin PMMA. Separately, Husband, Freakley and Davidson's group at Bath (University of Bath ISCC; Nature Communications, 2026) used UV initiation under oxygen-free conditions at 120–180 °C with non-chlorinated solvents, achieving >95% conversion and >70% monomer yield from consumer-grade waste plastic. Note the two routes use different chemistry and different energy inputs; both remain at lab or pilot scale, and real economics depend on feedstock sorting, energy prices, and the cost of the reagents.
