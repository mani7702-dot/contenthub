How did removing a dam bring salmon back to a river for the first time in 100 years?

2026-10-224 min

A century after the Elwha Dam silenced the river, salmon are swimming upstream again — and a tribe is reclaiming 1,100 acres of the land that was flooded for over 100 years.

How did removing a dam bring salmon back to a river for the first time in 100 years?

The river that lost its voice

For more than a century, the Elwha River on the Olympic Peninsula of Washington State flowed in silence where it used to roar. In 1910, builders raised the Elwha Dam across the river — a 105-foot wall of concrete that turned the flowing water into a quiet, brown reservoir called Lake Aldwell. No fish could pass. Not salmon, not trout, not anything that needed to swim from the ocean back up to spawn.

To the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, this was not just an environmental loss. It was the loss of a way of life. The river had fed their people for thousands of years before Columbus was born. When the dam went up, it flooded 1,100 acres of their ancestral land — including historic villages and the very spot, elders say, where the tribe was blessed into existence by the Creator.

The day the water came down

In 2012, something remarkable happened: the Elwha Dam came crashing down in the largest dam-removal project in U.S. history. Not overnight — piece by piece, slow enough for everyone to watch. And as the water rushed back through valleys that had been dry for a hundred years, something else rushed too: the salmon.

Salmon are born in rivers, swim to the ocean to grow big, then turn around and swim all the way back — sometimes thousands of miles — to lay their eggs in the exact stream where they were born. The Elwha's salmon remembered the way. Within just a few years of the dam's removal, chinook salmon were spawning again in places they had not reached since the 1890s.

The river did not just come back to life — it came back to memory.

One signature away from home

Now, more than a decade after the dam fell, the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe is one signature away from reclaiming that 1,100 acres of land once flooded by Lake Aldwell. A federal bill has passed both houses of Congress and now waits on a desk for an executive signature.

The parcel sits right along the free-flowing Elwha, between state highways 101 and 112, and it touches the edge of Olympic National Park. It is not just open space — it is a living, breathing classroom where kingfishers dive, mountain lions walk, dippers (a type of songbird that walks underwater) flit, and yes — bears, possums, and salmon all share the same wild corridor.

"This is part of who we are as people," says Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe Chair Melissa Gilman. The salmon aren't just food. They are relatives.

Try this at home

  • Dam simulation: Fill a tall glass with water and drop in a few pebbles to make a "dam." Watch how the water pools behind it. Now slowly lift one pebble out — can you see the water start to flow again? That's what happens when we remove barriers from rivers.
  • Salmon life cycle game: Draw the salmon's journey! Start in a river (ocean dot on your paper), swim downstream to the ocean (blue scribble), grow big, then swim ALL the way back to lay eggs. It's the longest commute in the animal kingdom.
  • River sound map: Sit quietly near a stream or creek (or just listen to recorded river sounds on a safe device). Close your eyes and listen for the different sounds — fast water over rocks, slow water in pools, birds calling. A healthy river is full of voices.

Fun Fact

The name "Elwha" comes from the Klallam word for the river — and the only other place a fish called a "resident rainbow" lives is... a fish tank. The Elwha River once hosted one of the largest runs of chinook salmon on the entire Pacific coast. After 100 years of being blocked, biologists thought the salmon might never find their way back. But salmon have a memory older than concrete. In 2023, scientists counted returning spawners at numbers not seen since the 1890s.

"The salmon aren't just salmon," says Tribal Chair Melissa Gilman. "This is part of who we are as people."
