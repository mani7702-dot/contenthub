How Did a Rare Bird Help Protect a Whole Rainforest?

2026-10-273 min

Scientists discovered a rare bird — the grey-necked picathartes — far to the south of its known range in the Democratic Republic of Congo's Luki Biosphere Reserve. This exciting find adds new urgency to plans for a transfrontier protected area that would link forests across borders, safeguarding chimpanzees, forest elephants, and countless other rainforest creatures.

How Did a Rare Bird Help Protect a Whole Rainforest?

A bird nobody expected to find

Deep in the green heart of central Africa, hidden among the trees of the Luki Biosphere Reserve, scientists made a surprise discovery. They weren't looking for a bird — they had set up hidden cameras to count the reserve's mammals, hoping to spot the chimpanzees that still call this forest home.

But when the photos came back, something unusual was hopping across the forest floor: a rare, long-legged bird called the grey-necked picathartes. The twist? This bird had never been seen anywhere near here before. It was discovered 600 kilometers (370 miles) south of where scientists thought it lived — basically finding a lost relative in the wrong neighborhood!

A bird that opens doors

The grey-necked picathartes is a very special bird. It is a ground-foraging bird, meaning it walks through the leaf litter on the forest floor hunting for insects and other tiny creatures. It's an "umbrella species" — protecting it means protecting dozens of other rainforest animals that share its home.

But this little bird is also a very rare one. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) lists it as Endangered — meaning it faces a very high risk of disappearing forever. And now, thanks to the surprise discovery in the DRC, scientists have a brand-new reason to protect the area. The bird's presence helped push forward plans for a Transfrontier Protected Area — a fancy name for a cross-border nature reserve that would link forest patches across the borders of the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Republic of Congo, and Angola.

More friends in the forest

The Luki Biosphere Reserve isn't the only home at stake. The proposed protected area would also shield the central chimpanzee, an endangered great ape that uses tools and lives in social groups. And the African forest elephant — the rainforest's biggest land animal — would find safe passage as these forests connect like stepping stones across borders.

All of these creatures depend on the same dense, humid rainforest that blankets this corner of Africa. Every species that lives here — from the tiniest beetle to the tallest tree — is part of one big, interwoven story. When we protect one piece of the forest, we're really protecting all of them.

Try this at home

  • Spot the difference: Look at pictures of the grey-necked picathartes and the white-necked picathartes. Both are "rockfowl" with bald heads and bright red or yellow skin around their eyes. Can you find three features that are the same and one that is different?
  • Build a model forest: Draw a cross-section of a rainforest on a piece of paper. At the top, draw the emergent layer (tallest trees). Below that, the canopy. Then the understory. And at the bottom, the forest floor. Place your picathartes on the floor layer!
  • Camera-trap detective game: Set up a toy camera (or a real one!) in your backyard or a local park. Take pictures of animal signs — footprints, feathers, chewed nuts, or holes in the ground. Can you guess who lives there?

Fun Fact

The grey-necked picathartes builds its nest using mud and sticks, and both parents take turns keeping the eggs warm. But here's the cool part — unlike most birds that nest in trees, this one often nests on bare rock or in shallow depressions on the ground, right in the middle of the forest. It's like choosing to sleep in a cave instead of a treehouse!

for-grown-ups:

The grey-necked picathartes (Picathartes oreas) is a ground-foraging passerine in the family Picathartidae, listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Its known range spans the Upper Guinean rainforests from southwest Nigeria through Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, and southwest Gabon, plus Bioko Island — but it had never before been recorded in the Democratic Republic of Congo. In 2026, ecologist Jean Semeki and colleagues installed camera traps in the Luki Biosphere Reserve (a small, severely threatened forest fragment in western DRC) and unexpectedly captured the species roughly 600 km south of its previously documented range. This southern record, combined with recent sightings in the Republic of Congo's southwest and Angola's northern Cabinda province, gives new weight to the proposed Mayombe Forest Transfrontier Protected Area (TFPA) — a cross-border conservation initiative linking fragments of the once-continuous Lower Guinean forest. The TFPA would protect not only the picathartes but also the endangered central chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes troglodytes), the African forest elephant (Loxodonta cyclotis), and hundreds of other endemic and threatened species. The discovery underscores how little we still know about rainforest biodiversity and how every intact forest patch matters for survival.
