How Did Antarctica's First Dinosaur Bone Hide in a Drawer for 40 Years?

2026-10-31T00:45:00+05:304 min

A small fossil collected on an icy Antarctic island in 1985 sat forgotten in a museum drawer for four decades — until a curious scientist opened it and realised it was the first dinosaur bone ever found on the continent.

How Did Antarctica's First Dinosaur Bone Hide in a Drawer for 40 Years?

A Notebook, a Sketch, and a Mystery

In December 1985, geologist Dr. Mike Thomson was working on James Ross Island, off the Antarctic Peninsula, as part of a British Antarctic Survey expedition. The team was mostly hunting for shellfish fossils called ammonites, but one find stood out. In his field notebook, dated 9 December 1985, Thomson drew a small neat sketch of a bone about 10 centimetres wide and wrote beside it: "vertebra of large reptile."

Nobody was quite sure what it was. The fossil was packed up, shipped home, and filed away in the geology collection of the British Antarctic Survey in Cambridge. There it stayed — for almost forty years.

The Day Someone Asked "What's in This Drawer?"

Dr. Mark Evans, a palaeontologist who manages the collection, was going through thousands of specimens one day when the bone caught his eye. "It's only when you start thinking 'what's in this drawer', that sometimes you come across something and you think, 'Ah, this looks interesting,'" he said.

Evans called in Professor Paul Barrett from the Natural History Museum in London. The moment Barrett held it, he knew. "As soon as I saw it, I knew what we were dealing with… it was a dead cert we were dealing with a Titanosaur," he said. The bone's ball-and-socket shape was unique to that group.

What Was a Titanosaur Doing in Antarctica?

Titanosaurs were four-legged plant-eaters with very long necks and long balancing tails. They include the largest dinosaurs that ever walked the Earth, some reaching over 35 metres and weighing about 60 tonnes. This Antarctic one was far smaller — roughly 6 to 7 metres — either a youngster or a genuinely small species.

It lived about 82 million years ago, when Antarctica was not icy at all but covered in lush forest, perfect for a hungry plant-eater. The discovery, published in the journal Acta Palaeontologica Polonica, also hints at how dinosaurs spread. Titanosaurs probably travelled from South America across Antarctica to Australia and New Zealand, which were all joined at the time.

Antarctica has the sparsest dinosaur record of any continent because most of its rock is buried under ice, so every bone matters — even one that waited forty years to be noticed.

Fun fact

More than 100 species of titanosaur have been identified worldwide, and a cast of the biggest one of all, Patagotitan mayorum, has been on display inside Peterborough Cathedral in England.
