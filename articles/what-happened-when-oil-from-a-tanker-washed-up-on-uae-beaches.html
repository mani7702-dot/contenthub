What happened when oil from a tanker washed up on UAE beaches?

A trail from space

Imagine spilling a single drop of cooking oil into a pool — then watching it fan out into a long, dark ribbon. That is, in a very big way, what happened off the coast of the United Arab Emirates this month.

Satellite pictures from space first spotted the problem. A tanker called the Gem No.2 started leaking oil near the Strait of Hormuz — a narrow waterway that connects the Arabian Gulf to the rest of the world and through which millions of barrels of oil pass every day. From orbit, the spill looked like a curved silver streak trailing behind the ship.

How far did it travel?

At first the leak was small. By October 19, satellites could see a thin plume coming from the tanker. But oil moves fast on water, pushed by wind and currents. Over the next two days, radar satellite images showed the slick had grown to more than 35 km (22 miles) long.

By October 22, the oil was reaching the shore. It washed up on beaches near the northern UAE city of Umm Al Quwain — coloring the sea black right where families like to swim and play.

What are people doing about it?

The UAE's Ministry of Climate Change and Environment said it "immediately began containment" — meaning teams moved in to try to trap the oil before it spreads further, using long floating barriers.

Scientists are worried about the local wildlife. Prof Steve Widdicombe from the Sharjah Marine Science Research Centre said some impact on sea creatures was "inevitable," but he also said it was too soon to know exactly how big the damage is. That is why collecting samples and watching the beaches stays important over the next few weeks.

Why you probably haven't heard about it

The Strait of Hormuz might sound like a grown-up topic, but it matters to everyone — because so much of the world's oil travels through it. When a tanker is damaged, that oil can end up in the sea and wash up on distant beaches, even affecting the smell of the air for miles inland. In fact, residents across several UAE cities reported a "fuel-like" smell in the days after the spill.

The good news? Satellites that watch our planet from space — like the European Copernicus Sentinel missions — can spot a spill within hours, helping cleanup crews race to the right spot faster than ever before.

Try this at home

Make a "spill map" with a baking tray. Fill a shallow tray with water, add a few drops of blue or green food coloring, then drop one tiny dot of oil-based paint thinner or cooking oil in the center. Watch how the "spill" spreads — and try placing a cotton-ball barrier around it to see how containment works. (Adult help required; do this on a washable surface and never do this with real chemicals!)

Fun Fact

Earth is often called the "Blue Planet" because water covers over 71% of its surface. That means protecting our oceans — from tiny plankton to the biggest whales — helps protect the whole planet.
