How Did 53 Orangutans Find Safety After Borneo's Devastating Fires?

2026-10-30T02:21:00+05:304 min

When forest fires swept across Borneo this summer, a dedicated rescue team sprang into action — saving 53 orangutans and giving them a second chance in the wild.

How Did 53 Orangutans Find Safety After Borneo's Devastating Fires?

1. When the Flames Arrived, the Rescue Began

In August 2026, forest and peatland fires began sweeping across West Kalimantan on the island of Borneo. For the critically endangered orangutans who call these forests home, the flames meant danger — their food sources were disappearing, and their canopy shelters were turning to ash.

But help was already on the way. The Yayasan Inisiasi Alam Rehabilitasi Indonesia — known as YIARI — immediately launched emergency operations alongside local environment authorities. Between August 1 and October 21, their team responded to urgent calls, racing to find stranded orangutans in fire-affected areas.

2. Fifty-Three Orangutans, All in Need

In total, 53 Bornean orangutans (Pongo pygmaeus) were rescued during this three-month window. Many arrived at YIARI's Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre in critical condition — severely underweight from days or weeks without adequate food, and covered in cuts and injuries from the burning landscape.

Dr. Karmele Llano Sanchez, YIARI's CEO and a wildlife veterinarian, examined each animal upon arrival. She noted that younger orangutans were especially vulnerable — separated from their mothers in the chaos, they had nowhere to hide and no source of their usual forest fare.

3. The Long Road Back to the Wild

Recovery took time. Under veterinary care, each orangutan received food, medical treatment, and a safe space to regain strength. The thin, frightened babies gradually learned to eat again and climbed onto climbing frames to rebuild their muscle strength.

But rehabilitating them was only half the challenge. Unlike animals that could simply be returned to their original territory — now charred and barren — these orangutans needed new forest preserves. Many were rewilded to Gunung Palung National Park, a protected area several hours away that still offered the tall canopy trees and fruiting vines orangutans need to thrive.

4. A Centre Full, a Mission Not Done

By late October, YIARI's rehabilitation centre had reached full capacity. The team continues to patrol burned areas, ready to respond to the next emergency call. Seasonal rains — delayed from October into November — are expected to help extinguish the remaining blazes and bring new growth to the scorched landscape.

The fires burned more than 48,500 hectares (about 120,000 acres) across West Kalimantan in 2026 — an area roughly the size of Vienna. While the numbers are sobering, the story of those 53 rescued orangutans reminds us that dedicated conservationists never stop fighting to protect these gentle giants.

Fun Fact

Orangutans are the only great apes (besides humans) with long, orange-red hair. The name "orangutan" actually comes from the Malay/Indonesian words orang (person) and hutan (forest) — so it literally means "person of the forest." They spend up to 90% of their waking hours eating and resting in trees.
