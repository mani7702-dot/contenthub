How Does the Glue Holding Your Cells Together Help Clean Up Dead Ones?

1. A glue with a hidden talent

Your body is lined with thin sheets of cells that fit together like tiles, forming the skin, the gut and the airways. The protein that holds those tiles in place is called E-cadherin — think of it as the body's molecular glue.

Scientists have long known what E-cadherin does: it links each cell to its neighbours. But a team at the Center for Genomic Regulation in Barcelona has discovered it has a second, unexpected job. It also helps cells swallow their dead neighbours.

That matters more than it sounds. Cells in busy tissues die all the time, and if their leftovers pile up, they can trigger inflammation. Someone has to clear them away — and it turns out the glue is part of the cleanup crew.

2. Catching cells in the act

The researchers watched living tissue do this in real time, using embryos of zebrafish and mice, which have cells that behave much like our own. They saw the E-cadherin machinery gather at the exact spot where a healthy cell touched a dying one.

To test what was really going on, they ran two clever experiments. First, they removed E-cadherin from the dying cells — the healthy cells ate them anyway. Then they offered tiny droplets of fat instead of cells, carrying only the "eat me" signal a dying cell normally shows. The cells swallowed those too.

So the glue on the outside of the dying cell wasn't the point. It was the E-cadherin complex inside the surrounding tissue that did the work.

3. Like dancers with linked arms

Here's the tricky part: an epithelial sheet has to stay sealed, like a waterproof wall, even while one of its cells stretches to swallow something the size of another cell.

The team found that the two surfaces of a single cell behave differently. The lower surface stretches and curves around the dying cell. The upper surface barely changes at all, keeping the barrier around it intact.

The study's senior author, Verena Ruprecht, compares it to a line of dancers standing with their arms linked: their upper bodies stay steady while their feet perform increasingly complicated steps. "It's the same dancer with a different choreography," she says.

The work was published in the journal Nature Communications.

4. Why it matters

When the body's cleanup system fails, dead-cell debris can build up and drive long-term inflammation. Understanding exactly how healthy tissues remove that debris — and keep themselves sealed while doing it — gives scientists new clues about what goes wrong in chronic inflammatory conditions.

It's a tidy reminder that the parts of our bodies we think we already know can still surprise us.

Fun Fact

Cells that eat dead neighbours were first watched under the microscope more than 100 years ago. But for decades, most scientists assumed this was a job for specialist immune cells alone. We now know that ordinary tissue cells, like the ones lining your skin and gut, quietly help out too — every single day.
