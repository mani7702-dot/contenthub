Why Did a Rare Beetle That Rides on Bees Come Back to Wales After 60 Years?

2026-11-013 min

A little beetle that hitches a lift on bees to raise its young has turned up breeding in Wales for the first time in six decades — and its comeback is a hopeful sign.

Why Did a Rare Beetle That Rides on Bees Come Back to Wales After 60 Years?

The beetle that vanished

Imagine a small, shiny black beetle that disappeared from an entire country for 60 years. That is exactly what happened to the Short-Necked Oil Beetle in Wales. It was not seen there from the late 1940s onward, and as its flower-rich coastal habitat shrank, scientists feared it had quietly slipped away for good.

This autumn, that worry turned into cheers. After a three-year search, surveyors found the beetle's larvae — its young — in a small patch of the Castlemartin Range in Pembrokeshire, confirming that the species is not just surviving in Wales, but breeding there.

A most unusual childhood

The Short-Necked Oil Beetle (Meloe brevicollis) has one of the strangest life stories in the insect world. Its newly hatched larvae climb onto flowers and wait. When a solitary bee lands to collect nectar, the tiny beetle grabs hold and hitches a ride back to the bee's nest.

Once inside, the larvae feed on the pollen the bee has stored for its own young. It is a clever trick — and it also means the beetle cannot survive anywhere its bee neighbours do not. Take away the bees, the flowers, or the bare earth where the beetle lays its eggs, and the whole chain falls apart.

The beetle needs a very particular home: bare earth for egg-laying, thick vegetation for shelter, and dense flowers for food. That combination is rare, which is why only a handful of sites in the whole of the United Kingdom suit it.

Why this year is different

The news is especially welcome because last year's survey found only dead adults, which raised fears the beetle might finally be gone. During this year's patient search, the team spotted larvae instead — a sign of a new generation.

Experts at Natural Resources Wales say the number and spread of the little larvae make them hopeful. Anne Hobbs, from the Bumblebee Conservation Trust, called it "very exciting" that the evidence pointed to the species breeding again on the site.

Fun fact

The beetle is monitored as part of a project that protects the Shrill Carder Bee — and the two share the same coastal home. Protect the bee, and you protect the beetle that secretly depends on it.

A small win worth celebrating

The Short-Necked Oil Beetle is still nationally rare in Great Britain and listed as vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Adults grow to about 24 millimetres (roughly 0.9 inches) and live for around a year, most active on warm, sunny days between March and June.

No single discovery saves a species. But a beetle riding a bee back to a Welsh flower patch — after 60 long years away — is a gentle reminder that nature can return when we give it room to.
