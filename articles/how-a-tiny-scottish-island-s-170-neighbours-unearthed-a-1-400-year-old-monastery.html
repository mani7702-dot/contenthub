How a tiny Scottish island's 170 neighbours unearthed a 1,400-year-old monastery

2026-10-274 min

On a 9-mile-long island off Scotland's west coast, a few dozen neighbours crowdfunded an archaeology dig — and found a medieval monastery so rich it may have rivalled Iona.

How a tiny Scottish island's 170 neighbours unearthed a 1,400-year-old monastery

170 people. One big question. A giant surprise.

Lismore is a long, thin island off the west coast of Scotland. It is just 9 miles across, and only about 170 people live there. It sounds like the kind of place where nothing very exciting ever happens — but a small group of neighbours on the island had a hunch.

They belonged to the local Lismore Historical Society, and they wondered about something old and mysterious sitting right under their feet: a ruined 1,300-year-old cathedral. Was it really just a little island church? Or was it hiding a secret from the distant past?

So they did something amazing. They crowdfunded an archaeology dig — regular people, mostly, chipping in their own money to hire professional archaeologists and start digging.

The dig found not one secret, but three

The professional team (from a company called Argyll Archaeology) set to work around the old cathedral. And the soil gave up three huge discoveries:

1. An ancient graveyard. At least six burials were found, dated by scientists to between 650 and 1040 CE — more than a thousand years ago. Carved stone cross fragments lay nearby.

2. A mysterious workshop. They found an oval-shaped building with a floor paved in limestone. Inside were handmade pottery, tools, and signs of craftspeople at work.

3. A royal-style metalworking site. In one trench, diggers pulled out more than 1,000 pieces of metalwork debris — including tiny crucibles and molds where melted gold, copper and silver were once poured into shapes.

That last part was the biggest surprise of all. Making jewellery and precious metal objects was serious, high-status work. The archaeologists say it's one of the largest collections of metalworking finds from any site of that time — even bigger than most castles or churches.

Why it matters

The island's name, Lios Mòr, comes from an old monastery founded by a saint called Mo Luóc in the 500s — around 1,400 years ago. For centuries, historians knew it from old records and local stories, but nobody could prove where it was or how big it really was.

Now they can. The dig shows Lismore wasn't a quiet little island church at all. It was a wealthy, powerful centre of learning, worship and craftsmanship — possibly as important as Iona, one of the most famous monasteries in early Britain.

"Far more than a small island church," said the researchers who studied the finds.

Try this at home

You don't need a team of archaeologists to be a history detective. Pick one place near you — a park, a school, an old building in town — and try to find three clues about how old it is or who used to be there. Look at the shapes of bricks, old maps, or even stories a grandparent tells. Every place has a hidden past, and it only takes curiosity (and sometimes a shovel!) to start uncovering it.
