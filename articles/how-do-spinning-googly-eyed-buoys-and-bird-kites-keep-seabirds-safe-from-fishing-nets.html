How do spinning googly-eyed buoys and bird kites keep seabirds safe from fishing nets?

2026-10-263 min

Off the coast of Cornwall, engineers are testing colorful 'scarecrow' devices — spinning buoys with big googly eyes and soaring bird-of-prey kites — that warn seabirds away from fishing nets, cutting accidental catches by up to 71%.

How do spinning googly-eyed buoys and bird kites keep seabirds safe from fishing nets?

The invisible danger in the water

Seabirds like the common murre (also called the common guillemot) are amazing divers. They can plunge underwater and "fly" through the ocean using their wings, hunting small fish for miles at a time. But there's a hidden danger beneath the waves: long walls of nearly invisible fishing nets called gillnets. These nets stretch across the water like underwater curtains, and diving birds can't see them in time to avoid them. When birds get tangled, it's a problem for both the birds and the fishing boats.

A team of scientists in England had an idea: what if the nets had their own guards — like a pair of big, spinning, googly eyes that stare back at the birds?

Googly eyes and scary kites

Off the coast of Cornwall, in southwest England, researchers tested two clever deterrent devices on real fishing boats:

1. Spinning "looming-eye" buoys — bright buoys with giant eyes that swivel on top, creating what scientists call an "animated eye effect." It looks like a big face watching the water!

2. Bird-of-prey kites — kites shaped like hawks and eagles that glide and dive above the nets, warning other birds to stay away.

The fishermen helped out by setting nets in pairs: one net with the deterrents, and one without. A tiny camera and GPS tracker recorded everything. Over 17 months, they set more than 1,000 nets stretching about 80 miles of mesh. The results? Both devices worked really well — the kites cut accidental bird catches by 71%, and the spinning-eye buoys cut them by 59%.

When does it work best?

The scientists noticed that accidental catches were highest during winter — especially January and February — when sea bass and mackerel migrate close to shore. This means that if fishermen use these protective devices just during the winter months, it could save thousands of birds without changing how they fish all year long.

But the devices aren't perfect. The bird kites are tricky to handle in very strong or very calm winds, and the buoys can sometimes attract seals instead of deterring birds. The researchers say more testing and design tweaks will make them even better. As one scientist said: "Seeing both deterrent types work well across such a large real-world trial was genuinely encouraging."

Try this at home

  • Make your own googly-eyed buoy out of a plastic bottle, some foil, and a paper plate with big drawn eyes. Float it in a birdbath or pool (with adult help) and watch how the "staring" eyes seem to follow you around — that's the same trick these buoys use!
  • Fly a bird-of-prey kite (or make a simple one from paper and string) and see how its shadow moving overhead makes other birds scatter. You'll be testing the same scarecrow idea used on fishing boats!
  • Draw a picture of a fishing net and add the deterrents: floating eyes, spinning buoys, and diving kites. How many creative ways can you think of to warn animals away from danger?

Fun Fact

There are more than 360 species of seabirds, including puffins, gannets, cormorants, and the murres and razorbills mentioned in the study. These birds are some of the ocean's best underwater hunters — the common murre can dive to depths of over 500 feet and stay down for more than a minute!
