Why do tropical trees breathe at night — and how does it help them survive drought?

2026-10-253 min

Some tropical trees have a special trick: they open their pores at night, store carbon dioxide as a sour juice, and keep them closed all day. Scientists found that an ancient genome-duplication event gave different Clusia trees different versions of this water-saving superpower.

1. Why do tropical trees breathe at night — and how does it help them survive drought?

The night shift photosynthesizers

Imagine if you had to hold your breath all day and only breathe at night. That sounds impossible for a person — but for some tropical trees, it is the secret to surviving the dry season.

Most plants open their tiny pores — called stomata — during the daytime. That is when sunlight powers photosynthesis, the process that turns water, carbon dioxide, and sunlight into sugary food. But every time a plant opens its pores, it loses water through evaporation. In a hot, dry climate, that is a big problem.

Certain tropical trees have solved this puzzle with an unusual schedule. They keep their stomata closed during the day and open them at night instead. That is when they grab carbon dioxide from the air — but they do not use it right away. They chemically store it as a sour, energy-rich molecule called malic acid, like a battery charging overnight.

A discovery that dates back 200 years

The story starts around 1800 with a famous explorer named Alexander von Humboldt. While studying a tropical tree in South America, he placed a leaf in a glass of water and shone sunlight on it. He expected to see bubbles of oxygen — the usual sign that photosynthesis is working. Instead, he saw nothing. The leaf was a Clusia tree, and it was keeping its photosynthesis schedule flipped.

Modern scientists now know this trick is called CAM photosynthesis — short for Crassulacean Acid Metabolism. (It was first discovered in the crassula family of succulent plants, which is where the name comes from.) But the Clusia genus is special: it includes the only known trees that use CAM, and different Clusia species use it in different ways.

How ancient DNA rewired the drought trick

A research team led by Wolfram Weckwerth at the University of Vienna wanted to understand how CAM evolved in Clusia. They compared the genomes of three species: Clusia rosea, Clusia minor, and Clusia major. Each one uses CAM, but in its own way — from strong CAM (nighttime-only) to stress-triggered and hybrid strategies.

The surprise? All three species are ancient polyploids. At some point in their evolutionary past, their entire genome was copied — think of it as a full backup of all their DNA instructions. Over millions of years, those copied genes were reorganized and reshaped in a process called diploidization. Some gene copies were lost, some became inactive, and some took on entirely new jobs.

That genetic reshuffling gave each Clusia species its own version of the CAM toolkit — a bit like having the same recipe book but different chefs who each add their own twist. One species might open its pores only during extreme drought, another might use a mixed strategy, and a third relies fully on the nighttime approach.

Try this at home

Test your plant's breathing rhythm! Pick two plants — one that you suspect uses CAM (like a succulent or cactus) and one that does not (like a houseplant with ordinary leaves). Cover one pot with a clear plastic bag overnight and leave the other uncovered. In the morning, feel the inside of each bag. The CAM plant’s bag may feel damper — because it breathed while everything else was sleeping!

Fun Fact

Clusia trees can grow up to 15 meters tall. And while most of the world's trees use daytime photosynthesis, Clusia is one of the very few tree genera on Earth that has fully switched to the night shift — making it a living laboratory for how plants adapt to a drying world.
