How Did a Famous Math Tile Start Bending Light Into Pinwheels?

2026-10-313 min

A lopsided 13-sided shape solved a famous maths puzzle by never repeating. Now scientists have found it can twist laser light into tiny pinwheels.

Anyone who has tiled a bathroom floor knows the golden rule: lay the same shape over and over, and it lines up in a neat, repeating pattern. A checkerboard repeats. A honeycomb repeats. So for decades, mathematicians wondered whether a single shape could ever cover an endless floor without ever repeating itself. In 2023, a lopsided 13-sided tile nicknamed the "hat" finally did it. Now that same strange shape has surprised scientists a second time — this time by doing something unexpected to light.

A shape that never repeats

The puzzle is known as the Einstein problem, and the name is a cheeky German pun: ein Stein means "one stone." The goal is a monotile — one single shape that can tile a whole surface aperiodically, never settling into a repeating rhythm. The hat, discovered by amateur mathematician David Smith, was the first shape ever shown to pull it off, and the finding made headlines around the world.

That solved the maths. But it left an open question: if a pattern never repeats, what strange things might it do to energy passing through it?

Turning the tile into a light maze

A team at the University of Tokyo's Institute of Industrial Science built the answer. They recreated the hat pattern at the nanoscale — far smaller than a grain of sand — on thin films of silicon nitride, using a technique called electron-beam lithography. Then they shone laser light through it.

Instead of the neat rainbows you might expect from an ordinary crystal, the light came out as pinwheel-shaped patterns, spinning one way rather than another. Those swirls are the signature of chirality — a fancy word for handedness, the way your left and right hands are mirror images that can't be stacked on top of each other. Because the hat pattern has no mirror symmetry, the light inherits that one-sidedness too.

Why handedness is a big deal

Chirality shows up everywhere in nature, from the spiral of a snail's shell to the twist of the molecules inside medicines. Light that carries this handedness is a powerful tool for telling left-handed molecules from right-handed ones — important for making safe drugs. The surprising part here is that the pattern creates this effect all by itself, with no lenses or special mirrors needed, just geometry doing the work.

The researchers also found the effect flipped whenever they mirrored the pattern, proving the light really was following the shape's symmetry. Writing in the journal Nature Communications, the team says such structures could one day lead to more sensitive medical tests and faster, more secure communication — all from a tile that began life as a curious puzzle.

Fun fact: The "Einstein" in the Einstein problem has nothing to do with Albert Einstein. It comes from the German ein Stein, meaning "one stone" — a single tile.
