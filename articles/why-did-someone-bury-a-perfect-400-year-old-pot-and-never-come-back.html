Why Did Someone Bury a Perfect 400-Year-Old Pot and Never Come Back?

2026-11-014 min

A small clay pot, still completely whole after four centuries underground, has left archaeologists in Florida with a wonderful mystery: why would anyone bury something perfectly usable and simply walk away?

Some finds shout. This one is so quiet you could hold it in one hand. Buried for about 400 years beneath a Spanish mission in Florida, a little clay pot has been lifted from the soil completely intact — and nobody can explain why it was left there.

A pot that survived when almost none do

The vessel was shaped by hand, probably by a member of the Potano-Timucua people of North Florida. It is compact enough to sit in your palm, with a plain rim, a smooth grey surface, and no decoration at all. Its purpose is a mystery, as is the number of people who once used it.

What makes it remarkable is simply that it survived. Pots that go into the ground almost always crack or crumble as soil pressure, farming and building shift the earth around them. Whole ones are vanishingly rare.

"It's very unique. Typically, we find things that were broken and discarded," said Gifford Waters of the Florida Museum of Natural History. "You don't leave things behind that you can still use, so why was this purposefully put into a pit?"

The students who found it

The discovery came from a University of Florida field course. Every Friday, students practised real excavation techniques at Mission San Francisco de Potano, a wooded site in northwest Gainesville, using careful one-metre squares and hand trowels.

The Potano moved to this spot in 1585 after Spanish forces pushed them from their earlier home. In 1606, Spain sent a friar to build a mission — one of the first of more than 100 founded across Florida. At its height the village likely held several hundred Potano residents and just one European. The mission was abandoned in 1706, and its traces slowly vanished until artefacts began turning up again in the 1950s.

A clue inside a soldier's building

The little pot was found inside a Spanish-built structure whose purpose is still unclear. Nearby, students uncovered a gun's rear sight and a musket side plate, hinting the building may once have been a barracks for soldiers stationed there as British attacks grew more frequent.

The pot shows no burnt-food residue, so it probably was never set directly over a fire. Soon after, students found fragments of a much larger pot with a rougher surface and unfamiliar material — possibly carried in through trade with St. Augustine, or brought by a newcomer with different traditions.

The two pots, so different from each other, hint at a village buzzing with movement, trade and change — small details in a big story that is still being pieced together.

Fun fact

Even a pot placed in the ground whole rarely makes it to an archaeologist in one piece. This one is the first unbroken pottery vessel the museum's team has ever excavated from a land site — a survivor of four centuries of shifting soil.
