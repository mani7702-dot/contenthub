What Amazing Secrets Has the Tower of London Kept for 1,000 Years?

2026-10-29T15:00:00+05:304 min

For nearly a millennium the Tower of London has worn many crowns: royal palace, armoury, treasury, menagerie, mint, and prison. Its stones have witnessed daring rescues, disguises, a jewel heist, and the footsteps of kings, queens, and even a few famous beasts.

What Amazing Secrets Has the Tower of London Kept for 1,000 Years?

A Stone That Shaped a Nation

Tucked along the north bank of the River Thames in the heart of London stands a fortress so old that its very stones have stories to tell. The Tower of London was founded in 1066, right after William the Conqueror defeated King Harold at the Battle of Hastings, and the mighty White Tower at its heart was built in 1078 as a symbol of his new rule. From that moment on, the Tower would keep changing — growing into a royal palace, an armoury, a treasury, a menagerie, the home of the Royal Mint, and, perhaps most famously, a prison.

For nearly a thousand years it has attracted millions of visitors, all drawn to its mix of grandeur and mystery. But what makes the Tower truly special are the human tales hidden in its halls — stories of courage, clever escapes, daring disguises, and even a jewel heist or two.

1. The Great Crown Jewels Heist (and the Pardoned Thief)

In 1671, Irish adventurer Thomas Blood and several companions hatched a bold plan to steal the Crown Jewels, then kept in the Tower's Jewel House. Posing as a clergyman, Blood befriended the keeper, Talbot Edwards, and even arranged for his accomplice — actually his son — to court Edwards's daughter.

When the jewelled collection was revealed, the thieves struck: they attacked Edwards, seized the crown and other treasures, and made a desperate dash for their horses. But their escape was short-lived. Blood was caught, brought before King Charles II, and astonishingly pardoned. The king even granted him land in Ireland, and the Crown Jewels stayed safely behind.

2. Escapes That Defied the Walls

The Tower's walls have held many prisoners, but some of its most memorable residents simply refused to stay. In the 12th century, Bishop Ranulf Flambard became both the Tower's first recorded prisoner and its first known escapee. His trick? Getting his guards drunk. An accomplice smuggled a rope hidden inside a gallon of wine, which Flambard shared with his watchmen until they were helpless. He then climbed out through a window of the White Tower and vanished into London.

Centuries later, in 1716, the Earl of Nithsdale faced certain execution after the failed Jacobite rising. His wife, Winifred, orchestrated a daring rescue. With the help of two ladies-in-waiting, she smuggled her husband out of his cell disguised in women's clothing — paint for eyebrows, a wig for a beard, a dress for a gown. She returned to the room and even fooled the guards by answering her own questions in Nithsdale's voice, until the coast was clear and escape was complete.

3. The Royal Menagerie and Its Feathered and Furred Residents

For roughly six centuries, the Tower was home to a royal menagerie that would make any zoo envious. It began in 1235 when Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II sent King Henry III a trio of big cats — described at the time as "leopards," though they may well have been lions. A few years later, Louis IX of France gifted the king an elephant, which became quite the London attraction before it died after only a few years.

By the 17th century, the collection had grown to include eagles, pumas, tigers, jackals, lions, and leopards. The menagerie even inspired one of history's earliest April Fools' jokes — in 1686, visitors were reportedly sent to the Tower to witness the (entirely fictional) annual "washing of the lions" ceremony. The animals got the last laugh, though: in 1831, King William IV ordered the menagerie moved to Regent's Park, where many joined the newly opened London Zoo.

Fun Fact

The Tower is said to be one of the most haunted places in Britain, but not everyone who "haunts" its corridors is human. During the 16th and 17th centuries, prisoners were sometimes subjected to torture — and the Tower's infamous rack could apparently creak loudly enough on its own. Staff have reported hearing unexplained noises, and some longtime Yeomen Warders (the Tower's famous Beefeaters) have sworn they've seen shadowy figures in Tudor clothing drifting through the White Tower at dusk.

The Enduring Mystery of the Princes

Even today, the Tower guards one of history's greatest unsolved mysteries: the disappearance of the Princes in the Tower. When young Edward V was declared king in 1483 at age 12, his uncle Richard took the throne for himself. The boy and his younger brother were lodged in the Tower — and then vanished. In 1674, workmen discovered two small skeletons beneath a staircase, and many believe those bones may belong to the lost princes. The question of what truly happened to them has never been answered, leaving the Tower with one secret it refuses to give up.
