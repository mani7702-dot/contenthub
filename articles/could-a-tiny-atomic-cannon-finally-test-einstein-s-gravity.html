Could a Tiny 'Atomic Cannon' Finally Test Einstein's Gravity?

2026-11-014 min

For more than a century, physicists have watched ordinary objects fall at exactly the same rate. But nobody has ever tested whether the heavier particles of nature obey the same rule. Now scientists in Switzerland have built a laser-straight beam of exotic atoms — nicknamed an 'atomic cannon' — to find out.

Drop a feather and a hammer together on the Moon, and — if there is no air in the way — they land at the same instant. That strange fairness of gravity has been tested for centuries. But there is one big family of particles nobody has ever checked, and a team in Switzerland has just built a clever machine to try.

A Rule That Has Never Been Broken

When air resistance is left out, ordinary objects fall at the same rate no matter what they are made of or how heavy they are. Galileo noticed this long ago, and it became a cornerstone of Albert Einstein's theory of gravity. It is called the universality of free fall.

So far, scientists have only tested the rule with the light, familiar particles of matter — like the atoms in our bodies — or their first-generation antimatter cousins. Nobody has ever measured how the heavier generations of particles respond to gravity.

Meet Muonium, the Odd Little Atom

Nature comes in three families of particles. The electrons and quarks that build everyday things belong to the first. The second generation includes the muon, a chunky cousin of the electron. Physicists still do not fully understand why these heavier generations exist at all — or why there are three of them.

A muon is tricky to study because it is charged, and electric fields would swamp the whisper-soft tug of gravity. The fix is to pair a positively charged antimuon with a negatively charged electron. Their charges cancel out, making a neutral atom called muonium. Most of its mass comes from the heavy antimuon. Best of all, being neutral, it can simply fall.

Using Liquid Helium as an 'Atomic Cannon'

The team, from ETH Zurich and the Paul Scherrer Institute, had two problems. Muons vanish after an average of just 2.2 microseconds, and older muonium sources spat atoms out in every direction at wildly different speeds.

Their solution is elegant. They cooled superfluid helium to near absolute zero — about minus 273 °C (-459 °F). In this strange quantum fluid, antimuons glide in, grab an electron, and become muonium. Because the atoms would rather not stay inside the helium, they get flung straight up off the surface — "so we're using the chemical potential as an atomic cannon," explains lead author Jesse Zhang. The result is a tightly bunched, near-parallel beam, slow enough and orderly enough to measure.

Why It Matters

If the heavier particles fall exactly as Einstein predicts, the theory gets one more powerful, long-awaited check in a place it has never been tested. If they fall differently — even by a hair — it would be a genuine surprise and could open a window onto entirely new physics. As physics professor Anna Soter puts it, the team has "taken an important step toward carrying out an exciting experiment." The cannon is loaded; next comes the drop.

Fun Fact

Muonium is a strange case: it behaves a little like hydrogen, the simplest element, but its "nucleus" is not a proton at all — it is an antimuon, a particle that does not normally survive longer than a few millionths of a second. It is a real atom and an unstable one at the same time.
