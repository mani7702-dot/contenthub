How did NASA's Artemis I fly past the Moon with less radiation danger than expected?

2026-10-284 min

NASA's uncrewed Artemis I rocket zipped around the Moon in late 2022 carrying tiny crystal detectors inside human-shaped dummies — and surprisingly, the cosmic radiation near the Moon, while stronger than in Earth orbit, is not nearly as dramatic a jump as scientists first thought.

How did NASA's Artemis I fly past the Moon with less radiation danger than expected?

A daring test flight around the Moon

In late November and early December 2022, NASA's Artemis I rocket thundered off the launch pad on an uncrewed test flight — a giant rehearsal for the day humans return to the Moon. The spacecraft, called Orion, looped around the Moon and sped back to Earth, covering more than a million kilometers through a region of space where no human has traveled since the Apollo missions ended.

But this wasn't just a routine trip. Tucked inside Orion were more than a dozen tiny, crystal-clear detectors — along with two human-shaped mannequins named Helga and Zohar. Together, they formed the MARE experiment (Matroshka AstroRad Radiation Experiment), a collaboration between NASA, Germany's DLR, and Israel's space agency, quietly measuring every particle of radiation that brushed past the spacecraft on its journey.

Crystal detectors that work like photographic film

The key players were lithium fluoride crystals developed by scientists at the Institute of Nuclear Physics of the Polish Academy of Sciences (IFJ PAN) in Cracow. Unlike bulky traditional detectors, these crystals are just a few millimeters across — small enough to wear like badges or tape onto the mannequins' bodies.

They work a bit like photographic film: when cosmic particles pass through, they leave invisible "color centers" in the crystal. Later, scientists shine a blue light on the crystals, and the damaged spots glow red under a microscope — revealing the path of each particle, one by one.

The big advantage? No messy chemical etching required. Older plastic detectors had to be bathed in acid to reveal their tracks. With lithium fluoride, researchers can examine tracks directly under a regular fluorescence microscope, making the data faster and clearer to read.

Near the Moon: more radiation, but not as scary as expected

Back on Earth, scientists compared the MARE readings from near the Moon with data from an earlier experiment aboard the International Space Station. What they found was surprising.

In low Earth orbit, the Van Allen radiation belts — the doughnut-shaped zones of trapped particles around Earth — are the main source of radiation astronauts encounter. Those low-energy protons bathe astronauts unevenly, hitting some body parts more than others.

Near the Moon, conditions are very different. The Van Allen belts do not extend that far, so galactic radiation — high-energy particles from deep space — becomes dominant. These particles carry enough energy to pass straight through the human body, depositing their energy evenly across the whole torso.

That means radiation doses near the Moon are indeed higher than in Earth orbit. But the difference is not as large as many people assume. The low-energy protons that contribute so heavily in Earth orbit simply are not present near the Moon — and without them, the overall jump in total dose is manageable.

What this means for the next Moonwalkers

These findings matter a lot for the astronauts who will walk on the Moon again. The compact crystal detectors proved themselves capable of recording radiation events that older technology could not even detect. That gives mission planners a clearer picture of what to expect — and how to best shield future crews.

For the Artemis III and IV missions — which NASA now targets for the mid-2020s through 2028 — knowing the exact radiation environment near the Moon helps engineers design better protective clothing and spacecraft hulls. The MARE experiment showed that, with proper planning and the right detectors, the trip to the Moon can be far less of a radiation lottery than it once seemed.

Fun Fact

The MARE mannequins — Helga (shielded) and Zohar (unshielded) — were built to match the body mass and shapes of a male and female astronaut. They wore actual flight suits, helmets, and even had realistic bone density, so the radiation readings would match what real astronauts would experience.
