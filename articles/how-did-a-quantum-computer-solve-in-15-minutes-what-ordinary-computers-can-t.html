How Did a Quantum Computer Solve in 15 Minutes What Ordinary Computers Can't?

2026-11-014 min

A new kind of computer just finished a puzzle in about 15 minutes that would take the world's best ordinary computers an impossibly long time — and for the first time it could also prove its answer was right. IBM and the University of Chicago call it a milestone in 'quantum advantage.'

A puzzle built to be almost impossible

Imagine asking a friend to describe a pattern of billions of flickering lights — so complicated that no ordinary computer could ever work out how it was made. That is the idea behind a famous test called random circuit sampling, which scientists have long used to see whether a quantum computer can do something a regular one cannot.

The trouble is, once a puzzle gets that hard, it also becomes hard to check whether the answer is even correct. You can be fast and wrong.

So a team from IBM and the University of Chicago built a cleverer version of the test. Their design keeps the puzzle just as tough for ordinary computers, but adds a hidden structure that lets errors be spotted while the machine is running. Their experiment is described in a paper called "Sampling hard circuits with verifiably high fidelity."

70 tiny "qubits" working together

The heart of the machine is the qubit — the quantum version of a computer's basic on/off switch, but one that can be in a strange blend of both states at once. Qubits are powerful yet fragile; the slightest noise can scramble them.

To protect the calculation, the team encoded 70 logical qubits — qubits wrapped in a protective layer, like a message written inside a sealed envelope. With them, the computer carried out 2,415 two-qubit operations and 468 special "T gates," two measures of how intricate the puzzle was.

The protection paid off. Errors during the run were about 10 times rarer than the machine's raw hardware would normally allow.

A finish line crossed in minutes

When the researchers tried to reproduce the result the ordinary way, the best classical methods would have needed an impractical amount of time. The quantum computer finished in roughly 15 minutes.

"We are now firmly in the quantum advantage era," said Jay Gambetta, director of IBM Research. University of Chicago professor Bill Fefferman, whose group helped design the verification, said checking the answer has long been "one of the biggest challenges" in the field — and this experiment makes that check far more trustworthy.

Fun fact: Ordinary computer bits are like light switches, firmly on or off. A qubit is more like a spinning coin — until you look, it is a blur of possibilities, and that blur is exactly what lets it explore many answers at once.

Why it matters

Speed alone is not enough — a computer must also be believable. By pairing a huge quantum calculation with a way to prove it was done correctly, this result points toward machines we can trust as they grow, tackling problems in medicine, materials and energy that are out of reach today.
