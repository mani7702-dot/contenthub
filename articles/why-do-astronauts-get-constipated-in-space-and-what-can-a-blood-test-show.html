Why Do Astronauts Get Constipated in Space — and What Can a Blood Test Show?

2026-11-013 min

Blood samples from 52 astronauts hint that microgravity slows food through the gut, nudging bacteria to ferment protein instead of fiber.

Why Do Astronauts Get Constipated in Space — and What Can a Blood Test Show?

Constipation is an oddly common complaint for people living in orbit. Now, a study of blood samples from 52 astronauts offers a simple and surprising explanation — and it starts with how food moves through the body when gravity stops helping.

A Gut That Slows Down

On Earth, gravity helps pull food and waste through the intestines. In the weightless environment of the International Space Station, that assist disappears. Researchers from the University of Copenhagen, working with NASA, suspected that food might simply linger longer in the digestive tract.

To test the idea, they looked at metabolites — tiny molecules that circulate in the blood and act like a diary of what the body is doing. The samples came from astronauts on three different missions, collected over many years aboard the station.

Bacteria Switch Their Menu

The blood told a clear story. Within weeks of arriving in space, the astronauts' gut bacteria began fermenting protein more than usual, and the change lasted until they returned to Earth.

Protein fermentation happens when the bacteria in our intestines run low on fiber — their preferred fuel — and start breaking down protein instead. The slower food moves, the more time the microbes have to make that switch. That fits neatly with the constipation astronauts report.

Why It Matters Beyond the Bathroom

The products of protein fermentation aren't just a digestive nuisance. The researchers note that these compounds have been linked to kidney strain and to effects on mood and focus, traveling from the gut to the brain along the gut-brain axis.

That makes the finding more than a curiosity. As space agencies plan longer trips to the Moon and eventually Mars, crews will spend far more time away from Earth's gravity, so understanding — and possibly easing — these changes could matter a great deal.

The scientists suggest some practical counters: more dietary fiber, prebiotics, or treatments that keep the gut moving. The same idea might even help people on Earth who are confined to bed and face similar slowdowns.

Fun Fact

The astronauts' blood also revealed smaller, surprise signatures of fish and caffeine — a reminder that the molecules in our blood quietly record what we eat and drink long before we feel any effect.
