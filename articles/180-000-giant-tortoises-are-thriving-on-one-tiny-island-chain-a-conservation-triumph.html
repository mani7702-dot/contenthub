180,000 giant tortoises are thriving on one tiny island chain — a conservation triumph

On a ring of coral islands far out in the Indian Ocean, the slowest travelers on Earth are living their best lives. A new survey has counted nearly 183,000 Aldabra giant tortoises roaming free on Aldabra Atoll in the Seychelles — a number so big it almost doesn't fit on the page.

How many is 180,000, really?

Picture this: every single person living in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, suddenly turned into a giant tortoise. That is roughly how many of these gentle giants now shuffle across the atoll's grasslands, scrub, and mangrove swamps.

It is the first full update since scientists last counted about 100,000 in the late 1990s. The new study, published in the journal Ecology and Evolution, put the best estimate at 182,940 adult and sub-adult tortoises — and the number is holding steady.

From fewer than 1,000 to over 180,000

It was not always this way. In the 1800s, hunters visited Aldabra again and again, packing the islands' tortoises aboard ships to sell as fresh meat in far-off ports. By the 19th century, fewer than 1,000 Aldabra tortoises remained.

Then, starting in the late 1960s, researchers began protecting the atoll in earnest. In 1981, Aldabra became a strict nature reserve. In 1982, UNESCO named it a World Heritage Site. No more hunting. No more collecting. Just tortoises, doing what tortoises do — eating grass, taking naps in the shade, and slowly, steadily, multiplying.

"It is proof of what long-term commitment to conservation can achieve," said Dr. Frauke Fleischer-Dogley, CEO of the Seychelles Islands Foundation.

Why the count is so hard

Counting 180,000 tortoises is not as simple as it sounds. Aldabra's vegetation is thick and tangled — perfect for tortoises, terrible for people trying to spot every last one.

"Aldabra's dense vegetation makes counting tortoises genuinely difficult in places," explained Dr. Nancy Bunbury, a co-author of the study. "By modeling what we didn't see, as well as what we did, we've produced the most reliable baseline yet for this iconic Seychelles species."

That modeling skill matters more than ever, because the tortoises' future is not completely safe yet.

What still threatens the giants

Climate change is already making Aldabra hotter and drier, with longer and more frequent droughts predicted. Less shade and less food could make life harder for tortoises of all sizes.

There is another, sneakier problem: invasive mammals. Feral cats and black rats have slipped onto the atoll and now prey on tortoise eggs and baby hatchlings — the next generation of giants before they even get started.

Conservationists have a plan: remove the last of these invaders. If they succeed, the tortoises may finally grow without that hidden threat — giving them the best shot at facing a changing climate.

"The fantastic work done in the field" has already brought the population back from the edge, said Dr. Dave Hudson of the University of Exeter. Now the goal is to keep it climbing.

Fun Fact

Aldabra giant tortoises can live a very, very long time. One famous resident, Jonathan, living on the island of St. Helena, is the oldest verified tortoise in the world — and he is well over 190 years old. Another, Esmeralda, is 182. In other words, the tortoises alive today on Aldabra may literally outlive every single one of us reading this.

Try this at home

Tortoises are nature's ultimate slow-livers. They take the slow and steady approach to everything — eating, moving, even surviving. Next time you feel rushed, ask yourself: would a 180-year-old tortoise hurry? Probably not. Try taking three extra-slow breaths, like a tortoise moseying across a meadow, and notice how it changes your day.
