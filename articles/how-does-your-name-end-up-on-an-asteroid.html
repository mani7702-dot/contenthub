How Does Your Name End Up on an Asteroid?

2026-11-012 min

Two astronomers in Northern Ireland just learned that chunks of space roughly 11 km wide now carry their names. Here's how a rock drifting in the asteroid belt becomes a lasting honour.

Most of us will never set foot on another world. But a few lucky people get to send their name out into the solar system anyway — attached to a giant rock tumbling around the Sun.

That's exactly what just happened to two astronomers at Armagh Observatory and Planetarium in Northern Ireland. Dr Rok Nežič and Shane Kelly each learned that an asteroid now officially carries their name.

A rock called (20560) Nežič

Dr Nežič grew up in Slovenia. As a child in 1999, he had no idea that an asteroid discovered that same year in New Mexico would one day be named after him. It took nearly three decades for that to happen.

His asteroid, officially (20560) Nežič, is about 10.9 kilometres across — a big one, as named asteroids go. His colleague's is even larger, at 15.6 kilometres, and goes by (7050) Shanekelly.

"They're both actually quite large as far as these asteroids that are named after people tend to be," Nežič explained, "because most of the really large ones were discovered first."

How the naming actually works

Here's the surprising part: you can't name an asteroid after yourself. The International Astronomical Union, which keeps official records of the sky, allows asteroids to be named only by the people who discovered them — and only once the object's orbit is well understood.

Someone else has to nominate you. In Nežič's case, his PhD supervisor and a former observatory astronomer put his name forward. Kelly, who has spent 27 years recording the weather at the observatory, was nominated by its emeritus director.

Nežič's work focuses on comets studied through polarised light. Kelly opens a special white box called a Stevenson screen every day to read the thermometers inside — a task he has performed more times than any of his 17 predecessors.

A small place punching above its weight

There are now 29 asteroids connected to the Armagh institution, and the planetarium plans to celebrate with a new LED dome showing where those asteroids and their orbits sit in space.

Both men's rocks are drifting safely in the asteroid belt, "just going around the Sun, just like we are."

"It's a dream come true," Nežič said. "I'm not sure how to believe it myself."

Fun fact: Asteroid numbers aren't random — they're assigned in order of discovery. The "(20560)" in front of Nežič's name tells you it was the 20,560th object in that catalogue, discovered in 1999.
