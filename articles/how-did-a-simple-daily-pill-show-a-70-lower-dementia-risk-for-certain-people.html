How Did a Simple Daily Pill Show a 70% Lower Dementia Risk for Certain People?

2026-10-30T05:52:00+05:303 min

Scientists studying over 13,000 adults found that a common, inexpensive daily pill was linked to a 70% lower dementia risk — but only for people whose genes give them a particular trait. The surprise? It all comes down to platelets.

How Did a Simple Daily Pill Show a 70% Lower Dementia Risk for Certain People?

Imagine if one tiny pill — something you might already find in a first-aid kit — could be linked to a dramatically lower chance of dementia in some people. That is exactly what a team of scientists at Monash University in Australia set out to investigate, and their discovery is rewriting how we think about both this common medicine and the role our genes play in brain health.

1. A Giant Study With a Simple Question

The researchers tapped into data from the ASPREE trial — a landmark study that followed more than 13,500 older adults for years to see how low-dose aspirin affects health. Aspirin is one of the world's oldest and most widely used medicines, often taken daily to keep blood flowing smoothly. But scientists had noticed something puzzling: earlier trials found that, on average, aspirin did not seem to prevent dementia.

So the Monash team asked a sharper question: could aspirin help some people more than others, depending on their genes?

2. Platelets, Genes, and a 70% Surprise

To find out, the researchers grouped participants by their genetic scores for platelet count. Platelets are the tiny particles in your blood that help it clot when you get a cut — think of them as your body's internal band-aid system. Some people naturally have more, some have fewer, and that difference turns out to be written in their DNA.

Among the people in the highest 20% of platelet-related genetic scores, something remarkable happened: only 1% of those taking aspirin developed dementia, compared to 3.3% of those taking a placebo. That translates to roughly a 70% lower relative risk.

3. Why It Matters — and What to Watch

The findings, published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, suggest that a person's genetic makeup might one day help doctors decide who could benefit most from preventive treatments. Instead of a one-size-fits-all approach, future care could be tailored to your unique biology.

But there is an important catch: in the group that seemed to benefit most, aspirin users also faced a higher risk of serious bleeding (4.4% vs. 2.1%). This tells us that the same trait that may protect the brain can also thin the blood more than expected. It is a reminder that medicines work differently in different bodies, and that genetic testing could play a role in keeping the benefits while minimizing the risks.

Fun Fact

The ASPREE acronym stands for "Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly" — a name that sounds like a superhero comic, but a study that quietly followed thousands of volunteers across the United States and Australia, making it one of the largest investigations of its kind ever attempted.
