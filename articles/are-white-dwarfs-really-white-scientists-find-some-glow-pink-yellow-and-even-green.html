Are white dwarfs really white? Scientists find some glow pink, yellow, and even green

2026-10-244 min

White dwarfs are supposed to be white — but new research shows that leftover pieces of shattered planets in their atmospheres can make them glow pink, yellow, or even green. About 1 in 30 white dwarfs may look very different from their name.

Are White Dwarfs Really White? Some Glow Pink, Yellow, and Even Green

The stars that broke their name tag

When astronomers first discovered white dwarfs, they found something strange right away. These stars are the crushed, ultra-dense leftovers of Sun-like stars after they burn out and puff away their outer layers. But here is the puzzle: a white dwarf should glow only white — or blue-white if it is still hot — because it is a perfectly hot, smooth ball of dense material.

Yet a new study from the University of Warwick found that some white dwarfs are glowing pink, yellow, and even green. Out of 151 nearby white dwarfs they studied, five turned out to be oddly colored. That is about 1 in every 30 white dwarfs — which might not sound like a lot, but across the entire galaxy, that is millions of strangely colored stars hiding in plain sight.

What makes a white dwarf change its color?

The answer is planetary leftovers.

Stars are normally "perfect radiators" — scientists call them blackbodies — meaning their color depends only on how hot they are. Hotter stars glow blue, cooler ones glow red, and somewhere in the middle, they should look white. That is what the color of a star normally tells us.

But white dwarfs have such strong gravity that anything floating nearby gets pulled in and smashed into their atmosphere. When a white dwarf shreds a passing asteroid, comet, or even a small planet, the debris falls onto the star. Those leftover pieces — made of carbon, sodium, and other elements — tint the white dwarf's glow.

The Warwick team found that:

  • Carbon in the atmosphere makes a white dwarf glow pink or purple
  • Sodium-rich debris can make it look green
  • Cooler white dwarfs with hydrogen and helium gas tend to look cyan or blue

Try this at home

On a clear night, look up at the stars and see how some look bluer while others look redder. That is their surface temperature showing through. But imagine if you could sprinkle a pinch of glitter on a flashlight's lens — the beam color would change, even though the bulb is still the same. That is what is happening to these odd white dwarfs. Their "flashlight" is still burning hot, but leftover space junk has tinted their light.

Fun fact: Our own Sun will become a white dwarf someday. In about 5 billion years, the Sun will puff up into a red giant, then shed its outer layers into space. What is left will be a hot, dense white dwarf — and over the next 10 trillion years, it will slowly fade from white to red to black. It will never go supernova because it is not massive enough. That is the quiet ending most stars get.

For grown-ups: The study is led by Dr. Mark Hollands (University of Warwick, Department of Physics) and published in RAS Techniques and Instruments. The team modeled the atmospheres of 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs (65 light-years) and found five with color-causing contamination from accreted planetary debris. Dr. Boris Gänsicke, also at Warwick, notes that while 3.3% seems small, it translates to millions of stars across the galaxy. Wikipedia confirms that white dwarfs pack about 60% of the Sun's mass into an Earth-sized volume, making them incredibly dense — a teaspoon of white dwarf material would weigh as much as a ton on Earth. These stars radiate residual heat and cool over trillions of years, eventually becoming theoretical "black dwarfs" (though the universe is not yet old enough for any to exist).
