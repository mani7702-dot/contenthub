How Did Scientists Date 128,000-Year-Old Ice From Tibet?

2026-10-29T14:30:00+05:304 min

Ancient ice cores drilled from the Guliya Ice Cap in Tibet carry climate secrets stretching back more than 128,000 years — far older than scientists once believed. By studying radioactive isotopes, researchers built the first reliable timeline for these frozen archives.

How Did Scientists Date 128,000-Year-Old Ice From Tibet?

A Frozen Time Capsule on the Roof of the World

High in northwestern Tibet, the Guliya Ice Cap sits more than 20,000 feet above sea level — a gleaming white dome perched on the roof of the world. For decades, scientists thought the ice here told the story of just the last few thousand years. But new research reveals something extraordinary: some of those ice layers are over 128,000 years old, making them the oldest high-altitude ice cores ever recovered outside the polar regions.

The discovery rewrites what we thought we knew about how mountain glaciers survive ice ages — and it opens a priceless window into Earth's ancient climate.

Two Drilling Trips, One Surprising Match

The research team collected ice cores from the Guliya Plateau in two separate expeditions — first in 1992, then again in 2015, a span of 23 years. If the ice had been flowing or melting over that time, the layers should have shifted. But to everyone's amazement, both sets of cores told almost exactly the same story.

By measuring oxygen isotopes — tiny chemical fingerprints left in the ice that reveal past temperature and precipitation patterns — the scientists found strikingly similar records between the two drilling sites. That consistency means the ice has preserved a remarkably reliable and reproducible archive of climate history.

"It's a really critical finding, because outside of the polar regions, this is the only ice core we have from the mountaintops that goes back that far," said Lonnie Thompson, the lead author and a veteran ice-core researcher at Ohio State University.

Dating Ice with Cosmic Clocks

Dating ice that has stayed frozen for over 100,000 years is no simple task. Regular radiocarbon dating only works for about 50,000 years, so the team turned to a different approach: measuring radioactive isotopes of beryllium and chlorine that build up in the ice over time.

These isotopes decay at predictable rates, acting like slow-ticking clocks embedded in the ice. By checking how much of each isotope had accumulated, the researchers could calculate the ice's true age.

They also used a clever cross-check: detecting chemical signatures of the Leschamp Geomagnetic Excursion, a dramatic wobble in Earth's magnetic field that happened around 41,000 years ago. That event left its own unmistakable mark in the ice — a natural timestamp that confirmed their dating was on solid ground.

When they compared the ice data with oxygen isotope records from nearby Tibetan caves, everything lined up: the Guliya ice record extends well beyond 128,000 years into the previous ice age.

What the Ice Teaches Us About Tomorrow

Why does this matter beyond ancient history? The findings help answer a big question: did Tibet's massive ice sheets survive the last ice age, or did they melt away long ago?

Previous studies suggested the ice only reached back to the Holocene — the warm period that began about 12,000 years ago. But the new timeline shows some of that ice is far older, meaning these giant ice masses persisted through one of Earth's biggest climate transitions.

"If we only had Holocene-era ice in Tibet, that would mean that the big ice sheets from the last ice age didn't persist through the early Holocene," Thompson explained. "But evidence shows that it did."

That knowledge helps scientists understand how mountain ice responds to warming — a crucial insight as today's glaciers face their own rapid changes.

Fun Fact

The Guliya Ice Cap holds the record for the world's highest-dated ground: at over 20,000 feet above sea level, it is the highest place on Earth where scientists have recovered ice core samples with confirmed ages exceeding 100,000 years.
