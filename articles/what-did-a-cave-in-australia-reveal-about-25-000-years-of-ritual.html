What Did a Cave in Australia Reveal About 25,000 Years of Ritual?

2026-10-314 min

Deep inside a dark Australian cave, layer after layer of ancient ash tells an extraordinary story: for roughly 25,000 years, generations of people carried whole grasses underground and burned them in ceremonies of magic, healing and care.

What Did a Cave in Australia Reveal About 25,000 Years of Ritual?

Some places hold their history in stone. One cave in southeastern Australia held it in ash — twenty-five thousand years of it.

Cloggs Cave sits in the traditional lands of the GunaiKurnai, an Aboriginal nation recognised as the custodians of Gippsland and the southern Victorian Alps. Their oral traditions, along with accounts written down in the 1800s, describe caves in this region as places where respected spiritual practitioners called mulla-mullung carried out rituals involving magic, healing and cursing.

Now, science has found physical proof that people were doing exactly that — for an astonishing stretch of time.

Clues Too Small to See

The evidence isn't bones or tools. It's phytoliths: microscopic silica structures that plants build inside their tissues and that survive long after the plant itself has vanished.

Researchers from Australia, France and New Zealand examined thousands of these tiny plant "skeletons" from two excavation pits inside Cloggs Cave. Because no plants grow inside a lightless cave, any plant remains there must have been carried in — by people, or by animals like possums on their fur.

To be certain, the team focused on half-burnt phytoliths from ashy layers, which could only have arrived through human hands.

Seventy-Three Layers of Ash

What they found was remarkable: 73 thin layers of ash, laid down by people between roughly 4,400 and 1,600 years ago, dated using optically stimulated luminescence and carbon dating.

The plant crystals were unusually well preserved — even delicate hair cells survived. Grasses made up as much as 97 percent of the material, and they weren't a random mix. People had clearly chosen specific kinds of grasses from across the wider landscape, then spread them out in thin layers and burned them.

Buried among the layers was another surprise: a small standing stone, 28 centimetres tall, that had been deliberately placed inside the cave for ritual purposes about 2,000 years ago.

The work was requested by the GunaiKurnai Land and Waters Corporation, and GunaiKurnai community members helped design the study, conduct the fieldwork and interpret the findings.

Fun fact: Phytoliths are so tough that they can outlast almost everything else from a plant — which is why a 25,000-year-old campfire can still tell us exactly which grasses were gathered on the day it was lit.
