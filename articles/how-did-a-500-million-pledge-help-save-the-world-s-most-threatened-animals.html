How Did a $500 Million Pledge Help Save the World's Most Threatened Animals?

2026-10-273 min

At the United Nations biodiversity summit in Armenia, the Bezos Earth Fund joined forces with eight conservation groups to pledge more than $500 million toward saving the planet's most threatened plants and animals. A new Species Recovery Alliance will coordinate the money and efforts so that every endangered creature — from tiny frogs to majestic hornbills — gets the targeted help it needs.

How Did a $500 Million Pledge Help Save the World's Most Threatened Animals?

A new team to help the most at-risk

Imagine if you had a friend who was feeling very, very sick — so sick that they might disappear forever. Now imagine that instead of just building a big fence around them (which is what many conservation groups do), someone found money to give them exactly the special care they need to get better.

That is the idea behind a brand-new team called the Species Recovery Alliance. At a meeting of world leaders in Armenia (called COP17), the Bezos Earth Fund announced that they are joining hands with eight other conservation groups to put more than $500 million toward helping the most threatened animals and plants on Earth.

But why do they need a special recovery plan instead of just protecting more land? Well, some animals need very specific help. A sea turtle might need a clean beach to lay eggs. A snow leopard might need local people to help protect its prey. A tiny orchid might need just the right kind of soil. One-size-fits-all protection doesn't always work — the Alliance wants to give each species exactly what it needs.

What is being done, and how much?

The $500 million will be spent over five years. About one-third of that is brand-new money, and the rest builds on money that was already pledged. The plan started with something called the Phoenix Species Project, which began in August 2026 with $200 million to help 100 of the most critically endangered species across 30 countries.

The new Alliance says it will work with local conservation groups and communities — because saving a species means working with the people who live near them. The money will support things like breeding programs, habitat restoration, anti-poaching patrols, and research to understand exactly what each species needs to survive and thrive.

Success will be measured using special tools from the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) — the global authority on which species are at risk. Some animals recover quickly, while others (like certain slow-growing trees or tortoises) may take decades to bounce back.

The bigger picture: why this matters

Numbers from the Living Planet Report (published by the World Wide Fund for Nature) show that wildlife populations have fallen by about 68% on average since 1970. That means there are fewer than one out of every three animals left compared to 50 years ago. In some places, like parts of Africa, the loss is even worse — about 80% fewer animals.

Creating protected areas — like national parks — is one important way to help, but the new Alliance recognizes that simply drawing lines on a map is not enough. Some species are so small in number that they need hands-on, targeted care to pull back from the edge.

Try this at home

Build a "recovery center" for toy animals. Gather stuffed animals or toy figures that represent different species. Give each one a "care plan" — what food, habitat, and helpers each needs to thrive. Draw a map of a "protected area" on paper and decide which animals need extra help beyond just being in the safe zone.

Create a biodiversity garden. Plant native flowers, herbs, or trees in your neighborhood. Even a small patch of native plants can become a haven for birds, butterflies, and pollinators. Track what visits your garden over time — you are doing your own mini-species recovery project!

Fun Fact

The word "biodiversity" was coined in 1985 by a group of scientists who wanted a shorter way to say "biological diversity." But the idea of protecting all life on Earth is much older — many Indigenous cultures have lived in harmony with nature for thousands of years, understanding that every plant and animal has a role to play in a healthy world.

The Species Recovery Alliance announced at COP17 in Yerevan, Armenia, aims to direct over $500 million over five years toward targeted interventions for the world's most threatened species. This complements the $200 million Phoenix Species Project launched in August 2026 supporting 100 critically endangered species across 30 countries. The funding addresses Target 4 of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which calls for halting human-induced extinctions and aiding species recovery. The Living Planet Report (WWF, 2022) found a 68% average decline in monitored vertebrate populations since 1970, with Africa seeing an 80% decline. Success will be measured using IUCN indicators. The Alliance works with Re:wild, Rainforest Trust, Fondation Sgr, and five other organizations alongside the Bezos Earth Fund, coordinating investments through local conservation partners. About one-third of the $500 million is incremental new money.
