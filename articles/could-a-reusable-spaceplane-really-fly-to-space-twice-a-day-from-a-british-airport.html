Could a Reusable Spaceplane Really Fly to Space Twice a Day from a British Airport?

2026-10-313 min

Spaceport Cornwall and New Zealand firm Dawn Aerospace have signed an agreement to explore flying a reusable, runway-launched spaceplane from Newquay — a sleek rocket-powered craft that could one day reach the edge of space and be back on the ground in time to do it all again the same day.

What if going to space looked less like a towering rocket launch and more like a plane taking off down an ordinary runway? That is exactly the idea behind a new agreement between Spaceport Cornwall and a company called Dawn Aerospace, which are exploring whether a reusable spaceplane could one day operate regular flights from Cornwall Airport Newquay.

A plane that touches space

The vehicle at the heart of the plan is called Aurora. It is a rocket-powered spaceplane that takes off and lands on a normal runway, just like an airliner. Instead of carrying people, its job is to climb high above most of the atmosphere — up to about 62 miles (100 kilometres) — and give scientific experiments a taste of near-space conditions, including a couple of minutes of weightlessness.

Dawn Aerospace's Mk-II Aurora has already flown more than 60 times, and the company plans to begin flying the full-performance version in 2027. The craft is designed for a famously short turnaround: in as little as four hours it can be refuelled and checked over, which is what makes the idea of flying twice in a single day possible.

Why Cornwall?

Spaceport Cornwall, at Newquay, became the United Kingdom's first licensed spaceport in 2022. It sits on one of the country's longest runways, where ordinary aviation, aerospace testing and spaceflight can all share the same site. That horizontal, runway-based approach suits a spaceplane perfectly — nothing needs to be launched straight up from a pad.

For now, the two organisations have signed a memorandum of understanding. That is a promise to study the idea together rather than a firm order for flights. Their first task is to work through how such a service would run in practice, covering the technical, regulatory and operational questions that come with flying a rocket plane from a busy corner of southwest England.

The bigger picture

The appeal is simple: making access to the upper atmosphere as routine as catching a flight. If a spaceplane can be turned around in hours rather than months, researchers could run experiments far more often, and at a fraction of the fuss of a traditional launch.

There is a long road between a signed agreement and a departure board full of spaceflights, and spaceports often take years to move from plans to regular missions. But the ambition is a striking one — a future where reaching the edge of space is a short trip down a Cornish runway, twice before dinner.

Fun fact: Aurora is designed to fly faster than three times the speed of sound, yet it still lands on the same kind of runway a holiday jet uses.
