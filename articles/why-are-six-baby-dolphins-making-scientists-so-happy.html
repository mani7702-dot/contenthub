Why are six baby dolphins making scientists so happy?

2026-10-263 min

Six Irrawaddy dolphin calves were born in Cambodia's Mekong River this year, lifting the tiny population to about 118 — and not one dolphin has died in five years.

Why are six baby dolphins making scientists so happy?

A dolphin that lives in a river

Most dolphins live in the ocean, but one special kind — the Irrawaddy dolphin — swims in rivers too. In Cambodia, a small group of them lives in a stretch of the Mekong River about 120 kilometres long, in the country's northeast. These dolphins have round heads and a smiley-looking face, and people along the river think of them as sacred animals.

There is a problem, though: this group is very, very small. In the late 1990s there were about 200. Then, year after year, the number went down. By 2017 only about 89 were left.

Six new babies

Now there is good news. This year, scientists confirmed six new dolphin calves were born in the river — five in Kratie province and one in Stung Treng. The sixth baby was spotted at a famous dolphin pool called Kampi, and it pushed the estimated population up to about 118 dolphins.

Even better: the last time a dolphin died in this group was in 2021. That means almost five years with no known deaths — a huge change from the years when the group was shrinking.

Why are they coming back?

Dolphins can't recover on their own. People helped. The Cambodian government and conservation groups worked together to make the river safer:

  • Patrols on the river stopped illegal fishing, including electric fishing that stuns everything in the water.
  • Families living nearby were helped to earn money in ways other than heavy fishing, so they did not need to catch so many fish.
  • Scientists photograph each dolphin's fin — every fin is shaped a little differently — so they can tell exactly who is who and count the babies.

Six babies in one year is a record for a group this small. It is a sign the dolphins are still having young, and that they can bounce back if the river stays safe for them.

A cautious cheer

These dolphins are still in danger. Fishing nets, dams far upstream, low water in the dry season and pollution are all still threats. But 118 is the highest number in years, and each calf is a tiny, splashing piece of hope.
