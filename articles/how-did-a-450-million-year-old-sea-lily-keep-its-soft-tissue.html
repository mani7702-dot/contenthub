How Did a 450-Million-Year-Old Sea Lily Keep Its Soft Tissue?

2026-10-314 min

A fossil dug up decades ago and tucked away in a small museum has turned out to be a one-in-a-million find: a sea creature that lived 450 million years ago, preserved with its delicate soft parts still intact — the oldest such crinoid ever found.

How Did a 450-Million-Year-Old Sea Lily Keep Its Soft Tissue?

Long before the dinosaurs, before forests, before animals crawled onto dry land, the shallow seas were already bustling. Among the strangest residents were crinoids — relatives of starfish and sea urchins that looked like feathery flowers on stalks, waving their arms to catch food drifting past. Now, one remarkable crinoid has given scientists a rare window into that vanished world.

A Fossil That Should Not Exist

Crinoids are common fossils. Their hard skeletal plates survive for hundreds of millions of years, so paleontologists have millions of them to study. But the soft parts — the skin, the organs, the tiny fleshy structures the animals used to feed and move — almost always rot away within days of death. That is what makes this find so surprising. A fossil of Dendrocrinus simcoensis, unearthed in sediments more than 450 million years old, still preserves its tube feet: the delicate, finger-like extensions along its arms that crinoids use to trap food and pass it toward their mouths.

It is only the second crinoid ever found with soft tissue preserved, and at more than 450 million years old, it is the oldest known example.

Caught in the Perfect Moment

For soft tissue to fossilize, decay has to be interrupted almost instantly. Oxygen levels, sediment chemistry, burial speed and microbial activity all have to line up before scavengers and bacteria destroy the remains. "Preservation like this is truly one in a million," said Dr. Lena Cole, a paleontologist at the University of Oklahoma. "Crinoid fossils number in the millions, and this is only the second time soft tissues have ever been found."

A Hidden Treasure Already in a Drawer

Perhaps the most charming detail is that nobody had to dig it up. The fossil had already been collected and stored at the Musée de paléontologie et de l'évolution in Montréal, a small institution supported entirely by community donations. Its importance only became clear when Cole and her colleague Dr. David Wright — both crinoid specialists — examined it during a research visit. Their expert eyes spotted structures that could easily have been mistaken for scratches in the surrounding rock.

Comparing the ancient tube feet with those of living crinoids revealed a noticeably different arrangement, hinting that early crinoids fed in ways with no modern equivalent. The work was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

Fun fact: These soft tissues are more than 200 million years older than the oldest dinosaur — meaning this little sea lily was already ancient history by the time the first dinosaurs hatched.
