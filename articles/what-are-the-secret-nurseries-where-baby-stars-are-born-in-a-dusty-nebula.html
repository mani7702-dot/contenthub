What are the secret nurseries where baby stars are born in a dusty nebula?

2026-10-213 min

The James Webb Space Telescope has revealed a cauldron of hidden baby stars in NGC 7129, a dusty stellar nursery 3,300 light-years away where newborn suns blast out jets of gas like cosmic firehoses.

A dusty rosebud hiding newborn stars

If you point a small telescope at the constellation Cepheus on a clear autumn night, you will not see much — just a faint smudge where the Flower Bud Nebula, formally known as NGC 7129, hangs in space. But that smudge is hiding something spectacular: dozens of baby stars, no more than a million years old, still buried inside thick blankets of cold gas and dust.

The James Webb Space Telescope can see right through that dust. Webb sees in infrared light, which travels easily through dark clouds the way a flashlight beam cuts through morning fog. What Webb revealed is a cosmic rosebud — a bubble blown by newborn stars, its walls glowing rosy pink where dust grains are heated by the intense light of the young cluster at its heart.

Why do baby stars shoot out jets?

As gravity pulls clumps of gas tighter and tighter, the center spins faster and faster — like an ice skater pulling in their arms. The core keeps collapsing until, at last, it becomes a protostar: a glowing ball of hot gas that is not quite a star yet.

Around the protostar, leftover material flattens into a spinning disk. Magnetic fields loop through the disk and, near the baby star, channel some of that material into narrow jets that shoot out along the star's rotation axis — like a cosmic firehose pointing in two opposite directions.

These jets race through space at hundreds of thousands of miles per hour. When they slam into the slow, dusty gas around them, they crash, heat up, and light up — which is exactly what Webb's infrared eye caught: sharp streaks of red, white, and gold, some pointing toward us, others arcing away into the dark.

It is how our own Sun probably began, 4.6 billion years ago.

Try this at home

On a dark night, find the North Star and look nearby for the faint, hazy patch of Cepheus. With a pair of binoculars, scan slowly — you are hunting a stellar nursery where hundreds of suns are still gathering their first bites of gas. No telescope can show you the jets directly, but knowing they are there, shooting across the darkness, makes every pinprick of light feel like a hidden story.

Fun fact: The young stars at NGC 7129 have already cleared hollow bubbles around themselves in the dust. Their jets heat molecules of carbon monoxide, which then glow in infrared — painting the nebula with the complex, lace-like patterns Webb captured.
