What Happens When Two Dead Stars Collide and Make a Brand-New Magnetar?

2026-10-314 min

When two dead stars crashed together more than six billion years ago, they flashed with X-rays for nearly ten minutes — far longer than usual. Astronomers think the collision may have left behind a newborn magnetar, one of the strangest objects in the cosmos.

What Happens When Two Dead Stars Collide and Make a Brand-New Magnetar?

Somewhere in the sky, long before our Sun even existed, two dead stars spiralled into each other and exploded. Their light finally reached Earth in July 2025 — and it carried a puzzle that astronomers are only now beginning to understand.

A flash that refused to fade

When big stars die, they can leave behind neutron stars — tiny, incredibly dense balls of matter. A single teaspoon of one would weigh billions of tonnes. When two of these orbits close enough, they collide, shaking space itself with gravitational waves.

Normally, such a crash announces itself with a "short gamma-ray burst" — a blast of energy that vanishes in less than two seconds. Blink at the wrong moment and you'd miss it entirely.

But this event, called EP250704a, did something unusual. Its gamma-ray flash lasted only half a second, and then something strange happened: instead of disappearing, the object kept glowing in X-rays for nearly ten minutes. That makes it the longest-lasting X-ray flash ever recorded from a neutron star merger.

Meet the magnetar

So what kept the lights on? The team's best explanation is a magnetar — a neutron star with a magnetic field trillions of times stronger than Earth's. Magnetars spin fast and carry enormous magnetic power. As they shed that power into their surroundings, they can make an explosion shine brighter and last longer than it otherwise would.

"It could keep bursting for longer," explained Professor Eleonora Troja, one of the study's co-corresponding authors, who works with the Einstein Probe collaboration. When she first saw the X-ray data, she knew something was up.

Astronomers spotted the event thanks to the Einstein Probe, a satellite launched in January 2024 that scans wide swathes of the sky for soft X-rays. That wide view is what let it catch the glow that narrower telescopes would have missed.

How scientists cracked the case

The researchers raced to point some of the world's biggest instruments at the fading signal — including the Very Large Telescope in Chile. Graduate student Niccolò Passaleva led the follow-up work. "I was travelling home by train," he recalled, "and all of a sudden I was rushing against time to commandeer one of the largest telescopes in the world from my laptop."

By splitting the light into its component colours, the team measured the event's redshift and worked out its distance. The explosion happened more than six billion years ago — its light travelled all that way before finally reaching us.

They also checked for a supernova, the bright explosion a dying massive star would leave behind. There was none. Distance, missing supernova and the burst's behaviour all pointed to the same answer: two dead stars had merged and, quite possibly, built a magnetar.

Fun fact: a magnetar's magnetic field is so powerful that, if you could stand a few hundred kilometres from one, it would rearrange the atoms in your body — thankfully, the nearest known magnetar is thousands of light-years away.
