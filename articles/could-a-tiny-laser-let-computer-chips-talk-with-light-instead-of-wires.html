Could a Tiny Laser Let Computer Chips Talk With Light Instead of Wires?

2026-11-013 min

Inside today's computers, information still travels as electricity — which makes heat and slows things down. Engineers in Denmark have built a laser so small that thousands could sit on a single chip and swap signals for beams of light.

1. A chip that talks in light

Right now, the internet already races along as pulses of light inside fibre-optic cables. But open up the computer on your desk and the picture changes: inside the chip, information still moves as electricity through tiny metal wires. That works, but it wastes energy as heat and puts a ceiling on how fast data can travel.

Researchers at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) have built a nanolaser — an extremely small laser that could one day let chips carry information with photons, the particles that make up light, instead of electrons.

2. Small enough to fit thousands

The trick is size. Ordinary lasers are far too bulky to sit inside a microchip by the thousand, but the DTU device is designed to be embedded by the thousands on a single chip. At its heart is a tiny structure called a nanocavity that squeezes light into a space once thought impossibly small. When a beam of light hits it, both light and electrons crowd into the same microscopic spot, letting the laser work at room temperature while using very little power.

The nanocavity's shape was originally designed by Professor Ole Sigmund's group at DTU Construct, and the laser itself was built in the university's cleanroom, DTU Nanolab. The team, led by Professor Jesper Mørk, describes the device in the journal Science Advances.

3. Why it matters

Mørk estimates that swapping electrical signals for light-based ones inside computers could cut energy use by as much as half. That would be good news for everything from laptops to the giant data centres that power the internet, which use enormous amounts of electricity and need constant cooling.

There is a catch: today the nanolaser is powered by shining a light on it, and the next big step is getting it to run on electricity. The team thinks that challenge could be solved within five to ten years.

Fun fact

Much of the internet already travels as light — the change would just bring that same trick indoors, onto the chip itself. Think of it as upgrading the wiring inside a house to the fibre-optic speed of the street outside.
