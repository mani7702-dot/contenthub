What giant-clawed 'unconquered warrior' dinosaur hid in a museum drawer for 100 years?

2026-10-233 min

A fossil skull fragment discovered on England's Isle of Wight over 100 years ago has finally been recognized as a brand-new species of feathered dinosaur — Invictibellatrix amberina, a small but fierce predator with curved claws up to 20 cm long.

What giant-clawed "unconquered warrior" dinosaur hid in a museum drawer for 100 years?

A fossil that waited a century to be seen

In 1924, amateur fossil hunter Reginald Walter Hooley walked the cliffs of the Isle of Wight — a spit of land off England's south coast that is famous for its dinosaur bones. He picked up a strange piece of skull, about the size of a large book, and handed it over to the Natural History Museum in London.

For nearly 100 years, that skull fragment sat in a museum drawer, unnoticed. Paleontologists passed it by, never realising that it belonged to a dinosaur species nobody had ever seen before.

That changed in 2026, when a team led by University College London finally gave it a closer look — and discovered a creature unlike anything found in Europe.

The naming of a warrior

The dinosaur's new scientific name is Invictibellatrix amberina. Let us break that down:

  • Invictibellatrix means "unconquered female warrior" — a tribute to how megaraptoran dinosaurs grew to become mighty apex predators.
  • amberina honours both the golden amber found in the rock layers where the fossil was discovered, and the name Amberina.

The research team chose the warrior name deliberately. In their paper, they wrote that it gives "hope and strength to all those who are bullied, discriminated, victimized, and oppressed" — a reminder that even small creatures can grow up to be heroes.

Built for hunting, not just hiding

Based on its closest relatives, scientists believe Invictibellatrix was about three to four meters long — that is roughly as long as a small car. It stood just over a meter tall at the hip, about as tall as a human child.

Unlike the famous T. rex, which crushed prey with its massive jaws, Invictibellatrix belonged to a group called megaraptorans — dinosaurs built more like agile hunters:

  • Powerful arms with giant curved claws up to 20 cm (8 inches) long
  • Feathers covering its body (like most dinosaurs discovered today)
  • Slender skull, perfect for snapping at small prey

It probably ate baby dinosaurs, lizards, and small mammals — filling the same role that hyenas do in today's world: a smart, scrappy hunter that cleans up leftovers and keeps the ecosystem balanced.

Solving a 120-million-year-old mystery

Using CT scans (the same kind doctors use, but for fossils), the team created a 3D model of the skull. The scans revealed unique features — including a special oval opening where the spinal cord met the brain, and an hourglass-shaped crest — that proved this was a brand-new species.

Even better, Invictibellatrix is the first megaraptoran ever found in Europe. For years, scientists thought these dinosaurs originated in Asia, then travelled west through Europe to reach the southern continents. This fossil finally proves that theory was correct.

Try this at home

Make a dinosaur claw tool. Using cardboard or thick paper, trace and cut out a claw shape about 20 cm long (roughly the length of a ruler). Attach it to a stick or dowel with tape — that is your dinosaur's weapon! Now imagine you are Invictibellatrix: try picking up small objects, digging into a sandbox, or pointing at "prey" (toy animals or blocks). Remember — those claws were perfect for grabbing, not for big biting!

Fun Fact

The name Invictibellatrix is the first time a new dinosaur has been named specifically to encourage children facing bullying. The research team said the name sends the message: "Even the smallest creature can grow into something powerful, and tomorrow can belong to you."
