Why Can't Viking Museums Let Go of the Warrior Myth?

A picture most of us share

Close your eyes and picture a Viking. Chances are you see a tall, broad-shouldered man with a sword, maybe a beard and a modern-looking haircut. It is a vivid image — and according to new research, it is mostly a made-up one.

Julia Håkansson, a museum researcher at Lund University in Sweden, studied how three of Scandinavia's biggest national museums present the Viking Age to their millions of visitors. Her conclusion: the exhibitions know the real story is richer and far more varied, yet they keep returning to the familiar warrior image.

What the museums show

The three museums — in Copenhagen, Stockholm and Oslo — draw on spectacular finds: ships, swords, jewellery and goods carried along trade routes that stretched across Europe and beyond. These objects are genuine and thrilling. The puzzle is in the framing around them.

Håkansson found the male Viking appears again and again as a warrior, a seafarer and a merchant. He is also shown as a farmer, but that quieter, everyday life tends to sit in the background. One Swedish display even notes that many free men and women owned weapons, even though only a few truly saw themselves as warriors.

Women get a similar treatment. Rather than ordinary lives, the galleries tend to spotlight exceptional figures such as noblewomen or shieldmaidens, while everyday women stay almost invisible.

Why the myth sticks around

The answer reaches back to the 1800s, when many European nations were busy building a sense of shared identity. The Viking Age became a handy symbol for Scandinavia — bold, distinct and memorable. Since then, the image has travelled the world and become what visitors expect to see.

That leaves curators in a tricky spot. They want to give crowds the drama they came for, while also telling the truth about a diverse society of farmers, traders, settlers and craftworkers — most of whom never picked up a sword in battle.

Fun fact

One of the most famous Viking symbols is hiding in plain sight: Bluetooth technology is named after Harald Bluetooth, a 10th-century Danish king.

So who were the real Vikings?

They were farmers and fisherfolk, weavers and traders, families and travellers — a whole society, not just a raiding party. Museums are slowly widening the picture, and that is good news for anyone curious about the past.

The next time you meet a Viking in a gallery, it is worth asking: is this the whole story, or just the loudest one?
