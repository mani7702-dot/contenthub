How did a volcanic eruption 27 million years ago create a new U.S. national park?

2026-10-253 min

Chiricahua, known as the 'Land of Standing-Up Rocks,' is now America's 64th national park. The stunning rock spires formed from a volcanic eruption 27 million years ago and have been protected since 1924.

How did a volcanic eruption 27 million years ago create a new U.S. national park?

Close your eyes and picture a mountain made of hundreds of stone spikes — some tall as buildings, others shaped like teacups and castles. Now imagine that every spike was carved by ash, wind, and water over millions of years. That place exists, and this fall it became America's 64th national park!

1. The day a volcano changed everything

About 27 million years ago, a supervolcano in what is now Arizona erupted with a force unlike anything you can imagine. Thick, white-hot ash rained down from the sky, piling up in layers nearly 2,000 feet deep. When the ash cooled, it turned into a rock called rhyolitic tuff — softer than granite, but easy for water and wind to carve into shapes.

For millions of years, rain, freezing, and flowing water slowly chipped away at the rocky layers. What was left behind? Millions of tall stone needles — some slender as pencils, others as wide as houses. The Apache people who lived there named the place "Chiricahua," meaning "dark-colored running water" — for the creeks that still flow after storms.

2. From monument to national park

Long before it was a national park, Chiricahua was protected as a national monument. Back in 1924 — when cars were brand new and women had just won the right to vote — President Calvin Coolidge used the Antiquities Act to set it aside "to protect the area's extensive hoodoos and balancing rocks."

For over 100 years, families have driven the 8-mile scenic road, hiked winding trails, and camped beneath dark night skies. Then in 2020, a group of students, park rangers, and Apache elders asked: Why isn't this a national park?

They worked for years. In 2023, laws were introduced in Congress. In 2026 — almost exactly 102 years after it first became protected — both the House and Senate agreed, and Chiricahua officially became the 64th national park in the United States.

3. What makes the rocks so special?

The rocks here look like something from another planet — or another world in a fairy tale! Hikers walk through the "Wonderland of Rocks", a maze where stone spires lean at funny angles and natural bridges arch overhead like frozen rainbows.

Scientists call these formations hoodoos — tall, thin spires that form when harder rock sits on top of softer rock that washes away below. Each hoodoo tells a story. Some look like mushrooms, others like giants standing in formation.

Today, visitors can ride a free shuttle to trailheads, walk the Echo Canyon path, and look up at the towering rocks from Massai Point. Rangers say the best time to visit is spring — when wildflowers bloom between the stones like nature's own fairy lights.

Try this at home

Collect some clay or play-dough and roll it into a big lump. Then poke thin straws — or rolled-up paper — straight up into the clay. Now sprinkle a little water on top and wait 10 minutes. The "ash" washes away between the straws, leaving rock-like pillars behind. That's how the real rocks at Chiricahua began — one layer at a time!

Fun Fact

Chiricahua National Park sits right where two deserts meet: the Sonoran Desert (hot, with saguaro cacti) and the Chihuahuan Desert (cooler, with yucca plants). That's why animals like javelina, coatimundis, and elegant trogons all live there — it's the only place on Earth where you can find both desert families hanging out together!
