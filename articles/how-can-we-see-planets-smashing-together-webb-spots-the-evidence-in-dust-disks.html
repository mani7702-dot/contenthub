How Can We See Planets Smashing Together? Webb Spots the Evidence in Dust Disks

1. The Epic Collision That Made Our Moon

About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized space rock named Theia crashed into the early Earth. The collision was so powerful that debris was flung into orbit around our planet. That debris eventually came together to form the Moon — the only place humans have walked beyond Earth so far.

But how do scientists study collisions that happened billions of years ago? They look for younger solar systems where similar crashes are happening right now.

2. What Webb Sees Around Other Stars

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope is watching stars that still have leftover disks of dust and rock around them. Some of these disks are "extreme" — they glow with so much warm dust that they tell a story of recent, violent collisions.

A research team led by Kate Su from the Space Science Institute studied 21 of these extreme debris disks using Webb. About one-third of them contain silica-rich dust — the same kind of material you'd expect from a high-energy crash between Mars-sized worlds. The other two-thirds show signs of smaller, gentler impacts.

3. Reading the Dust Like a Cosmic Fingerprint

When planets smash together, the dust they produce has a unique signature. The researchers found that silica-rich disks (from big impacts) appear around very young stars — less than 300 million years old. Silica-poor disks (from smaller impacts) show up around stars of all ages.

"Before Webb, we had limited information," said Su. "Now we have enough data to truly understand these systems and what they tell us about planet formation."

This is the first time scientists have gathered enough of these systems to see the pattern clearly. Webb's infrared eyes can see the heat glow of the dust, revealing what kind of collision created it — whether it was a Mars-smasher or a Moon-sized graze.

Try it yourself: On a clear night, look for the Moon in the sky. Imagine the giant impact that created it — a Mars-sized object hitting Earth 4.5 billion years ago! You can also try making your own "planetary collision" demo: roll two different-sized balls of clay or Play-Doh together and see what shape the "debris" takes.

Fun Fact: Only about 1% of young stars show visible extreme debris disks. That means these violent collision phases are short-lived — most don't last long enough for us to catch them in the act!

For grown-ups: The research, published in The Astrophical Journal (Oct. 1, 2026), helps us understand how rocky planets like Earth form. The extreme debris disks act as cosmic fossils, preserving evidence of the final, violent assembly phase when planetary embryos collided and merged. This connects directly to the giant-impact hypothesis for Moon formation and the Late Heavy Bombardment period in our own solar system's history.
