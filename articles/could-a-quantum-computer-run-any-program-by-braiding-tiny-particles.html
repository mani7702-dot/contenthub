Could a Quantum Computer Run Any Program by Braiding Tiny Particles?

2026-11-014 min

A laptop can run almost any software you throw at it — but quantum computers have long been limited to a narrow set of tasks. Now physicists have braided and fused strange particles called anyons to unlock the full toolkit.

A computer that can run any program

Think about your laptop. You can use it to write a story, edit a photo, or play a game, because it speaks a general language that can handle almost anything. For years, quantum computers have been a bit different. They are dazzling at a few special jobs, but they could not yet run every kind of quantum program.

Now a team of scientists says it has taken a major step toward fixing that. Their secret ingredient is a family of odd particles called non-Abelian anyons.

Meet the anyons

Anyons are not like the everyday particles you learned about in school. They do not exist on their own in nature. Instead, researchers create them inside a quantum circuit, by entangling many ordinary quantum bits (qubits) so tightly that they behave like one new and unusual object.

Each anyon carries a hidden internal state. That state changes when one anyon is braided — looped around another. Because the exact order of those loops matters, the anyons can store and shuffle information in a way ordinary particles cannot.

Why braiding is not quite enough

Scientists first built these anyons on real hardware in 2024, but braiding alone could not perform every operation a universal quantum computer needs. To close the gap, the new work added a second trick called fusion — bringing two anyons together and measuring what results.

Working on a trapped-ion processor called H2, the team used 54 entangled qubits to build anyons based on a mathematical symmetry named S3 (the rotations and mirror flips that leave an equilateral triangle looking unchanged). By mixing braiding and fusion, they created a full set of tools that can, in principle, produce any quantum operation.

"It's creating little universes — alternative universes, but ones that reflect some of the properties of our own," said study co-author Ruben Verresen of the University of Chicago.

A shortcut around a costly chore

Quantum computers are famously fragile. Engineers usually protect them by spreading information across many qubits, then spend a big chunk of their machine making special "magic states" to fill in the missing operations. The anyon approach may avoid that expensive step altogether — a rare "dark horse" that could make future quantum machines both more flexible and more reliable.

Fun fact: An anyon is not a single particle you could hold, but an emergent one — a shared pattern of many qubits that acts like a brand-new particle with its own rules.
