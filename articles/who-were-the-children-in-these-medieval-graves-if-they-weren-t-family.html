Who Were the Children in These Medieval Graves — If They Weren't Family?

2026-10-313 min

Ancient DNA from 142 people in Scandinavia shows that adults and children buried side by side in the same medieval grave were usually not close relatives at all.

Who Were the Children in These Medieval Graves — If They Weren't Family?

A picture that looked obvious

Picture an old churchyard in Scandinavia. An adult and a small child lie side by side in the same grave. For generations, archaeologists looked at burials like this and made a natural guess: this must have been a parent and a child, laid to rest together.

New research suggests that guess was often wrong.

What the DNA revealed

Scientists at Stockholm University studied the genetic material of 142 people who lived during the late Viking Age and the Middle Ages. More than 60 of them were children and teenagers, found together in graves at three places in Sweden: Sigtuna near Stockholm, Västerhus in Jämtland, and Fjälkinge in Skåne.

By testing biological relationships directly, the team could finally check whether the people sharing a grave really belonged to the same family. The answer was surprising. Close relatives only rarely shared a grave — even in churchyards where the wider community was full of family connections.

"We often assume that adults and children sharing a grave were parents and children," says Maja Krzewińska, who led the study. "In most cases, that was not what we found."

So who chose who was buried with whom? Probably religion, friendship, community ties, or simply the circumstances of how and when people died.

The tale of Lady 56

One burial brought the numbers to life. At Västerhus, researchers reconstructed the family of a woman they call Lady 56. Her DNA linked her to her mother, father, brother, and two daughters — four generations of one real family.

Her grave also held something unusual: a scallop shell. In medieval Europe, that shell was the badge of pilgrims who walked to Santiago de Compostela in northwestern Spain, thousands of kilometres away. Lady 56 died before she turned 30, and the shell hints she may have made that enormous journey — and come home again.

Fun fact

Bones alone often can't tell scientists whether a very young child was a boy or a girl, because those physical differences haven't fully formed yet. DNA testing let the team fill in the blank — and showed that boys and girls were buried exactly where adult men and women were.
