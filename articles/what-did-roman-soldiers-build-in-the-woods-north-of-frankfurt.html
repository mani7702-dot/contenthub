What did Roman soldiers build in the woods north of Frankfurt?

2026-10-213 min

Archaeologists in Germany have uncovered an entire Roman military town — including a temple to the war god Mars — just inches below the forest floor near Frankfurt.

A temple hiding in the trees

North of Frankfurt, in a quiet forest, archaeologists have uncovered something extraordinary. Just inches below the forest floor, they found the remains of a Roman military town — including a whole temple to Mars, the Roman god of war.

The site is called Kapersburg, and it sits along a famous ancient border called the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes. This was no ordinary boundary — it was a 342-mile wall of forts, watchtowers, and gates that marked the very edge of the Roman Empire, stretching from the Rhine River all the way to the Danube.

Why dig here?

Since 2024, a team of state archaeologists and university scientists has been carefully lifting the soil. What they discovered is remarkable: thanks to the forest canopy protecting the ground for centuries, the conservation layers here are unrivaled. While farm fields above other Roman sites disturb and mix the earth, this spot stayed undisturbed — so archaeologists can read it like a storybook, page by page.

They uncovered the vicus — the name Romans gave to the civilian settlement that grew up beside every fort. Inside the vicus were living quarters, craft workshops, and at its center, a rectangular temple about 16 feet long. At its heart stood a smaller three-walled room where worship once happened. Inscribed stones mention Mars by name, and pottery fragments of Victoria — the winged goddess of victory — were found alongside.

Soldiers, gods, and time

The temple was built during the reign of Emperor Trajan or Hadrian, when the limes was at its mightiest. A unit called the Numerus Nidensium — about 160 soldiers plus 700 support staff — held the fort for 150 years. They kept watch through peace and conflict until, in the 3rd century, the empire could no longer hold its borders. The soldiers marched away, and the forest slowly reclaimed the town.

Today, those same trees protect a story that lasted half a millennium. The limes itself is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized in 1987 and expanded in 2005 and 2008 — a reminder that borders change, but the ground remembers.

Try this at home

Build a Roman fort model from cardboard boxes. Cut a gate, add watchtowers at each corner, and place a small "temple" in the center. Then ask: what would you leave inside for the next person to find centuries later?

Fun fact: Mars and Victoria were often worshipped together, especially by Roman soldiers — victory in battle was seen as Mars' gift, celebrated through Victoria's blessing.
