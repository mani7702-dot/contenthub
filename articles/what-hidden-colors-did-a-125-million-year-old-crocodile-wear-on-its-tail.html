What hidden colors did a 125-million-year-old crocodile wear on its tail?

2026-10-313 min

A 125-million-year-old crocodile fossil from Spain has revealed its secret stripes — hidden band patterns preserved as soft tissue that only show up under ultraviolet light, making it the oldest known crocodylomorph with evidence of coloration.

What hidden colors did a 125-million-year-old crocodile wear on its tail?

A fossil found in 1902 finally tells its story

Some of the most exciting discoveries happen when scientists take a second look. In a quarry in the Catalonia region of Spain, engineer and geologist Lluís Marià Vidal uncovered a small fossilized skeleton in 1902. For over a century it sat quietly in a museum collection, admired but not fully understood. Now, more than 100 years later, that fossil — a 125-million-year-old crocodile relative called Montsecosuchus depereti — has revealed secrets about its skin, its senses, and even its tail stripes.

Seeing with ultraviolet light

The breakthrough came when paleontologists Oscar Castillo-Visa and Jesús Serrano were cataloging fossils from the Montsec limestone beds. They decided to shine ultraviolet light on the old specimen, and suddenly details that had been invisible for decades began to glow.

"UV light allows us to see details that would otherwise remain completely hidden in the rock," explains Castillo-Visa.

Under the UV light, the team could make out not just bones but delicate soft-tissue remains — skin scales, sensory structures, and cartilage. Most remarkably, the fossil showed alternating light and dark bands running across the tail, which the researchers believe represent the crocodile's original color pattern.

Stripes that camouflaged a tiny hunter

The fossil, cataloged as MGB-512, belongs to a small crocodile relative about 50 centimeters (20 inches) long. Its tail bands may have served as disruptive camouflage — the same trick some animals use today to break up their outline so they blend into mottled light and shadow. If confirmed, Montsecosuchus would become the oldest known crocodylomorph with preserved evidence of coloration.

The patterns likely helped the ancient predator sneak through the shallow coastal lagoons and river channels of what is now northeastern Spain. Unlike today's bulky crocodiles, this slender hunter could dart between rocks and plants, using its camouflage to get close to unsuspecting prey.

What else UV revealed

Beyond the striking tail stripes, the ultraviolet examination found:

  • Epidermal scales of varying shapes and sizes across the body — the first detailed look at this species' skin
  • Sensory structures in small scales around the neck and limbs — early versions of the pressure-detecting organs modern crocs use to sense prey in water
  • Cartilaginous chest structures indicating an efficient respiratory system, showing that sophisticated breathing adaptations evolved far earlier than scientists previously realized
  • A missing tail fin — unlike modern crocodiles, the fossil shows Montsecosuchus lacked the high tail fin used for powerful swimming

A window into the age of dinosaurs

The fossil comes from the Early Cretaceous period, a time when flowering plants were just beginning to spread. The Montsec site has yielded more than 8,000 fossil specimens, including fish, insects, plants, and even the world's first known flowering plant, Montsechia.

The new study, published in the Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society, was led by researchers from the Institute of Paleontology, the Museum of Natural Sciences of Barcelona, and institutions in New Zealand and the United States.

Fun Fact

The fossil was found at La Pedrera de Meià, a site inside a UNESCO Global Geopark that has produced more than 8,000 fossils. This 125-million-year-old crocodile is just one of them — proof that even century-old museum drawers can still hold major discoveries.
