How does a bird that flies thousands of miles generate thrust on the way UP?

2026-10-213 min

The European nightjar migrates from Europe to Africa and back each year — yet its wings sacrifice speed for slow-flight agility, even pushing the bird forward on the upstroke. A trick once seen only in bats and insects.

Every year, a bird the size of your hand makes one of the longest journeys in the insect-eating world. It flies from the forests of Europe all the way to the savannas of southern Africa — and back again — covering thousands of miles without stopping for food. That bird is the European nightjar, and its wings hold a secret that could help design better drones.

1000 miles of night flying

The nightjar is built for a life between dusk and dawn. Its wings are long and broad, its flight slow and ghost-like. Scientists at Lund University in Sweden wanted to know: how does a bird that migrates thousands of kilometers handle the very different demands of long-distance travel versus hunting on the wing?

They put nightjars in a wind tunnel and filmed them at different speeds, measuring the air currents around each wingbeat. What they found challenges everything we thought we knew about efficient flight.

The wing that does two jobs at once

Most migratory birds have wings optimized for one thing: cutting through the air with minimal wasted energy over thousands of miles. Think of an albatross — long, narrow wings that glide for hours on end.

The nightjar is different. Its wings are built for slow, maneuverable flight — the kind you need to swoop down on moths and beetles at dusk. The broad wingtips help it turn sharply, hover briefly, and snatch insects mid-air. But there's a catch:

At higher speeds, those same broad wings generate lift less efficiently.

"This runs counter to the intuitive image of a long-distance migrant as a perfectly optimized flying machine," says Christoffer Johansson, the study's lead researcher. "Instead, the nightjar's wings appear to be a compromise between several different demands."

The upstroke surprise

But here is the really surprising part. While most birds tuck or fold their wings during the upstroke — the upward part of each wingbeat — the nightjar keeps pushing forward. Its wings generate thrust on the way up, just like bats and insects do.

This is unusual in the bird world. Birds and bats evolved flight completely independently, yet both settled on the same trick. The nightjar's wings can't fold very much during each beat, so the researchers think the upstroke thrust is an aerodynamic workaround.

What this means for robots

Engineers are already taking notes. Drones and flapping-wing robots face the same mechanical limits as birds — their wings can only fold so far during each beat. Learning how nightjars squeeze thrust out of every part of their wing motion could help build robots that fly farther, turn sharper, and use less energy.

In the meantime, the nightjar keeps doing what it has done for thousands of years: flying through the night, wings beating in a rhythm perfected by evolution, from one season to the next.

Try this at home

  • On your next bike ride, hold your hand out the window and tilt it at different angles. Feel how it catches the wind on the way up and down — that is a tiny version of what a nightjar's wing is doing.
  • Find a video of a nightjar in flight and watch its wingtips. Notice how they don't fold tight against the body during the upstroke like a pigeon's wings do.
  • Look up at dusk near a park or field and listen for the nightjar's distinctive "booming" call — it's their way of saying "I'm here" without seeing you at all.

Fun fact

The nightjar's wings aren't the only thing that makes it special at night. Its eyes are huge — the biggest of any bird relative to body size. And inside each eye is a special reflective layer called the tapetum lucidum that bounces light back through the retina, letting the bird see in near-total darkness. That's why their eyes seem to glow red when car headlights catch them.
