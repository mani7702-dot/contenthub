Why Has a 100-Year-Old Mushroom Mystery Suddenly Grown So Complicated?

ScienceContentHub2026-11-013 min

Every year, mushroom farms across the United States lose part of their harvest to a stubborn blemish. The white button mushrooms — the mild, familiar kind that crowns salads and pizzas — develop brown and yellow patches on their caps, and once that happens many of them can no longer be sold.

Farmers have fought this problem for more than 100 years, and for most of that time they had a tidy explanation: a single bacterium was to blame.

A new study from the University of Florida suggests that story was far too simple.

What did the scientists find?

Researchers at the University of Florida Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (UF/IFAS) studied mushrooms affected by the condition, called bacterial blotch, gathered from growing facilities across the southern United States.

They used DNA sequencing, chemical profiling and ordinary laboratory tests to work out which microbes were present. Rather than one villain, they found more than 17 species of harmful bacteria living alongside the damage — a whole community of suspects, not a lone offender.

Why does that matter?

If a disease comes from one germ, a treatment aimed at that germ should fix it. But when many different bacteria can cause the same blotches — and when the mushrooms grow in warm, humid indoor rooms that suit bacteria perfectly — a single-target spray may simply miss most of the problem.

That could explain something growers have long found baffling: treatments that seem to work on one farm, or one season, quietly fail on the next.

The blemishes add another wrinkle. Sometimes the mushrooms look perfectly white when they leave the farm, and the discolouration only shows up after they have reached a grocery store or a shopper's kitchen, making the losses hard to predict.

What happens next?

The team is already looking for gentler solutions. Follow-up work is testing plant-based essential oils, helpful microbes and other natural products as alternatives to conventional chemicals.

They also hope the findings lead to sharper diagnostic tools, so growers can spot the true culprits early and treat accordingly — saving more of each harvest while leaning on greener methods.

"There is more to this disease than we thought," the researchers explain, which is exactly the kind of surprise that moves a century-old puzzle closer to an answer.

Fun fact: A mushroom is not a plant — it is the fruiting body of a fungus, and it can grow in complete darkness, which is why many commercial mushroom farms skip sunlight altogether.
