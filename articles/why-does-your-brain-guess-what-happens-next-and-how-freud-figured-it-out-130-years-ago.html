Why does your brain guess what happens next — and how Freud figured it out 130 years ago?

2026-10-223 min

Your brain is constantly guessing what comes next, then checking if it was right. Modern neuroscience has a name for this — but psychoanalyst Sigmund Freud was describing the same thing over a century ago.

Why does your brain guess what happens next — and how Freud figured it out 130 years ago?

Your brain is a prediction machine

Close your eyes and picture your classroom. Do you see the walls, your desk, your backpack? Your brain just guessed what those things look like — even though your eyes were closed. That is because your brain is constantly playing a game: it predicts what will happen next, then checks itself against what really comes in through your senses.

This idea is one of the biggest in modern neuroscience today. Scientists call it the prediction paradigm — and believe it or not, a man named Sigmund Freud was talking about almost the exact same thing more than 130 years ago.

Two smart ways of seeing one brain

Neuroscientists use microscopes, brain scans, and math to study the brain. Psychoanalysts — the kind of therapists who listen and ask questions — study how people feel and think from the inside. For a long time, these two groups studied the same brain but seemed to speak completely different languages.

Now, researchers from the University of Oslo have found that both groups are describing the same thing — just from different seats in the classroom. Neuroscience sees the brain's biological machinery. Psychoanalysis sees the same process as a person's inner experience.

What Freud called "projection"

Freud noticed that people often guess things about others that do not match reality. If you have felt left out before, you might assume a new friend is being quiet because they are angry with you — even when they are just shy. Freud called this projection: letting past feelings shape today's guesses.

Modern brain science has a similar word: prediction. Your brain uses old experiences to build expectations. When those expectations are too strong, you might jump to the wrong conclusion — just like the projection Freud described.

Why it helps (and sometimes hurts)

The brain's prediction habit is usually a lifesaver. It lets you catch a ball without thinking, or duck before a door slams. But sometimes those predictions are wrong — especially when feelings are involved.

A brain that always expects trouble may have trouble making new friends. A brain that expects to be hurt might miss chances to be happy. The good news? Psychologists and neuroscientists now think both talking therapy and brain-based approaches can help rewire those old, sticky predictions — one new experience at a time.

Fun Fact

The word "psychoanalysis" comes from Freud's office couch — but Freud actually used a regular chair most of the time. The couch was borrowed from a friend, and patients only lay down because they fell asleep sitting up during long sessions!

Try this at home

Your brain loves to guess — so let's put that to a fun test! Have a grown-up help you hide a small toy under one of three coffee mugs, shuffle them around, then try to point to the right one. At first your brain will guess based on what it saw before — and that is exactly what scientists mean by prediction! Try it a few times and see how fast your "guessing brain" learns to be right.
