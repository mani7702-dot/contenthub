How removing rats and pigs gave a tiny island's wildlife a second chance

2026-10-233 min

On Nu'utele Island in Samoa, a team of conservationists, community members, and even a rugby star removed invasive rats and pigs — and within a year, native birds are nesting again, seedlings carpet the forest floor, and a land crab unseen for decades has returned.

How removing rats and pigs gave a tiny island's wildlife a second chance

Hidden in the blue-green waters of the South Pacific, a small island called Nu\u2019utele was in trouble. Rats and feral pigs had turned its forest floor upside down, and native birds had nowhere safe to nest. But in just one year after a determined team kicked those invaders out, the island is waking up again.

1. The invaders that hurt the island

Nu\u2019utele sits in Samoa\u2019s Aleipata peninsula, and its native forest is one of the most special places for wildlife in the whole country. But two tough visitors — rats and feral pigs — had made life hard for the island\u2019s native plants and animals.

Rats are clever and hungry. They sneak into bird nests on the ground and gobble up eggs and baby birds before anyone even knows. Pigs are even messier: they root through the soil looking for food, turning whole patches of forest floor into muddy wallows. That stops native seeds from sprouting and washes away the food that baby birds and other creatures depend on.

2. A team effort to remove them

Nothing good comes easy, and removing rats and pigs from an island is like organizing a very big, very careful hunt. More than 130 days of fieldwork were spread across 12 expeditions between March 2025 and May 2026.

The team brought together Samoa\u2019s Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment, the Pacific Regional Invasive Species Management Support Service, Island Conservation, the Secretariat of the Pacific Regional Environment Program, and neighbors from the nearby village of Aleipata. Even former New Zealand rugby star Glen Osborne joined the expert hunting teams to track down the last few pigs hiding in the thick forest.

3. Nature bounces back — fast

With the rats and pigs gone, the forest started healing almost right away. Conservationists say native seedlings are now carpetting the forest floor in many areas — tiny green signs that the trees are ready to grow back.

Birds are coming back, too:

  • The shy ground dove, rare across the Pacific, was spotted nesting just one month after removal.
  • The Samoan starling built a nest, and experts watched breeding activity from the endemic flycatcher and whistler too.
  • A land crab that had never been recorded on Nu\u2017utele before suddenly appeared at the island\u2019s edge — proof that the ocean and forest are talking to each other again.

In the coming months, the recovering forest will welcome an even special guest: the threatened Mao, a native forest bird that conservationists hope to move to the island soon.

Fun Fact

Islands are evolutionary playgrounds. Because they sit alone in the ocean, the plants and animals on them often have no natural enemies. That is why island birds evolved to nest on the ground — safe, until rats and pigs arrived. Removing invaders doesn\u2019 just help animals; it restarts millions of years of natural story.

Try this at home

You can build a mini island ecosystem in a jar! Grab a clear container, add a layer of small stones for drainage, then potting soil, then a few small native seeds or fast-sprouting beans. Water lightly and place near sunlight. Watch how the seeds sprout and grow — then try adding a small plastic "invasive" figurine (like a toy rat) and see how it might disturb the soil. Remove it, and watch nature bounce back!
