Could a Laser Beam Really Work as a Radio Antenna?

2026-10-313

Engineers have turned a laser beam into a working radio antenna that looks like a lightsaber — and it could change how spacecraft talk to Earth.

Radio antennas have one dependable rule: to send or catch a different frequency, you usually have to change the antenna's size. That means motors, hinges, or folding metal arms — heavy parts that are tricky to build and even trickier to pack into a rocket.

Now engineers at North Carolina State University have tried something completely different. Instead of shaping metal, they used a laser to carve an antenna out of thin air.

A beam of charged air

When a laser is focused with just the right power and width, it strips electrons from the atoms in a narrow column of air. The result is plasma — a gas full of electrically charged particles, often called the fourth state of matter alongside solids, liquids, and gases.

Because those particles carry charge, they can interact with radio waves in ways ordinary air cannot. The team's glowing column, called a plasma filament, became a working antenna. To feed it a signal without touching it, the researchers sent the laser through a metal ring that acts like a capacitor, passing energy to the plasma through an invisible electric field.

In their very first test, the beam successfully transmitted radio waves at 30 megahertz, inside a band used for everything from air traffic to FM radio.

Stretching a signal with a flick of a laser

The clever part is how adjustable it is. Want a longer antenna? Turn up the laser. Want to aim the signal in a new direction? Just tilt the beam. No moving metal parts required.

"By controlling the parameters of the laser, you can control the characteristics of the plasma filament — including its length," said Prya Darshni, the study's lead author.

Why spacecraft might love it

Satellites and probes face brutal limits on weight and bulk, and unfolding a giant antenna in orbit is a delicate dance. A laser-made antenna could one day be created and retuned on the spot. In low Earth orbit there is still enough thin air to form plasma, though the team stresses that this is only a first proof of concept.

Fun fact

Astronomers have spotted plasma antennas in nature too — the charged gas around stars can bend and reflect radio signals, which is one reason giant clouds of plasma make such useful cosmic "mirrors."
