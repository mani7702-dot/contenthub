How did phosphorus keep Earth's oxygen flowing after the Great Oxygen Event?

2026-10-224 min

About 2.3 billion years ago, Earth's atmosphere gained its first lasting breath of oxygen. But a mystery remained — how did oxygen stay abundant enough for life to thrive over millions of years? The answer lies in an ancient recycling system driven by phosphorus in the oceans.

A 2.3-billion-year-old question

About 2.3 billion years ago, Earth went through a life-changing moment. Oxygen — a gas that had been almost absent from the atmosphere — suddenly began building up in the skies. Plants and animals had not appeared yet, but the stage was being set for them.

This event, called the Great Oxidation Event, happened because tiny ocean creatures called cyanobacteria learned to photosynthesize — they breathed in sunlight and breathed out oxygen, just like plants do today. But here is the puzzle: once that oxygen appeared, how did it stick around for millions of years instead of disappearing again?

The ocean's recycling trick

Scientists studying ancient rocks from South Africa have found a surprising answer. As oxygen spread into the oceans, it changed the water's chemistry. More oxygen meant more sulfate — a type of salt — dissolved in the sea.

That sulfate let certain microbes break down dead plants and animals much more efficiently. When they finished digesting, they released a vital nutrient back into the water: phosphorus.

Think of phosphorus like fertilizer for the ocean. With more of it available, tiny sea creatures grew and multiplied. When they died, some of their remains sank to the ocean floor and got buried in mud — taking carbon with them. Because less organic matter sat around to soak up oxygen, the gas stayed in the atmosphere where it belonged.

A feedback loop that made Earth livable

It became a loop that kept going: more oxygen → more sulfate → more phosphorus → more life → more buried carbon → more oxygen. Like a snowball rolling downhill and gathering more snow as it goes.

This cycle may explain why oxygen levels stayed high enough over long stretches of time to eventually allow complex life to evolve. Without it, the early atmosphere might have stayed too thin in oxygen for anything bigger than a bacterium to thrive.

Try this at home

Fill a clear jar with water and add a few drops of food coloring. Drop a small piece of spinach or seaweed into it and leave it in sunlight. Over a few days, watch as the plant releases tiny bubbles (oxygen) and the water gradually clears — a living reminder of how photosynthesis changes a world.

Fun Fact

About 21% of the air we breathe today is oxygen. All of it — every breath you take — owes its lasting presence to cycles like this one that began billions of years ago, proving that even the grandest changes start with tiny creatures in the sea.

The Great Oxidation Event (~2.4-2.3 Ga) marked when oxygen first became a lasting part of Earth's atmosphere, produced by cyanobacterial photosynthesis. ScienceDaily reports a study in Nature Communications by UC Riverside's Andrey Bekker showing that as ocean oxygen increased, sulfate concentrations rose, enabling microbes to break down organic matter more efficiently and release phosphorus back into seawater. This phosphorus fueled biological productivity, allowing more organic carbon to be buried in sediments. Since decomposing organic matter consumes oxygen, burying more carbon helped maintain atmospheric oxygen levels — a positive feedback loop. Wikipedia's Great Oxidation Event article confirms the GOE occurred ~2.46–2.426 Ga, that cyanobacteria were the oxygen source, and that nutrient availability (including phosphorus) influenced the pace of atmospheric change. The study used a new technique to separate biologically available phosphorus from phosphorus locked in minerals in ancient South African rocks, providing clearer reconstruction of nutrient cycles than previous methods.
