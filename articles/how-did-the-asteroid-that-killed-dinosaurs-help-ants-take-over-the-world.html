How Did the Asteroid That Killed the Dinosaurs Help Ants Take Over the World?

2026-10-293 min

About 66 million years ago, a giant asteroid struck Earth and ended the Age of Dinosaurs. Now scientists have discovered that the same event may have helped ants rise to dominance — thanks to 'jumping genes' hidden in their DNA that activated right when the dust settled.

How Did the Asteroid That Killed the Dinosaurs Help Ants Take Over the World?

The day everything changed

About 66 million years ago, a giant asteroid slammed into what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. The impact was so massive that it threw up a global curtain of dust and debris, blocking sunlight for years and triggering the extinction of about three-quarters of all plant and animal species — including every non-avian dinosaur.

But while the giant reptiles disappeared, a much smaller group of creatures began their quiet rise to dominance: the ants.

Jumping genes: the hidden power of ant evolution

Ants had existed for millions of years before the asteroid hit. But they were relatively small and simple colonies. After the extinction event, something extraordinary happened. A new international study published in Science Advances reveals that bursts of activity from "jumping genes" — pieces of DNA that can copy and paste themselves to new locations in the genome — coincided precisely with the rise of major ant groups.

Jumping genes were once dismissed by scientists as mere "genomic parasites." But now researchers realize they can also spark evolutionary change by altering how genes work. When the scientists compared 163 different ant species representing 12 of the 16 living ant subfamilies, they found a striking pattern: lineages with the most jumping genes also tended to have the most species alive today.

The genetics of a super-organized society

The connection between jumping genes and ant success is especially visible in how ants communicate. Nearly every aspect of ant life — from finding food to recognizing colony mates to organizing complex nests — depends on chemical signals called pheromones. Ants detect these scents through odorant receptors, proteins that work like tiny chemical antennas on their antennae.

The study found that jumping genes may have helped expand the families of genes responsible for these odorant receptors. This meant ants could detect and interpret far more chemical messages than their ancestors — giving them the ability to coordinate larger, more sophisticated societies.

Think about it: if you could only smell three or four different things, your world would be very limited. But ants, thanks to their expanded chemical toolkit, can distinguish between hundreds or even thousands of different scents. That's what lets a single colony coordinate thousands of workers across complex tasks.

A timeline written in DNA

The researchers reconstructed 100 million years of ant evolution by comparing the genomes of 163 species. Their results paint a clear picture: as the dinosaur-killing asteroid reshaped Earth's ecosystems, new ecological opportunities opened up for ants. The bursts of jumping gene activity provided the genetic raw material for rapid adaptation.

This isn't unique to ants — scientists have found similar patterns in primates and bats. But ants provide the most dramatic example: from a few small forest-floor dwellers, they exploded into over 15,000 known species found on every continent (except Antarctica).

Try this at home

Collect different types of ants from your yard or a nearby park — you might find that ants from the same colony look identical, but ants from different colonies have different color patterns or sizes. Create a simple "ant hotel" using a clear container filled with moist sand. Watch how they build tunnels and chambers — each chamber might serve a different purpose, just like rooms in a house.

You can also experiment with communication. Try leaving a trail of flour or sugar across a table and watch how ants follow each other's scent paths. This is their version of leaving notes for each other — one ant discovers food, lays down a chemical trail, and dozens of others follow that same invisible roadmap.

Fun Fact

The study was led by Dr. Lukas Schrader of the University of Münster in Germany, who sequenced and compared genomes from 163 ant species to trace the history of jumping gene activity across 100 million years of evolution.

The DOI for the Science Advances paper is [10.1126/sciadv.aee0306](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aee0306).

for-grownups: The study "Bursts of transposable element activity accompany the radiation of ants" (Science Advances, October 2026; DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aee0306) was led by Dr. Lukas Schrader's team at the University of Münster. The K-Pg (Cretaceous-Paleogene) extinction event ~66 million years ago is dated to 66.0 ± 0.05 Ma, associated with the Chicxulub asteroid impact (10-15 km diameter) in the Yucatán Peninsula. Ants (family Formicidae) evolved from vespoid wasp ancestors in the Cretaceous, with modern subfamilies diversifying after the K-Pg boundary. Wikipedia confirms that transposable elements (TEs), first discovered by Barbara McClintock (Nobel Prize 1983), are DNA sequences capable of changing genomic positions and are present in most eukaryotic genomes, contributing to genetic diversity and evolutionary adaptation. The connection between TEs and evolutionary innovation has been documented across taxa including mammals, insects, and plants.
