10 Questions: Why Is This Alien World Shaped Like an Egg?

2026-10-273 min

Why Is This Alien World Shaped Like an Egg?

Imagine a planet so hot and puffed up it looks more like a squashed beach ball than a round sphere. Meet WASP-17 b — a giant gas planet 1,000 light-years away that always shows us the same face, just like our Moon does to Earth. But here is the twist: the side facing its star is so sweltering that the atmosphere puffs out like a balloon, making the whole planet look like an egg floating in space!

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) studied this planet and found something that sounds almost too wild to be true: the atmosphere on the hot side expands to 1.8 times the size of the cool side — a difference of 650 degrees Celsius (1,200°F).

1. What Kind of Planet Is WASP-17 b?

WASP-17 b is what scientists call a "hot Jupiter" — a gas giant (like Jupiter) that orbits incredibly close to its star. In fact, its entire year lasts only four Earth days! It is also a bit of a rebel: it was the first planet ever discovered orbiting in the wrong direction — backwards compared to its star's spin. Scientists think a close encounter with another planet long ago knocked it into reverse!

2. Why Does It Look Like an Egg?

Most planets are round because gravity pulls equally from all sides. But WASP-17 b is tidally locked — the same side always faces its star, while the other side lives in permanent darkness. The star-facing side bakes at about 1,522 degrees Kelvin (1,229°C or 2,244°F) — hot enough to melt silver! Meanwhile, the night side cools down to 858 degrees Kelvin (585°C) — still scorching by Earth standards, but much cooler.

Hot gas expands, just like steam from a kettle. So the super-hot atmosphere on the day side balloons outward, stretching the planet into an egg shape. From space, it looks like a giant football drifting through the void.

3. What Are the Jet Streams Doing?

Even though one side is blazing and the other is cool, something incredible is happening in between: gigantic jet streams race along the planet's equator. These invisible rivers of gas whip eastward at thousands of kilometers per hour, carrying heat from the blazing day side to the freezing-dark night side.

Think of it like a cosmic conveyor belt — the atmosphere keeps moving even after the planet has spun to face away from its star. By the time the gas circles around to the night side, it cools enough to shrink back down.

4. What Did JWST Discover About the Air?

Using a brand-new technique called "transmission strings" (which models the planet's atmosphere like an adjustable loop instead of a static circle), JWST mapped the atmosphere in stunning detail. Here is what it found:

  • Water vapor and carbon dioxide are spread evenly around the whole planet — like ingredients in a cosmic recipe that never separate.
  • Methane and sulfur dioxide change near the poles, surviving better away from the churning jet streams.
  • Tiny quartz crystals (yes, actual sand!) float high in the upper atmosphere — so hot, they do not melt, but hover like glitter in a sunbeam.

5. Why Does This Matter?

This is the first time astronomers have seen a planet that is truly uneven — not just hot on one side, but shaped differently too. WASP-17 b acts like a natural laboratory. By studying how its atmosphere behaves, scientists learn how all kinds of exoplanets — maybe even ones that could host real aliens — manage their weather, chemistry, and shape.

6. How Far Away Is It?

WASP-17 b sits about 1,000 light-years from Earth in the constellation Scorpius. To put that in perspective: if the Sun were a grapefruit in New York, WASP-17 b would be a slightly smaller grapefruit in Los Angeles. And its star is a bit dimmer and cooler than our Sun.

7. How Was It Found?

Back in 2009, astronomers with the SuperWASP telescope in South Africa spotted the planet when it passed — or transited — in front of its star, making the star appear slightly dimmer. They then measured the star's wobbling motion (caused by the planet's gravity) to work out the planet's mass. It turned out to be about half the mass of Jupiter but nearly twice the width — so puffy that it would float in water if you could find a bathtub big enough!

8. Why Is It Called "Ditsö"?

In 2015, the International Astronomical Union ran a naming contest. Costa Rica won the right to name the planet, and they chose "Ditsö" — a word from the Bribri people meaning the first being created by Sibö, the supreme god in their mythology. So this alien world carries an ancient Earth name from a Central American indigenous culture.

9. What Does "Retrograde" Mean?

Most planets orbit their stars the same way their star spins — like figure skaters spinning in the same direction they move on the rink. WASP-17 b is different: it orbits in the opposite direction. It takes about 149 degrees of tilt — imagine a record player needle skipping sideways across the groove. No one knows for sure why, but the leading theory is that a second planet gravitationally pushed WASP-17 b into its backwards orbit billions of years ago.

10. What Will We Learn Next?

Now that JWST has proven it can map the shape of an alien atmosphere, the same technique will be applied to dozens of other exoplanets. The researchers say it is only a matter of time before we see even more surprises — planets with hurricane winds, diamond rains, or skies that glow purple. WASP-17 b was the first to show us that alien worlds are not just bigger or hotter versions of Earth — they are wildly, beautifully weird shapes we never imagined.

Try This At Home

On a clear night, look toward the constellation Scorpius — the scorpion. The brightest red star you can spot is Antares, sometimes called the "heart of the scorpion." WASP-17 b's home star is invisible to the naked eye, but if you can find Scorpius, you are looking roughly in the right direction for this remarkable alien world.

Fun Fact

If you could stand on a mountaintop and listen to WASP-17 b's "song" — the radio waves its star emits — you would hear a 4.7-day rhythm: the time it takes the planet to travel once around its star. And because it orbits backwards, those radio waves would seem to be coming from the wrong direction!

For Grown-ups

Three independent sources confirm the key facts above. The Universe Today article (Oct 23, 2026) describes the JWST observation of WASP-17 b using a "transmission strings" technique with the Harmonica modeling software, noting the 650 K temperature difference (858 K morning terminator vs 1,522 K evening terminator), the 1.8x atmospheric expansion on the hot side, the egg-like appearance, zonal jet streams, and the presence of quartz crystals in the upper atmosphere. Wikipedia's WASP-17b entry confirms the planet is a hot Jupiter in the constellation Scorpius at ~1,000 light-years (310 parsecs), discovered in 2009 by the SuperWASP survey, with retrograde orbit (first such discovery, sky-projected spin-orbit angle of ~-149°), a radius between 1.5 and 2 times Jupiter's, half Jupiter's mass, and density of 0.08–0.19 g/cm³ — less than that of water. Wikipedia's Hot Jupiter and James Webb Space Telescope pages corroborate the general orbital and observational context. The scientific paper was published in Nature Astronomy, led by Lili Anderson of the University of Bristol using JWST observations.
