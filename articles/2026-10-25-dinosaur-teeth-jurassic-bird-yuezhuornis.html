A 150-million-year-old bird fossil from China called Yuezhuornis had dinosaur teeth, a sickle-shaped claw, and legs built for walking — not flying. Its mix of bird and dinosaur traits shows that early bird evolution took many surprising paths.

Why did this early bird keep dinosaur teeth and a killing claw instead of flying?

1. A bird that looks like a dinosaur

Imagine a bird the size of a crow that walked on two strong legs, snapped up prey with a mouth full of sharp, serrated teeth, and had a curved claw on its foot that looked like it belonged to a tiny Velociraptor. That bird actually existed — and its name is Yuezhuornis (pronounced roughly "Yway-zhoo-or-niss").

Scientists found this strange creature in rocks from 150 million years ago in southeastern China. It lived during the Late Jurassic period, the same time as the famous Archaeopteryx from Germany. At first glance, Yuezhuornis seems like a mix-up: it has the hips and legs of a bird, but its teeth, claws, and foot shape look more like a small meat-eating dinosaur.

2. The "killing claw" that ties birds to dinosaurs

One of the most striking features of Yuezhuornis is its second toe — the toe that sits behind the others when a bird perches. In most birds, this toe is short and simple. But in Yuezhuornis, that toe bends backward nearly 180 degrees and ends in a sickle-shaped claw about the length of a thumbnail.

This claw is the famous "killing claw" that paleontologists associate with dromaeosaurids — a family of agile, feathered predators that includes Velociraptor and Deinonychus. Archaeopteryx had a similar claw, and that is one of the main reasons scientists have long believed birds evolved from small theropod dinosaurs. Seeing the same claw on Yuezhuornis strengthens that link even more: the feature was not a one-off accident, but a trait passed down through many generations of early bird relatives.

Combined with its serrated teeth (birds today have beaks, not teeth), Yuezhuornis looks like a creature caught between two worlds — part bird, part dinosaur.

3. A bird that gave up flying

While Yuezhuornis carries dinosaur features, it also shows something surprising about how birds evolved. By measuring its leg bones and comparing them to modern birds, scientists found that its limbs were built for life on the ground, not for flying.

This means Yuezhuornis was likely flightless — one of the earliest known bird lineages to lose the ability to fly. For a long time, scientists assumed that bird evolution was a steady march toward better flight. But Yuezhuornis tells a different story: early birds were experimenting with many different ways of life. Some got better at flying, while others, like Yuezhuornis, became specialists at running and hunting on the forest floor.

The fossil came from a newly discovered fossil site called the Zhenghe Fauna in Fujian Province. As more fossils emerge from this site, scientists are learning that the early history of birds was a branching bush with many different paths — not a single straight line.

Try this at home

Cut out two bird shapes from stiff paper or cardboard. Leave one's "killing claw" — the toe on the inside — able to bend backward. On the other shape, draw a beak and short legs. Now pretend one is a sky-soaring hawk and the other is a ground-hunting dinosaur-bird. Ask: which one would be better at catching a scurrying lizard? Which one would escape a landslide by flying away? Both designs work — just in very different worlds.

Fun Fact

Birds are the only dinosaurs that survived the mass extinction 66 million years ago. That means every sparrow, eagle, and penguin alive today is a direct descendant of small theropod dinosaurs from the Jurassic period — the same group that gave us Tyrannosaurus and Velociraptor. In other words, dinosaurs never really went extinct. They just learned to fly... and sing!
