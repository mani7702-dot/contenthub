Why is the ground doing a strange dance beneath a volcano that looms over 2 million people?

2026-10-243 min

Satellites have discovered that the ground around Nicaragua's Masaya volcano is moving in opposite directions — one part sinking while another rises — as two hidden magma reservoirs behave very differently.

1. Why is the ground doing a strange dance beneath this volcano?

Something puzzling is happening underground

Just 20 kilometers (about 12 miles) south of Managua, the capital of Nicaragua, sits one of Earth's most unusual volcanoes. Masaya Volcano is not a single mountain with a pointy top — it is a giant caldera, a huge bowl-shaped depression formed by ancient eruptions so mighty they emptied underground chambers and caused the ground to collapse.

And here is the strange part: while one area beneath the volcano is sinking, another area is slowly rising — like the ground itself is doing a slow, silent dance. Best of all, scientists can watch this dance from space.

The reason this matters is simple: about 2 million people call the surrounding cities and towns home. Masaya is also a popular national park where visitors walk right up to a glowing lava lake and breathe air tinged with sulfur. Understanding what the ground is doing — and why — could help keep everyone safe.

Watching the volcano from space

You might think studying a volcano hidden beneath miles of rock would be impossible. But satellites can see it with invisible eyes.

The researchers used a satellite called Sentinel-1, which beams radar waves down at Earth and bounces them back. By comparing the returning signals over time, scientists can measure changes in ground height as small as a few centimeters — even through clouds.

The technique is called InSAR (interferometric synthetic aperture radar). It is like taking the same photograph of the volcano over and over and noticing that a single blade of grass has shifted. Over six years — from 2018 to 2024 — the team watched the ground around Masaya move up and down in patterns that surprised even the experts.

Two magma tanks, moving in opposite directions

Here is where it gets curious. The ground inside the wider caldera first sank and then began to rise again — suggesting that fresh molten rock (magma) was draining away and then flowing back in to a deep underground chamber.

But the area around the Santiago crater — the active one with the bubbling lava lake — did the opposite. It kept sinking the entire time, even as the rest of the caldera began rising. This suggests there is a second, shallower magma reservoir that is steadily losing material.

Think of it like two bathtubs connected by a pipe. If you pull the plug on the lower tub, it empties and sinks. Meanwhile, water from elsewhere refills the upper tub and it rises. Masaya appears to have something similar happening underground — and the two tanks are not perfectly synced.

The lead researcher, Lizzie Johnson (now studying at the University of Hawaii), says that this tells us the volcano's plumbing is more complex than scientists used to think. "When it's subsiding, that means magma is draining away," she explained. "And when it starts uplifting again, fresh magma may be moving in."

Why this is good news, not bad news

Scientists were careful to say: there is no sign of an eruption coming. The ground movements are slow — just a few centimeters over months or years. The real value is learning what "normal" looks like for Masaya.

"If we know the everyday rhythm of the volcano, then when something changes, we know to pay attention," explained Young Cheol Kim, a co-author of the study. "That is how we protect 2 million people."

The same satellite tools can now be used to watch other volcanoes around the world — especially those with lava lakes that sit beneath growing cities.

Try this at home

Make a "volcano lake" and watch it move:

Fill a clear bowl halfway with water. Drop in a few drops of food coloring — that is your "magma." Now gently blow across the surface. Watch how the colored swirls move and spread in different directions. That is a little like how underground magma can flow in more than one path at once.

Satellite trick: On a sunny day, look straight down at your shadow on the ground. Now tilt your phone or tablet camera slightly and watch how the shadow seems to "shrink" and "grow." That is the same kind of trick InSAR uses — comparing signals from slightly different angles to measure tiny changes in the ground.

Fun Fact

Despite its dramatic reputation, Masaya's last major lava flow was in 1772 — over 250 years ago. Since then, the volcano has mostly just fumes sulfur dioxide gas (which is why visitors sometimes wear masks). It became Nicaragua's first national park back in 1979, protecting both the volcano and the unique wildlife that thrives in its mineral-rich soils.
