What ancient plants are surviving in England's coldest valley — and how are kids helping save them?

2026-10-233 min

In Upper Teesdale, England, a special group of plants that have lived since the last Ice Age are getting help from a new £65,000 conservation project — collecting seeds, training volunteers, and planting them in the high fells.

What ancient plants are surviving in England's coldest valley — and how are kids helping save them?

A valley that feels like the Arctic

Tucked away in the Pennines of northern England is a valley unlike any other. Upper Teesdale sits high on the moors, where the wind whistles, the rain falls often, and the ground stays cool even in summer. Scientists call it a "botanical hotspot" — a place where so many rare plants grow together that it ranks among the top five in all of Britain and Ireland.

But here is what makes it truly special: many of the plants here are leftovers from the Ice Age. That is right — they have been growing in this valley for over 11,000 years! While the glaciers melted and the world warmed up around them, these tough little plants stayed, clinging to the cold, high ground where it still feels a bit like the Arctic.

Meet the Ice Age survivors

Imagine a flower that looks like it belongs on a mountain in Scotland, not in England. The Spring Gentian is one of them — a delicate purple bloom that brightens the valley floor in April. Then there is the Bird's-eye Primrose, with sunny yellow flowers that seem to watch the sky. The Mountain Avens has white petals that sparkle after rain, and the Alpine Bistort sends up round pink puffs that look like tiny cotton balls.

These are called arctic-alpine plants — a name that tells you they like it cold. They grow in the harshest spots: on rocky ledges, in wet grasslands, and on the high moorland fells where few other plants can survive. But even the toughest plants need help sometimes.

A £65,000 rescue mission

That is why a group called the Teesdale Special Flora Research and Conservation Trust launched a new three-year project. They won £64,739 in funding — that is about the cost of a school playground or 650 bicycles! The money comes from a government program called Farming in Protected Landscapes, run by Defra (that is the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs).

The plan is simple but smart:

1. Collect seeds from the healthiest, strongest plants growing in safe spots.

2. Map where every rare plant grows so scientists always know where to look.

3. Plant baby plants (called "plugs") in careful trial plots on the fell.

4. Train volunteers — people from the local community — to learn plant names, collect seeds, and help with planting.

Why kids (and everyone) should care

Plants are the quiet heroes of our planet. They clean the air, hold the soil in place, give animals food and shelter, and even make oxygen that we breathe. The arctic-alpine plants of Teesdale are like living history books — each one a survivor story stretching back to when giant ice sheets covered England.

And here is the best part: you do not need to be a grown-up to help! If you visit a local park or nature area with a grown-up, you can look for wildflowers, draw them in a notebook, and learn their names. Gardeners often save seeds from their flowers in autumn — that is exactly what the Teesdale project does, just on a bigger scale.

Try this at home

Seed-saving experiment: Ask a grown-up to help you collect a few flower seeds (from a dandelion, sunflower, or any flower in your garden). Put them in a small paper envelope labeled with the date and plant name. Keep them dry and cool. In spring, try planting them in a pot. You are doing the same thing the Teesdale conservationists do — preserving plants for the future!

Fun Fact

Upper Teesdale is the only place in the whole world where so many arctic-alpine plants grow right next to plants that love sunshine and warmth. It is like nature's own experiment — a living greenhouse where cold-weather flowers and warm-weather flowers grow side by side, and no one else on Earth has exactly this mix!
