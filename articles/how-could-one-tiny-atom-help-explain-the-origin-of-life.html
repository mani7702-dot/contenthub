How Could One Tiny Atom Help Explain the Origin of Life?

2026-10-313 min

Life's building blocks had to gather somewhere before there were any cells to hold them. New research shows a single oxygen atom makes RNA unusually good at clumping into droplets — a small quirk of chemistry with enormous consequences.

How Could One Tiny Atom Help Explain the Origin of Life?

Every living thing on Earth, from a blue whale to a backyard mushroom, is built from the same basic ingredients. Yet the question of how those ingredients first came together — long before there were any cells to keep them in one place — remains one of science's great puzzles. A new study out of the University at Buffalo offers a surprisingly delicate clue.

The chicken-and-egg problem

For decades, many researchers have explored "RNA world" theory: the idea that RNA, a cousin of DNA, was one of the first molecules to matter for life. RNA is remarkable because it can both store information and help trigger chemical reactions.

But there was a catch. RNA is fragile, and early Earth was hot, acidic and harsh. Without a cell membrane to gather RNA molecules together, how could they possibly meet often enough to interact? It seemed like a problem with no obvious answer.

A difference of one single atom

The answer, it turns out, may hide in an almost impossibly small detail.

RNA and DNA differ by just one oxygen atom in each of their sugar units. That tiny extra group — called 2'-hydroxyl — changes how the molecule behaves in a big way.

In laboratory experiments, the team found that RNA began forming liquid-like droplets at temperatures about 10 °C lower than matching strands of DNA. In other words, RNA has a much stronger natural urge to cluster together.

That single group also helps RNA grip magnesium ions more tightly while holding on to fewer water molecules, making it easier for separate RNA strands to drift close and assemble as things warm up.

Droplets as a first shelter

Those droplets, known as condensates, have no membrane — but they concentrate RNA in one small space, boosting the odds of chemical interaction while offering some protection from the surroundings.

Even better, as the droplets grew more interconnected, they shifted from a fluid into a stiffer, gel-like material. That change could have helped shield fragile RNA through tough conditions, buying early molecules precious time.

The team then tested the idea further by chemically tweaking that same group, which reduced RNA's urge to cluster — a neat confirmation that this one detail really is doing the heavy lifting.

Fun fact

The two molecules of life are so alike that RNA and DNA differ by only one oxygen atom per sugar — a difference you could not see under an ordinary microscope, yet it may have helped decide the fate of an entire planet.
