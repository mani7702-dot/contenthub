How did Starlink fix the internet that drones depend on?

A connection from space

Imagine trying to video-call someone while riding on a fast-moving train — but the

phone tower keeps disappearing behind hills. For pilots of drones and soldiers in

remote areas, that is exactly the problem that Starlink solves, using not

towers but satellites floating in space.

Starlink is a network of more than 10,000 small satellites orbiting Earth in

what is called low Earth orbit — much closer than the satellites you might picture

from old movies. Because they are close, signals travel fast, and because there are

so many of them, the network can cover almost the entire planet, even places with no

cell towers at all.

Why drones needed it

On the battlefield, drones are like remote-controlled eyes and helpers. They can

spot things, carry small packages, and even defend against attacks — but they need

to stay connected to their operators. Regular radio or cell signals do not work

well when drones fly fast or far, especially over rough ground.

Starlink gives them a wireless bridge through space. A small dish on the ground or

on a vehicle points at the sky, "sees" nearby satellites, and bounces the signal

along from one satellite to the next until it reaches its destination. That is why

Ukraine's military calls it vital for "logistics and communications."

When the link broke

For about a week, something went wrong. The connection started limiting how fast

drones could fly — if a drone went over about 100 miles per hour (160 km/h), it

would suddenly lose its internet link. Slower drones stayed connected; faster ones

did not.

That is like having a highway where cars above a certain speed fall off the road.

It made some drone missions harder to run.

Speed limits lifted

On Saturday, Ukraine's defence minister said the connection was now "stable"

again and that the network was working at full speed — over 250 miles per hour

(400 km/h). The problem, he said, had been sorted out.

This matters because in many parts of the world — not just war zones — people and

machines still depend on space-based internet to stay connected where nothing else

reaches.

Try this at home

Look up on a clear evening and you may see tiny moving dots — those could be

Starlink satellites! Apps like "Find Starlink" show when and where to look.

Fun Fact: Each Starlink satellite is about the size of a pizza box and weighs

roughly as much as a cat. They are launched in batches on SpaceX's Falcon 9

rockets, with more than 60 satellites fitting on a single rocket.
