Why does Mount Etna sometimes erupt in hours and other times in weeks?

2026-10-313 min

Mount Etna can brew an eruption like a shaken soda bottle or let it fizz slowly for weeks. Scientists cracked the difference by reading minerals that grew inside the volcano's hidden plumbing system.

1. What makes an eruption wait?

Volcanoes are fed by enormous underground networks of magma and gas, but those systems don't always behave the same way. Even a single volcano can produce eruptions through very different paths. At Mount Etna in Italy, a shaken-soda-bottle kind of day and a slow-fizz afternoon can both happen, and the difference comes down to two invisible players: water and carbon dioxide.

2. The week-long eruption of 122 B.C.

A Cornell-led research team reconstructed two major eruptions from Etna's past and found just how dramatically the pathways can vary. Their first case was a "mafic Plinian" eruption around 122 B.C. — a fancy way of saying low-viscosity, gas-rich magma that once powered a towering eruption column.

Using a technique called Raman spectroscopy, the scientists examined tiny crystals that grew inside the magma. Those crystals act like time capsules, and by measuring how much gas was trapped inside them, the researchers could calculate the pressure — and from that, the depth at which each bubble formed.

The 122 B.C. magma began rising from about 20 kilometers deep, but instead of shooting to the surface, it slowly climbed and then stalled between 2 and 5 kilometers deep, where it lingered for several weeks, steadily releasing gas. Picture a soda bottle that's been opened slowly — the bubbles escape gently, no explosion.

3. The hours-long blast from 4,000 years ago

The team then compared that to another Etna event known as the Fall Stratified eruption, which occurred roughly 4,000 years ago. That magma traveled upward from an even deeper starting point — 24 to 30 kilometers below the surface — and this time it rose quickly and erupted within hours, like a shaken bottle of soda finally flipped open.

The key difference: the rapid eruption carried far more carbon dioxide. Etna is one of the few volcanoes in the world where both water and CO2 strongly shape eruptions, rather than one gas dominating. Higher CO2 can drive magma rapidly from great depths, while lower CO2 tends to let it stall and fizz slowly halfway up.

4. Why it matters

Understanding these different pathways helps scientists build better models for predicting what Etna — and volcanoes like it — might do next. The same technique is now being applied to volcanoes in Chile, Hawaii, and many other regions, promising a clearer picture of when a quiet mountain might suddenly wake up. Etna isn't just a scenic backdrop anymore; it's a teacher, showing us how the planet's hidden plumbing carries both whispers and roars.

Fun fact

The 122 B.C. eruption happened the same year that the future Roman Emperor Augustus was born. The same volcano is still active today — and is actually Europe's most active, averaging about 25 eruptions per year.
