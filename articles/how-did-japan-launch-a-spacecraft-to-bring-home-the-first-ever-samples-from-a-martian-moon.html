How Did Japan Launch a Spacecraft to Bring Home the First-Ever Samples From a Martian Moon?

2026-10-304 min

Japan's Martian Moons eXploration mission blasted off on October 19th, heading toward Phobos — a tiny potato-shaped moon where a robot rover will scout landing sites before the main craft drills up at least 10 grams of regolith to bring home to Earth in 2031.

1 — What is Japan's bold new mission to the moons of Mars?

On October 19th, 2026, a hush fell over mission control at Tanegashima Space Center as a sleek silver rocket climbed into the morning sky. It carried with it the dreams of thousands of scientists, engineers, and curious minds around the world: a spacecraft bound for the mysterious moons of Mars, carrying a message written in stardust and ambition.

The journey had been five years in the making. Every bolt, every circuit, every line of code was a step toward a single goal — to answer questions about our solar system that have fascinated humans for generations. As the rocket disappeared into space, a quiet cheer echoed through the control room. The adventure was underway.

2 — How the journey to Phobos and Deimos will unfold

The mission — called MMX, short for Martian Moons eXploration — is led by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), working hand in hand with NASA, the European Space Agency, and space agencies in France and Germany. This $578 million collaboration has one bold prize: bringing home the first-ever samples from a moon other than our own.

The roughly five-ton spacecraft will travel nearly three years through the void before reaching Mars in late summer 2027. There, it will study the planet's two oddly shaped moons — the larger Phobos, about 14 miles across, and the tiny Deimos. These potato-shaped worlds have puzzled astronomers for over a century. Are they captured asteroids, or the remains of a giant impact long ago? The samples MMX collects could finally settle the debate.

3 — The little rover with a big job

Before the main craft can land, it sends ahead a tiny companion — a microwave-sized rover named Idefix (after the loyal dog in the Asterix comics). This four-wheeled scout will bounce across Phobos' surface at a pace slower than a garden snail, mapping the terrain and searching for the safest place to touch down.

On Phobos, gravity is so weak — just half a percent of Earth's — that the rover might bounce like a rubber ball before finding its rest. When the scout gives the thumbs-up, MMX's lander will descend for a brief visit, using precision tools to drill into the dusty soil and collect at least 10 grams of material. The entire operation must be finished in just two hours before the craft returns to orbit.

4 — When will the samples return to Earth?

After its Martian adventure, MMX will swing by Deimos for a final look, then begin its long journey home. In 2030, a small capsule will peel away and arc through space toward a landing site in the Australian desert — its cargo of Martian dirt set to arrive on Earth in 2031.

Scientists are eager to study these grains, which may carry traces of Mars itself, blasted free eons ago and preserved untouched ever since. The mission will also gather data that could one day help humans live and work on Mars, turning Phobos into a potential stepping stone for future explorers.

Fun fact: The rover on Phobos moves at a pace of just 1 millimeter per second — slower than a garden snail! And because Phobos' gravity is so weak, the rover doesn't even need landing gear. It uses a spring mechanism to hop onto the surface and might bounce around until it finally settles down.
