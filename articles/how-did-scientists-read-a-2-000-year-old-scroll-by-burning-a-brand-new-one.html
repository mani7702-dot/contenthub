How Did Scientists Read a 2,000-Year-Old Scroll by Burning a Brand-New One?

2026-10-31T05:36:00+05:304 min

Nearly 2,000 years ago, Mount Vesuvius turned a Roman library into lumps of black charcoal. Now scientists have found a clever trick for peering inside those fragile scrolls — and to test it, they burned some brand-new ones of their own.

How Did Scientists Read a 2,000-Year-Old Scroll by Burning a Brand-New One?

A Library Turned to Charcoal

In 79 CE, Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman town of Herculaneum under roughly 20 metres of rock and ash. The same eruption famously swallowed nearby Pompeii.

One thing the disaster left behind was strange and precious: a library. The Villa of the Papyri held more than a thousand papyrus scrolls, and the intense heat carbonised them — turning them into brittle black logs. It is the only intact library known to have survived from the ancient world, possibly holding writings by Epicurus and other thinkers that exist nowhere else.

The trouble is reading it. Try to unroll a carbonised scroll and it often crumbles to dust. Early attempts after the scrolls were found in 1752 went so badly that Italian authorities eventually banned the practice.

The Lead in the Ink

Modern researchers can peer inside without opening them, using X-ray tomography — the same family of scanning that hospitals use. But there is a catch. Both the ink and the papyrus are made of carbon, so on a scan they look almost identical. The letters hide in the static.

Douglas Seiler, an affiliate of Berkeley SETI at the University of California, Berkeley, had an idea. Some ancient inks contain lead. Lead blocks X-rays far more strongly than carbon does — in his tests, around 25 times more. If a scroll's ink had lead in it, the writing should light up.

To prove the idea worked, his team had to make their own disaster. They bought papyrus and reed pens from Egypt and traditional lampblack ink from Japan, then paid high school students to copy out passages from Star Wars, the Bible, and a 1960s science-fiction show. They rolled the papyrus, sealed it, and burned it in a high-temperature furnace until it was thoroughly charred.

A Word at a Time

The plan worked. A handheld X-ray fluorescence scanner could spot lead in the fake scrolls at every concentration they tried, and X-ray tomography combined with custom software let the team read back words they had written.

Published in the journal PLOS ONE, the method offers a practical first step: screen a whole collection quickly for lead, then spend detailed scanning time on the scrolls most likely to give up their secrets. The homemade scrolls help in another way too, because researchers already know what the writing inside them says. That makes them perfect practice material for the computer programs that slowly learn to read ancient texts.

Fun fact

Seiler's test scrolls are among the few documents in history that were written purely so they could be set on fire — a fake library burned on purpose to rescue a real one.
