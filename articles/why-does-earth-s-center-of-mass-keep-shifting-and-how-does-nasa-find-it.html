Why does Earth's center of mass keep shifting — and how does NASA find it?

2026-10-233 min

Earth's center of mass drifts by a few millimeters every season as water, ice, and air shift around the planet — and NASA scientists now track those tiny movements with laser-ranging satellites and GPS.

Why does Earth's center of mass keep shifting — and how does NASA find it?

The planet that never sits still

You probably think of Earth as a big, solid ball that just spins in space. But even our home planet is always fidgeting! Earth's center of mass — the balance point where all its weight evens out — is not a fixed dot in the middle of the world. It drifts back and forth by a few millimeters every single season, shifting as water, ice, and even air move around the surface.

Water, ice, and air on the move

Imagine a giant scale with Earth on it. When winter snow piles up on the mountains of North America, that extra weight tips the balance ever so slightly. When summer melts that snow into rivers that flow to the ocean, the water sloshes to a new spot and the balance point shifts again.

Even the air plays a part. Dense, cold winter air sits heavier over polar regions, while warm, light summer air floats over the tropics. As the seasons change, those air masses trade places and nudge Earth's center of mass like invisible hands pushing a playground merry-go-round.

The distances are tiny — just a few millimeters, about the thickness of two stacked coins. But for scientists tracking satellites and measuring sea level, those tiny wobbles matter a lot.

The disco-ball satellites that watch Earth

To spot these ultra-small movements, NASA scientists use a clever trick: they track satellites from the ground.

Picture two space-age disco balls orbiting about 5,900 kilometers above Earth. These are the LAGEOS satellites — 60 centimeters wide, covered in 422 shiny glass mirrors that bounce light like a mirror ball at a party. Ground stations fire lasers at the satellites and time exactly how long the reflection takes to come back. Even a tiny shift in Earth's center changes the satellites' orbits just enough to measure.

By combining those laser measurements with GPS tracking and data from other satellites, scientists can pinpoint Earth's center of mass to within fractions of an inch.

Why does it matter?

You might wonder: who cares about a few millimeters? But modern life depends on extremely accurate positioning. GPS navigation, satellite maps, climate monitoring, sea-level measurements — all of them need to know exactly where Earth's center is. If scientists use an outdated location, even by a millimeter, airplane routes, weather forecasts, and flood maps can slowly drift off target.

Try this at home

Balance the world on your finger! Grab a round balloon and a sharpie. Draw a dot in the center — that is Earth's "geometric center." Now stick one small sticker or piece of tape off to one side — that is extra water, ice, or air. Try to balance the balloon on your fingertip. When the weight is not even, the balance point (the center of mass) shifts! Move the sticker around to different spots and watch how the balloon tilts. That is how Earth wobbles when water and ice move across its surface.

Fun Fact

Earth's center of mass shifts so slightly that the new NASA method found it moves about half as much as scientists thought eight years ago. It is like discovering your bedroom is only 2 inches longer — not 4 inches — and you had been measuring the rug wrong the whole time!
