How Does Vitamin A Help Build the Sharpest Part of the Human Eye?

2026-10-314 min

There's a tiny patch at the very centre of your retina that handles about half of everything you see. New research shows how a molecule made from vitamin A helps build it before birth — and overturns a 30-year-old idea about how eyes take shape.

How Does Vitamin A Help Build the Sharpest Part of the Human Eye?

Take a moment to read this sentence. To do it, your brain is relying on a patch of tissue smaller than a pinhead, tucked into the very centre of your retina. Scientists call it the foveola — and, remarkably, it handles roughly half of everything you see.

A new study from Johns Hopkins University has uncovered how that special patch gets built before birth. The surprise? A molecule made from vitamin A sits right at the centre of the story.

A tiny patch with a big job

Your retina is the light-sensitive lining at the back of the eye that turns light into signals the brain can read. At its middle sits the foveola, the region that lets you read, recognise a friend's face, or spot a single star in the night sky.

The foveola is packed with cone cells — the ones that give us daytime and colour vision. Humans have three kinds: red, green and blue cones. But here's the twist: the foveola contains red and green cones while leaving out the blue ones, and that neat arrangement is exactly what makes central vision so crisp.

A 30-year-old puzzle

For decades, the leading explanation was simple: blue cones showed up in the foveola early on, then quietly moved out of the way as the eye matured. Once a cone became a particular colour, the thinking went, it stayed that way forever.

Studying this in people is tricky, because mice and fish don't share our retinal layout. So the Johns Hopkins team grew lab-made human retinas, called organoids, and watched them develop over months.

What they saw instead

Between weeks 10 and 12 of development, a handful of blue cones appear in the foveola. By week 14, they seem to have changed identities — becoming red and green cones rather than travelling away.

Two chemical helpers drive it. First, retinoic acid, made from vitamin A, sets the pattern by limiting how many blue cones form in the centre. Then thyroid hormones nudge the leftover blue cones to convert. As lead scientist Robert J. Johnston put it, leaving those blue cones in place would blur your sharpest vision.

The team stresses one thing clearly: this is about how eyes form before birth — not a reason to take vitamin A supplements for better eyesight.

Fun fact: That pinhead-sized foveola is one of the first places to fail in macular degeneration, which is why understanding it matters so much. The study appears in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
