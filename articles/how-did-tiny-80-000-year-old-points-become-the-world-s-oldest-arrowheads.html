How Did Tiny 80,000-Year-Old Points Become the World's Oldest Arrowheads?

2026-11-014 min

High in the mountains of Uzbekistan, archaeologists have spotted something easy to miss: finger-sized slivers of stone, 80,000 years old, that may be the earliest arrowheads anyone has ever found.

Some of the biggest discoveries are almost too small to see. At a rocky shelter called Obi-Rakhmat, tucked into the mountains of Uzbekistan, archaeologists have found tiny triangular slivers of stone — and they may be the oldest arrowheads ever identified.

1. A speck of stone with a big story

The points are microlithic, which simply means very small. Each one is only a few centimetres long, barely bigger than a fingernail. Unlike many ancient tools, these were never carefully shaped or polished. They were left almost as they were chipped off.

That is exactly why they went unnoticed for so long. For decades, researchers digging at Obi-Rakhmat — first uncovered in 1962, high in the Tien Shan mountains at about 1,250 metres — walked right past them.

Then a team led by Hugues Plisson and Andrey I. Krivoshapkin took a much closer look. Under a microscope, the tiny stones told their story.

2. Reading the damage

Every time a sharp stone strikes a target, it leaves marks. By comparing the scratches and chips on these points with stones used in modern experiments, the team could work out how they had been used. The damage looked just like the wear an arrowhead gets when it is shot from a bow.

Their small size fits too. A light projectile needs to be sharp rather than heavy, so it can fly far and still pierce its target. Deliberately choosing such tiny points — when larger stone was readily available at the site — suggests a clever, purposeful design.

3. Why it matters

Arrow-and-bow style hunting is usually linked to Homo sapiens in Europe, roughly 25,000 years later, at a French cave called Mandrin in the Rhône Valley. Finding similar tiny points in Central Asia, dated to about 80,000 years ago, hints that this clever technology may have appeared far earlier — and far to the east — than the old story allowed.

The study, published in the journal PLOS ONE, doesn't settle who made the points. But it quietly redraws the map of when and where early people began hunting with light, fast, long-distance weapons.

Fun fact

The oldest known bow-and-arrow-style points are so small that many earlier diggers mistook them for leftover debris and simply brushed them aside. Sometimes the clue to a huge chapter of human history is smaller than a paperclip.
