Why Are JWST's Mysterious 'Little Red Dots' Secretly Growing Black Holes?

2026-10-31T22:21:00+05:305 min read

>-

Look deep enough into the night sky and you start seeing strange things. That is exactly what happened when the James Webb Space Telescope began scanning the distant universe: among the galaxies, it kept finding a large crowd of tiny, intensely red objects. Astronomers nicknamed them Little Red Dots, and for a while nobody could agree on what they were.

Now a new set of computer simulations offers a neat answer. The dots may be black holes growing at speeds we don't see anywhere in the modern universe.

A Cosmic Growth Spurt

The research, led by Sunmyon Chon of the Max Planck Institute for Astrophysics, used the powerful ATERUI III supercomputer in Japan. The team started with a young galaxy and zoomed in closer and closer, all the way down to individual clouds of gas.

What they found is that brilliant far-ultraviolet radiation from nearby galaxies can stop ordinary stars from forming inside certain gas clouds. Instead of breaking into thousands of small stars, the cloud collapses into one enormous star — which then falls in on itself and becomes a black hole seed.

Wrapped In a Blanket of Gas

Those seeds don't stay small for long. The simulations show they get wrapped in thick disks of dense gas. This gassy blanket traps radiation and lets the black hole swallow material far more efficiently than black holes can today — growing dozens of times faster.

And when the researchers compared their simulated black holes with the real Little Red Dots spotted by Webb, the two matched up closely.

Why This Matters

This isn't a small mystery. Some supermassive black holes contain millions or even billions of times the mass of our Sun, yet they already existed less than 600 million years after the Big Bang. How could anything get that big, that fast?

The new work suggests the answer may be hiding in plain sight. The simulations did not need any exotic physics or wildly unlikely events. Instead, the Little Red Dots may simply be an early chapter in the story of how black holes grew up.

Fun fact: The namesake "red" colour comes from the light of these objects being stretched out as the universe expands — so what started as brighter light reaches us as a deep, dusty red.
