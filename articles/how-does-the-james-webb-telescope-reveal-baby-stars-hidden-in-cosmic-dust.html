How Does the James Webb Telescope Reveal Baby Stars Hidden in Cosmic Dust?

2026-10-253 min

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has pierced the dusty veil of NGC 7129, a stellar nursery 3,300 light-years away in the constellation Cepheus. Webb's infrared eyes revealed over 130 baby stars — some less than 1 million years old — that were completely invisible to previous telescopes.

1. How Does the James Webb Telescope See Baby Stars That Other Telescopes Cannot?

Picture a cosmic nursery where dozens of baby stars are born inside thick, dusty clouds — invisible to ordinary cameras. Now imagine having a special pair of eyes that can peer straight through that dust to watch the stars grow up. That is exactly what NASA's James Webb Space Telescope can do!

Webb has just released a stunning new image of NGC 7129, a stellar nursery that sits about 3,300 light-years from Earth in the constellation Cepheus. Thanks to Webb's powerful infrared vision, astronomers found over 130 young stars — some less than a million years old — that were completely hidden before.

2. The Golden Glow of a Cosmic Cavity

At the heart of NGC 7129 shines a bright, young star called LkH-alpha 234 (say it "Lick-H-alpha two-three-four"). It is a pre-main-sequence star — a stellar teenager that is still gathering mass before it starts fusing hydrogen like our Sun.

This central star is so energetic that it blows powerful outflows — streams of hot gas shooting out from its poles. These outflows blow a large, golden cavity in the dusty cloud, which appears like a glowing bubble in Webb's image. The golden color comes from hot, atomic hydrogen gas that the star's light has energized. To the right of the cavity, red clouds of cooler gas have been shocked and stirred by the same stellar winds.

3. When Stars Are Born, Others Follow

It might sound surprising, but sometimes a baby star's birth triggers more baby stars to form! Here is how it works:

As LkH-alpha 234 and other young stars push gas away with their winds, parts of the cloud become squished and compressed. When a cold cloud of gas gets squeezed hard enough, gravity pulls it together even tighter — eventually forming new stars. This is called triggered star formation, and it is happening all around the golden cavity in NGC 7129.

Some of these newborn stars are so young they are still surrounded by thick disks of material. These protostars are the very first stage of a star's life — even before they reach the main sequence stage where our Sun currently sits.

Try this at home

You can set up a simple experiment to see how stars form! Mix water with food coloring in a clear jar, then sprinkle in some glitter. The glitter settles slowly, just like dust and gas in space gradually clumping together under gravity. For a real-world view, download a free planetarium app like Stellarium, find the constellation Cepheus, and look in the area of NGC 7129 on a dark night. Ask a grown-up to help you explore NASA's James Webb images at https://webbtelescope.org.

Fun Fact

NGC 7129 is nicknamed the Flower Bud Nebula because, in visible-light images, it looks like a pink rosebud! The young stars inside act like cosmic gardeners — their intense light and winds sculpt the surrounding gas and dust into beautiful, flowing shapes, just like wind shapes sand dunes on Earth.
