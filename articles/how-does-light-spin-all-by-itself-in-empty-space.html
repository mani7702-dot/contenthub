How Does Light Spin All By Itself in Empty Space?

2026-11-013 min

A beam of light can start out completely still and then, all on its own, grow twirling patches as it travels through empty space — no lenses, mirrors or special materials required.

1. Light that packs its own suitcase

Most of us picture light as something that just zooms in a straight line. But scientists can also build structured light — beams whose brightness, shape and direction are carefully arranged, like a marching band instead of a crowd.

One wild version twists as it travels, forming a corkscrew shape called an optical vortex. Another kind spins as it moves, and that spin comes in two mirror-image flavours: left-handed and right-handed. That "handedness" has a fancy name — chirality.

Until now, making light behave this way meant borrowing help: special surfaces, exotic materials, or powerful lenses focusing a beam extremely tightly. A team at the University of East Anglia in the UK, working with researchers at the University of the Witwatersrand in South Africa, has just shown light can do it entirely on its own.

2. A beam that hides its spin

Here's the surprising part. When the scientists prepared a beam in a certain balanced way, it began with no spin at all. But as it travelled forward through plain empty space, little spinning regions appeared and drifted apart.

"It starts off with no spin at all," explained student Light Mkhumbuza, who ran key experiments. "But as the beam travels forward, spinning regions appear and separate out — almost as if the spin was hiding and then revealed itself."

No mirrors. No lenses. No engineered materials. The beam simply unfolds a property it was secretly carrying all along.

3. The doughnut trick

So how does light "know" where to spin? The answer lies in topology — a branch of maths about shapes that stay the same even when you stretch or squash them.

"Imagine a mug and a doughnut," said Dr Isaac Nape of Wits. "You can morph one into the other without tearing it, because they both have one hole."

Light, it turns out, has its own version of that "hole count," hidden in the pattern of how its waves are polarised. Like a fingerprint, that pattern survives as the beam changes shape — and it quietly decides where the left- and right-handed spins pop up.

4. Why it matters

Because the effect needs no fragile materials, it could make light-based tools simpler and cheaper. Many medicines come in left- and right-handed forms that behave differently in the body, so this trick could sharpen medical tests and drug development. It could also help squeeze more data into laser beams for faster, more secure communication — and maybe even protect fragile signals in future quantum technologies.

Fun fact: The student who carried out the key experiments is actually named Light — Light Mkhumbuza.

As Dr Kayn Forbes put it: "For something so familiar, light is proving to be far richer, stranger and more powerful than anyone imagined."
