How Did 400,000-Year-Old Teeth Link Early Humans to Us Today?

2026-10-31T05:03:00+05:303 min

Tooth proteins pulled from 400,000-year-old Homo erectus molars are revealing quiet family ties between an early human ancestor, the Denisovans, and some of us alive today.

Question

What do proteins locked inside a set of 400,000-year-old molars tell us about who we are?

Three short sections below — plus a fun fact — explain why these ancient teeth matter to all of us.

1. Reading fossils without destroying them

Homo erectus was the first human in our genus to straggle out of Africa and settle across Eurasia, so its bones sit at a quiet crossroads of our family story. But bones and teeth rarely hand over DNA after that much time, and researchers cannot simply grind a fossil to dust — these remains are both scientifically irreplaceable and culturally precious.

A team led by Fu Qiaomei at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing found a gentler way: a micro-sampling acid-etch method that lifts molecular information from the enamel surface while leaving the tooth's shape perfectly intact. They applied it to six Homo erectus teeth from three Chinese sites — Zhoukoudian (the classic Peking Man locality), Hexian, and Sunjiadong — and pulled out recognizable fragments of enamel proteins.

2. Two mutations, two stories

One of the protein fragments carried a previously unknown quirk called AMBN-A253G. Because the same small change appeared in all six teeth from all three sites, the researchers have their first solid hint that these far-apart Homo erectus groups belonged to one shared population — a single, regionally connected family rather than isolated clumps.

The second mutation, AMBN-M273V, was once thought to be a private signature of the Denisovans, the archaic humans we mostly know from a finger bone and a few teeth in a Siberian cave. Finding the very same mark in the Homo erectus teeth suggests the variant did not start with the Denisovans after all — it flowed the other way, from Homo erectus into the Denisovan lineage through interbreeding long ago.

3. A legacy passed down to us

Denisovans themselves vanished, but their genes live on in people today, especially among populations across Southeast Asia and Oceania. If the shared variant entered the Denisovan line from Homo erectus, then a whisper of this ancient group may have survived all the way down to the present — carried along not in bones, but in the blood of millions of living humans.

The work, published in Nature (DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10478-8), is as much about method as discovery: each new technique for reading fossils gently makes the next breakthrough possible.

Fun fact: Enamel is the hardest tissue in the human body — harder than bone — which is exactly why protein fragments can cling to it for hundreds of thousands of years while everything else decays.
