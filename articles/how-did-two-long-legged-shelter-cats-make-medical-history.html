How Did Two Long-Legged Shelter Cats Make Medical History?

2026-10-313 min

Two shelter brothers with unusually long legs turned out to be the first cats ever documented with a rare condition known mostly in humans — and their story is teaching veterinarians something new.

How Did Two Long-Legged Shelter Cats Make Medical History?

Brothers Gary and Shaggy arrived at a shelter as ordinary-looking kittens — except for one thing. Their legs were remarkably long. The pair were littermates, adopted together, and as they stretched and grew, their lanky limbs kept turning heads. What nobody guessed was that these two shelter cats were about to help scientists write a first-of-its-kind chapter in veterinary medicine.

The kittens that stood out

Gary and Shaggy's legs weren't just a little long. Their shin bones, forearm bones and paw bones were each roughly 40% longer than those of an average male domestic shorthair cat. As the brothers grew, veterinarians also noticed unusual eye structures and changes to the body's main artery. Those clues together pointed toward something familiar from human medicine: Marfan syndrome.

A genetic detective story

Researchers at Cornell University went to work, examining both cats carefully and reading their DNA. They found changes in a gene called FBN1, which carries the instructions for fibrillin-1 — a protein that helps hold connective tissue together, supporting blood vessels, bones, ligaments, skin and eyes.

In people, a change in just one copy of FBN1 can be enough to cause the condition. Gary and Shaggy each carried two altered copies, one inherited from each parent — an exceptionally rare situation. Yet the brothers' particular version of the gene didn't switch off completely. It still allowed some normal activity, which helps explain how both cats lived comfortably into adulthood. Their case, published in the journal Scientific Reports, is the first detailed genetic characterisation of Marfan syndrome in domestic cats.

Why this story matters

Marfan syndrome affects about 1 in 4,000 people, but until now it had never been confirmed in felines. By describing Gary and Shaggy's unusual genes, the team has given veterinarians a foundation for spotting similar cases earlier — and possibly developing genetic tests. "It can help veterinarians recognise similar cases in the future," said senior author Dr. Jacquelyn Evans.

Fun fact

Marfan syndrome is linked to the same protein that gives your skin and blood vessels their stretch — which is why people (and cats) with the condition often tend to be unusually tall or long-limbed.
