How did 1,000 bison come to roam free across 600,000 acres of Montana prairie?

2026-10-213 min

On a vast rewilded prairie in Montana, a herd of free-roaming American bison has just passed 1,000 individuals — a milestone for the largest private conservation project in the US, working to restore a landscape where tens of millions once thundered.

How did 1,000 bison come to roam free across 600,000 acres of Montana prairie?

Close your eyes and imagine standing on an endless sea of grass. Now imagine that sea moving — because it's made of tens of thousands of bison, hooves thundering, heads bobbing, a living river stretching as far as the eye can see.

That's what North America looked like not so long ago. But today, a heroic effort in Montana is bringing back a piece of that ancient world — and their herd just hit a huge milestone.

From 30 million to 325 — and back again

Long, long ago, tens of millions of American bison roamed the Great Plains. Hunters piled bones higher than buildings. But by the 1880s, greed and guns had wiped out almost all of them. In just a few decades, the greatest herd the world had ever seen shrank to 325 animals in a single Texas pasture.

Today, there are about 500,000 bison — less than 2% of the original population. And get this: fewer than 1 in 10 of those bison live in true wild conditions. Most are in farms or small fenced herds that can't grow strong or healthy the way nature intended.

A bold dream: one million acres of wild prairie

In northern Montana, a group called American Prairie Reserve is trying to change all that. Their mission? To create the largest patch of truly wild prairie in the continental United States — a place where nature can run free without fences.

They've already stitched together 603,657 acres of grassland, hills, and waterways. That's bigger than 900 Central Parks put together! Their dream is to protect 3.2 million acres — an area so huge that if it became a national park, it would rank in the top 10 largest in the entire lower 48 states.

The herd that passed 1,000

When the team did their annual calf count at the end of the breeding season this year, they counted exactly 1,016 bison. That's the first time the herd has ever crossed the 1,000 mark — a major victory for a project that's been growing the herd for over 20 years.

The bison roam freely across the reserve, not locked in small pens. They choose their own mates, follow ancient migration patterns, and help shape the prairie ecosystem the way they have for thousands of years. It's how bison lived before the railroads and fences — and it's how they need to live to stay healthy.

The team's next goal? A herd of 5,000 bison. With 5,000 square miles of habitat, the reserve could eventually support tens of thousands — a fraction of what once was, but a powerful step toward restoring a lost world.

Not just bison — an entire ecosystem

As the bison herd grows, something magical happens: the whole prairie wakes up. Birds nest in the grass where bison hooves tear the soil. Bees find new flowers blooming in the trampled patches. Wolves and bears return to hunt. Even the soil itself changes, richer and more alive.

American Prairie's bison aren't just animals in a herd — they're engineers of an entire ecosystem, helping rebuild what 150 years of grazing and farming had broken.

Try this at home

Bison counting game: Print or draw 100 dots on a page (or count 100 beans, buttons, or coins). Group them into 10 groups of 10. That's how scientists count big animals in the wild — they count a sample area, then multiply. If you counted 10 bison in a square-acre patch, and the herd covers 600,000 acres... how many bison might live there?

Grassland in a jar: Layer soil, sand, and small stones in a clear jar. Add a pinch of grass seed. Watch how water flows through layers just like it does across the prairie — and think about how bison hooves help keep that soil healthy and alive.

Fun Fact

Bison are living tanks — their heads alone weigh as much as a house cat! When a stampede starts, each adult bison can run up to 35 miles per hour for short bursts. That's faster than most humans can sprint. And when a herd runs together, the ground actually shakes — which is why early railroad workers said it felt like the earth was rumbling before they ever saw the animals coming over the horizon.
