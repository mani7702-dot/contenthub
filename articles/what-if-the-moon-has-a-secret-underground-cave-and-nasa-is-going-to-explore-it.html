What if the Moon has a secret underground cave — and NASA is going to explore it?

What if the Moon has a secret underground cave — and NASA is going to explore it?

A giant hole hiding a secret tunnel

The Moon looks peaceful from Earth — a bright, quiet disk hanging in our night sky. But hidden in its shadows, there is a mystery waiting to be uncovered.

In a region of the Moon called the Marius Hills, there is a large, circular hole. It is not a crater made by a space rock. It is something far more fascinating: a pit crater that might open into a giant underground tunnel — a lunar lava tube.

NASA has just chosen a new mission to investigate this hole up close. If a hidden cavern really does stretch away beneath the surface, it could become humanity's first underground home on another world.

How does a moon cave form?

Long ago, the Moon was a very different place. Rivers of hot, bubbling lava flowed across its surface, like something from a fantasy movie.

When lava flows across the ground, the top layer cools and hardens first, forming a rocky crust — kind of like the skin that forms on warm pudding. But underneath, the lava keeps flowing, hollowing out a tunnel beneath that protective shell. Once the lava drains away, it leaves behind a long, hollow cave: a lava tube.

These tubes can be remarkably big. Scientists estimate that lunar lava tubes could be as wide as 500 meters — that is half a kilometer, or about five football fields side by side! And sometimes, the roof of the tube collapses, leaving a circular hole called a skylight that astronauts can see from orbit.

Why would astronauts want to live in a moon cave?

At first, you might think a dark hole on the Moon sounds scary. But to astronauts planning to stay there for months or even years, a lunar cave could be a lifesaver.

On the Moon's surface, astronauts face two big dangers:

1. Radiation — without a magnetic field like Earth's, harmful cosmic rays and solar particles blast down from space.

2. Extreme temperatures — the lunar day reaches 127 °C (260 °F) in the sunlight and plunges to -173 °C (-280 °F) in the darkness.

A lava tube buried underground would act like a natural shield, protecting astronauts from both radiation and temperature swings. Instead of building thick walls from scratch, humans could simply build inside a cave that the Moon made for free billions of years ago.

NASA's new GIMLI mission (Geophysical Instruments for Marius Lunar pit Investigation) will carry radar, seismic sensors, and cameras down to the Marius Hills pit to scan for exactly this kind of hollow space.

Try this at home

Build a lava tube model. Fill a clear jar halfway with warm water. Add a spoonful of cocoa powder and stir — that's your "lava." Now carefully pour a thin layer of oil on top (the "crust"). Watch how the oil floats while the cocoa-water mixture flows underneath. If you tilt the jar, you can see the "lava" moving beneath the "crust" — just like a lava tube on the Moon!

Fun Fact

The Marius Hills are not just random bumps on the Moon. They are a group of volcanic domes — the highest concentration of volcanic features anywhere on the lunar surface! The hills sit in a region called Oceanus Procellarum, and the pit crater itself is about 65 meters (213 feet) wide — roughly the size of a basketball court.
