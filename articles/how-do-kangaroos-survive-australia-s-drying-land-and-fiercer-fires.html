How do kangaroos survive Australia's drying land and fiercer fires?

2026-10-263 min

Meet Australia's hopping heroes

When you think of Australia, you probably picture a kangaroo standing up on its powerful back legs, tail curled like a fluffy question mark. There are actually 59 different kinds of kangaroos and their close cousins — the macropods — living across the huge, dry continent of Australia.

The four most famous are the red kangaroo (the biggest), the eastern grey, the western grey, and the antilopine kangaroo. Together, their numbers total somewhere between 30 million and 50 million — more than the human population of Australia!

How do they beat the heat?

Australia has just had 14 of its 15 warmest years on record, with long droughts and scary bushfires. During one terrible 10-year drought starting in 2000, kangaroo numbers in eastern Australia dropped by about 30 million. But kangaroos are clever survivors — by 2014, their numbers bounced back to around 47 million.

The secret? Big kangaroos can go without water for a long time, they get most of what they need from plants, and when it gets too hot they loll in the shade and lick their paws to cool down. Red kangaroos are built for the driest parts of the land — that is why they are the largest and most heat-tolerant.

The little ones need help

The biggest kangaroos are doing fine, but their smaller cousins are in trouble. The Gilbert's potoroo — a tiny wallaby-like marsupial — has fewer than 150 animals left in the wild in Western Australia. The mala (also called the rufous hare-wallaby) disappeared from the Australian mainland back in 1991 because of fires and predators, and only tiny island groups remain.

Scientists say these small macropods cannot bounce back the way the big kangaroos do. A single severe fire can wipe out a whole local group, and hotter, drier weather from climate change makes it even harder.

Fighting for the future

Australian researchers are working on ways to help. They are using Indigenous fire-management practices — letting people who have cared for this land for thousands of years guide when and where fires are set, so they stay gentle instead of racing out of control. They are also restoring natural waterways, protecting tree cover, and making sure native animals have quiet places to hide and drink.

So this World Kangaroo Day, we can all celebrate the bouncy, resilient kangaroos — and remember that the smallest among them need safe paths, water, and shelter to thrive alongside the biggest.

Fun fact

A group of kangaroos is called a mob! No, really. If you see five kangaroos together, you have yourself a mob.

Try this at home

Draw a big red kangaroo hopping across a wide page. Add a thermometer showing the temperature rising, then draw shady spots (trees, rocks) where the kangaroo can rest. Make a small Gilbert's potoroo too — and a sign saying "Help the little ones!" You just learned why size matters when it comes to surviving a changing climate.
