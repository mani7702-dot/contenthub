Can a molecule hit the ultimate speed limit for staying 'quantum'?

2026-10-213 min

Scientists created an ultra-clean crystal surface where individual molecules kept their delicate quantum properties until the very limit physics allows — the first time that has happened for anything sitting on a surface.

The quantum speed limit

Everything tiny in our universe has a kind of "quantum battery." An atom or a molecule can hold a special kind of energy for only so long before it naturally lets it go — like a raindrop that can stay balanced on a leaf for only so many seconds before it falls.

That maximum "held-together" time is called the Fourier limit. It is not a rule scientists made up — it is a feature of physics itself, set by the same uncertainty principle that says you cannot know a particle's exact position and speed at the same time. The faster something changes energy, the shorter its "quantum memory" lasts.

Why surfaces usually ruin quantum tricks

Many future gadgets — ultra-secure messages, lightning-fast quantum computers — depend on single atoms or molecules that can glow with pure, perfect light. Scientists call these quantum emitters.

The trouble is that most quantum experiments need ultra-clean, super-cold places (almost a vacuum). But a surface — like the tip of a microscopic probe — would be far more convenient. The problem: surfaces are usually dirty and shaky. A single stray gas molecule hitting the surface jolts the quantum emitter, and its delicate "quantumness" vanishes in a flash, long before the Fourier limit.

A crystal that cleans itself

Scientists at the Max Planck Institute in Germany found a clever fix. They used a special organic crystal that, at room temperature, slowly sublimates — it turns from a solid straight into a gas, layer by layer, like a bar of soap that wears away evenly.

As the top layers drifted off into the vacuum chamber, they took the surface's dirt and grime with them. What was left was a surface so clean and so still that they could cool it to just a hair above absolute zero (-273 °C) and place single molecules onto it.

Then they watched the molecules. Instead of losing their quantum properties in a heartbeat, the molecules hung on to them until they hit the very edge allowed by physics — the Fourier limit itself. This is the first time anyone has reached that ceiling while the emitter was sitting on a surface.

Why this matters

Reaching the limit means the technique works. If you can put a single molecule on a surface and keep it quantum for the maximum time physics allows, that surface is basically a perfect home for future quantum gadgets. It could let scientists "touch" and tune individual molecules with atomic-scale tips, building sensors and computers one glowing molecule at a time.

It also shows that a quiet, cold, self-cleaning surface is one of the best homes in nature for keeping a system quantum — even better than trapping things in a vacuum with magnetic fields.

Try this at home

You can model a quantum "speed limit" with a slinky or a long spring. Hold one end still and send a wave down it — the pulse travels at a fixed speed and keeps its shape. Now try sending two pulses that are almost identical: they spread out and blur over time, like a raindrop losing its balance. That spread is a bit like how a quantum system loses its edge. Keep the slinky still and smooth (like a clean, cold crystal) and the pulse stays sharp longer. (Adult supervision for the slinky-swinging experiment, please!)

Fun Fact

Absolute zero — the coldest temperature possible — is -273.15 °C (-459.67 °F). The researchers cooled their crystal to just a few degrees above that, where even atoms barely jiggle.
