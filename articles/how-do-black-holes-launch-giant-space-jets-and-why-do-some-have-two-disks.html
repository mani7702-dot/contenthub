How Do Black Holes Launch Giant Space Jets — and Why Do Some Have Two Disks?

2026-10-244 min

When a supermassive black hole swallows gas, it sometimes fires twin jets of energy into space. New X-ray images show these jets are powered by an inner hot disk sitting inside a cooler outer disk — a two-layer arrangement that solves a decades-old puzzle.

Why do black hole jets need a hot inner disk — and not a cold one?

1. The galaxy with a cosmic firehose

Picture a galaxy where a monster is sleeping at its heart. This monster is a supermassive black hole — millions or billions of times heavier than our Sun, but crushed into a space no bigger than a city. When gas and dust fall toward it, they don't fall straight in. Instead, they whirl around like water going down a drain, flattening into a bright, flat disk called an accretion disk.

Sometimes — sometimes — that black hole fires two giant jets of energy from its poles, shooting out at nearly the speed of light. These aren't just pretty lights: they are cosmic fire hoses that can blow from one end of a galaxy to the other, shaping how stars form and how galaxies grow.

Astronomers call these systems active galactic nuclei (AGN). One of the best-studied is 3C 120, a galaxy about 420 million light-years away in the constellation Taurus. It is the kind of place where these fiery jets blaze bright enough for our telescopes to study them in stunning detail.

2. The puzzle of the two disks

Here is the mystery scientists have wrestled with for decades. The leading theory says these jets are powered by the Blandford-Znajek mechanism — a process where the black hole's spin and its enormous magnetic fields team up to fling energy out into space. But that mechanism needs large-scale magnetic fields, and those only form easily inside a hot, thick flow of gas.

The problem? Many of the brightest, most jet-happy black holes are gulping gas at a furious rate. When they eat that fast, they are usually surrounded by a cold, thin disk — the kind that can't hold onto the magnetic fields the jets need.

It looks like a contradiction. A cold disk and hot jets — how do they go together?

3. X-ray eyes reveal the hidden layer

To solve the puzzle, a team of astronomers turned their most powerful telescopes on 3C 120. Using Japan's XRISM X-ray telescope, plus radio cameras that can see finer than a human hair held at arm's length, they mapped the region right around the black hole.

What they found: the cold disk does not go all the way in. It stops about 20 "gravitational radii away from the black hole — that's roughly the distance from Earth's surface to space, but measured in units of the black hole's own size. Inside that boundary, the thin cold disk is replaced by a hot, thick flow of gas.

That hot inner flow is where the magnetic fields gather. And right at the boundary — where the cold disk ends and the hot flow begins — the radio images show the jet starts to narrow, pointing straight back to the same spot. The two layers, cold on the outside and hot on the inside, are the missing link that powers the jet.

4. A rule written in starlight

This means the story is not "cold disk" or "hot flow" — it is both, in layers. The cold outer disk feeds the hot inner flow, and the hot inner flow launches the jet. That arrangement works even when the black hole is eating fast and messy, which explains how the most dramatic jets can coexist with the most aggressive feeds.

Try this at home

Fill a clear bowl with water and sprinkle in a pinch of cinnamon — that is your "cold disk." Now stir gently with a spoon so the cinnamon swirls into a thick, cloudy center. The swirling center is your hot inner flow. Shine a flashlight through the water and watch how the cinnamon traces two different layers — just like the two disk layers around this black hole.

Fun Fact

The name 3C 120 comes from the Third Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources — it was the 120th object listed when astronomers first mapped thousands of radio-bright sky objects in the 1950s. And despite being 420 million light-years away, its jet appears to move faster than light across our sky. That is not real faster-than-light travel; it is a trick of perspective, like a jet that seems to streak across the heavens but is only taking one step at a time.

for-grown-ups: Supermassive black holes launch relativistic jets via the Blandford-Znajek mechanism, which extracts rotational energy using large-scale magnetic fields anchored in the accretion flow. Cold, geometrically thin disks cannot sustain such fields, creating a paradox in high-accretion systems with strong jets. Coordinated X-ray spectroscopy by XRISM and radio/VLBI imaging (GMVA, VLBA, EAVN) of the broad-line radio galaxy 3C 120 (z=0.033, ~420 Mly, MBH ~ 6.3e7 M_sun) revealed a relativistically broadened Fe K-alpha line, showing the thin disk truncates at ~20 gravitational radii (R_g), with the inner region replaced by a hot, geometrically thick flow. Jet radius extrapolation toward the horizon matches the truncation radius, confirming jet launching is linked to the hot inner flow via the Blandford-Znajek process. The paper "Disk Truncation Triggers Relativistic Jet Launching of a Highly Accreting Supermassive Black Hole" by Noda & Hada et al. (arXiv:2609.27057) was accepted for publication in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
