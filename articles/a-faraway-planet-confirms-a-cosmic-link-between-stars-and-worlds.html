A Faraway Planet Confirms a Cosmic Link Between Stars and Worlds

2026-10-26T09:41+05:303 min

A scorching hot planet over 300 light-years away shares the same magnesium-to-silicon ratio as its parent star, confirming a long-held belief that helps scientists hunt for potentially habitable worlds.

1. A Planet 320 Light-Years Away Confirms a Cosmic Family Link

Imagine if you and your pet goldfish shared the exact same recipe for cookies — same flour, same sugar, same everything. That is basically what scientists discovered about a distant planet and its star. The planet, named WASP-189b, is like a cosmic sibling that inherited the same chemical "mix" from the same cosmic kitchen.

What is WASP-189b?

About 320 light-years away in the constellation Libra, there orbits a star called WASP-189. And circling that star is a planet named WASP-189b. But this is no ordinary planet — it is a member of a class called ultra-hot Jupiters, which are enormous worlds so close to their stars that they reach scorching temperatures where even rocks can melt into gas.

Because it is so hot, WASP-189b's atmosphere contains gases that let scientists study its chemical makeup. Using a special instrument called IGRINS (the Immersion GRATING INfrared Spectrograph) on the Gemini South telescope in Chile, a research team measured two elements floating in that atmosphere: magnesium and silicon.

Why do stars and planets share the same chemistry?

For a long time, scientists assumed that a star and the planets forming around it would have similar proportions of rock-forming elements. After all, planets are born from the same swirling disk of gas and dust that surrounds a young star. But until now, this idea was only checked inside our own Solar System.

The new study found something exciting: the amount of magnesium compared to silicon in WASP-189b's atmosphere is almost identical to the ratio in its parent star. This is the first time anyone has measured this cosmic "family resemblance" directly on a planet outside our Solar System.

How does this help find habitable worlds?

Knowing that a star's chemistry mirrors its planets is like having a shortcut for astronomers. Instead of visiting each distant world, they can look at a star and make a good guess about what its planets are made of. Rockier planets need the right mix of metals and minerals to build mountains, volcanoes, and maybe even the conditions that let life thrive.

This discovery also tells us something beautiful: the same ingredients that made Earth — magnesium, silicon, iron, and more — are sprinkled throughout the galaxy in predictable patterns. Every star with planets is like a cosmic recipe book, and we are just learning how to read it.

Try this at home

You can see WASP-189b's constellation, Libra, with your naked eye on a clear night! Look for a small, faint patch of stars shaped like a triangle or shield in the southern sky. While WASP-189b itself is invisible (it orbits too close to its star and is lost in the star's glare), you can use apps like SkySafari or Stellarium to find the star. Point a telescope at Libra and imagine the thousands of planets astronomers have discovered there — each one a world with its own story.

Fun Fact

WASP-189b is so hot — about 3,470 degrees Kelvin (that is over 3,200 degrees Celsius or 5,800 degrees Fahrenheit) — that any metal in its atmosphere exists as a gas. If you could stand on a heat-resistant platform millions of miles away and look at the planet, you would see a glowing, orange-red glow. Scientists think the planet's atmosphere might even contain vaporized iron and titanium oxide!
