1,000 feet of cliffs on Mars: What's Curiosity's mystery layer on Mount Sharp?

2026-10-223 min

1,000 feet of cliffs on Mars: What's Curiosity's mystery layer on Mount Sharp?

A rover's eye view of Mars' giant sand sculptures

High up on the slopes of Mount Sharp — a mountain so big it would dwarf any peak on Earth — NASA's Curiosity rover has captured one of the most breathtaking panoramas ever sent back from the Red Planet.

The photo, taken on August 11, 2026 (sol 4982), shows a field of strange, streamlined cliffs called yardangs. They stretch across roughly 10 miles of the Martian surface, like the teeth of a giant comb carved by an invisible hand. Curiosity snapped the image with its Mastcam — a camera that rides high on the rover's mast — at 8:30 a.m. local Mars time.

Unlike most photos Curiosity sends home, these shots kept their raw Martian colors, including a haunting blue haze in the foreground. That blue tint is real: fine dust high in the atmosphere scatters light just like it does on Earth, turning sunrise and sunset skies a deep, dreamy blue.

Climbing a giant made of mud and time

Curiosity hasn't just been taking pretty pictures — it has been climbing. Since it landed inside Gale Crater in 2012, the rover has been making its way up Mount Sharp (officially named Aeolis Mons), layer by layer.

The mountain is 5.5 kilometers (about 3 miles) tall — taller than any peak in the contiguous United States. But more incredible than its height is what it is made of: layer upon layer of dried mud and sand, each stripe a chapter in Mars' long-lost story of lakes, rivers, and maybe even rain.

So far, Curiosity has climbed over 740 meters (2,430 feet) in elevation — higher than any other rover on Mars. Each stop gives scientists a new page from the planet's history book.

The mystery layer that "looks out of place"

Not far ahead of where Curiosity stands today are those mysterious yardang cliffs. But something about them puzzles the science team. The rock layer holding them up is a different color from everything around it, and its stripes tilt at an angle no one expected.

"The yardang layer looks out of place," says Ashwin Vasavada, Curiosity's project scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. "It's the wrong color, the layers tilt at an odd angle, and it almost appears plastered on."

One theory? This layer might be ancient volcanic ash — powder from long-ago eruptions that rained down and hardened in place. If so, it would be a rare snapshot of Mars' volcanic past mixed in with the sedimentary layers scientists have been reading like a diary.

Curiosity is expected to reach the base of the yardangs in 2027, when it will study the rocks up close with its robotic arm, searching for chemical clues that could confirm — or rewrite — the story.

Try this at home

Stack three sheets of colored paper on top of each other, but tilt the middle one at an odd angle. Now blow sandpaper dust (or fine flour) across the layers using a hair dryer. Watch how the eraser-shaped dunes carve grooves — just like wind carving yardangs on Mars! Flip the stack over and you have a mini Mount Sharp: a mountain of layered stories waiting to be read.

Fun Fact

The material that makes up Mount Sharp has a special ingredient: jarosite, a mineral that only forms in the presence of acidic water. Finding it told scientists that Mars once had sulfuric acid rain — meaning the Red Planet was a very different, and much wetter, world billions of years ago.
