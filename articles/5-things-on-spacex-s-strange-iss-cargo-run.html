5 Things on SpaceX's Strange ISS Cargo Run

What did SpaceX deliver to the ISS this week — tiny brain models, 3D heart tissue, and the station's final solar arrays? Here are the five most fascinating experiments riding orbit right now.

1. Brain Organoids

Scientists sent tiny 3D brain models called organoids to the ISS. These miniature brains (grown from real cells) let researchers study brain biology in microgravity — something hard to do on Earth. They hope to learn more about conditions like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's. In space, without Earth's gravity pulling and settling things, brain tissue may grow and behave differently, which could reveal new treatment ideas.

2. 3D Heart Tissue

Doctors are testing 3D heart tissue models to see how the heart works in microgravity. These models copy important parts of real heart tissue and could help scientists test new medicines quickly during long space missions. The heart has to work harder in space, so understanding this helps protect both astronauts and people on Earth.

3. Artificial Retina Factory

SpaceX carried equipment to manufacture artificial retinas in microgravity. The idea: making these tiny sight-restoring devices in space might produce better results than on Earth, because without gravity pulling things around, the materials can form smoother, more precise structures. If it works, it could help restore vision for people with certain types of blindness.

4. Strange New Glass

Materials scientists are studying how a new type of glass forms in zero gravity. On Earth, gravity causes liquids to settle and separate, which affects how glass crystals form. In space, that disturbance is gone — so researchers can watch the glass form more naturally. The results might lead to better fiber optics, phone screens, and even medical devices.

5. Final Solar Arrays

The cargo included the last set of upgraded solar panels for the ISS — the IROSA units. These panels will expand the station's power supply so the crew can keep running science experiments and life-support systems. They're being installed later this fall, and once in place they'll help the ISS keep orbiting strong.

Try this at home

Build a simple "organoid" model using gelatin, sugar, and food coloring — the way the sugar crystals form patterns in the gel mimics how living cells organize themselves. Place it near a sunny window and watch how the crystals change as the gel melts. Ask an adult to help!

Fun Fact

The ISS orbits about 250 miles (400 km) above Earth and flies at 17,500 mph — that's fast enough to go around the whole planet in just 90 minutes. It's like a house-shaped lab the size of football field (with solar arrays) circling the world 15 times a day.
