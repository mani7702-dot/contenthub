How does a spider turn a silk web into a catapult that catches ants?

2026-10-243 min

A newly discovered Australian spider has built a spring-loaded silk trap that flings itself — or its web — at aggressive green tree ants, making it the first spider known to let its prey trigger its own capture.

How does a spider turn a silk web into a catapult that catches ants?

Imagine a trap so cleverly built that the moment its target walks into it, the trap springs itself. That is exactly what a newly discovered Australian spider has done — it turned its silk web into a tiny catapult, and the ant that triggers it never sees it coming.

A web that fires on its own

Most spiders build a quiet, sticky web and wait for dinner to fly in. But this new spider has taken a different path. It constructs a spring-loaded silk snare — think of a miniature torsion spring or catapult made entirely of spider silk. The web is carefully tensioned, storing energy like a stretched rubber band.

When an ant comes along to investigate the web, the ant itself pulls the trigger. Stepping onto the silk releases the stored energy in a flash, and the trap fires — flinging part of the web (or the spider itself) at the prey. The researchers say this is the very first spider known to build a web designed for one single prey species, and the very first where the prey activates the trap.

Why target the green tree ant?

The spider's chosen victim is the green tree ant (Oecophylla smaragdina) — a fierce, tree-dwelling insect found in Australia and Southeast Asia. These ants are famous for aggressively defending their territory. They live in nests woven from leaves and patrol the branches day and night, hunting smaller insects to bring back to their colonies.

By targeting only green tree ants, this spider has found a clever niche. The ants are strong enough to be challenging prey, but they are also predictable — they follow regular routes through the trees. A trap built specifically for them can be tuned to the right size, the right tension, and the right timing.

How spider silk becomes a spring

Here is the science magic: spider silk is a protein fiber that is both incredibly strong and surprisingly stretchy. When you stretch a spring, it stores elastic potential energy — energy that is released the moment you let go. The same thing happens with this spider's silk trap. The spider carefully winds and tensions the silk until it is ready to snap.

When the ant's weight pulls the trigger, that stored energy releases in a fraction of a second. The silk fires forward, wrapping the ant in a tangle of protein threads before it can sound the alarm to its colony.

Try this at home

You can build a simple version of this trap with a rubber band and two craft sticks. Hold the rubber band stretched between the sticks — that is your "spring-loaded silk." When you let go, the sticks snap together, just like the spider's web fires shut. Can you think of other things around the house that store and release energy this way? (Think: slingshots, wind-up toys, or even a snapped towel!)

Fun Fact

This is not the first clever trick spiders have invented. Bolas spiders swing a sticky blob on a string to catch moths mid-air. Some spiders use mimicry — evolving to look like something else to sneak up on prey. But this new discovery is special: it is the first time scientists have seen a spider build a trap that is tuned for just one species, and the first where the prey itself sets it off!

The short version: researchers (reported by Sam Lee via Mongabay, October 23, 2026) describe a newly discovered Australian "ballista spider" that builds a spring-loaded silk snare targeting the green tree ant (Oecophylla smaragdina). The trap uses elastic potential energy stored in tensioned spider silk protein fibers; when an ant steps onto the trigger, the silk releases its stored energy in milliseconds, flinging the web or spider at the prey. This is the first known spider whose trap is designed for a single prey species, and the first where the prey activates the trap. The mechanism resembles a biological catapult or torsion spring, consistent with how spider silk (a block co-polymer of spidroins rich in glycine and alanine) can store and release elastic energy — verified against Wikipedia entries on Spider_web, Spring_(mechanical), and Elastic_potential_energy. The green tree ant is a eusocial insect native to Australia and Southeast Asia, known for aggressive territorial defense and leaf-woven tree nests — confirmed via Wikipedia's Weaver_ants article. The broader evolutionary strategy of species-specific predation aligns with Wikipedia's Mimicry article on how predators evolve specialized hunting adaptations. The spider's family-level taxonomy is pending formal description; the term "ballista spider" refers to the catapult-like mechanism, not a formal taxonomic assignment.
