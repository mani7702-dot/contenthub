What Did a Balloon Telescope See in the Sun's Hidden Magnetic Web?

2026-10-304 min

A balloon-borne solar observatory called Sunrise-III soared 35 kilometers above Earth in 2024 to peer at the Sun's quiet regions. What it found: an intricate magnetic canopy of thread-like structures woven through the solar atmosphere, revealing how our star's hidden magnetic wiring may help heat its outer layers.

What Did a Balloon Telescope See in the Sun's Hidden Magnetic Web?

1. A balloon's eye view of our nearest star

When you look up at the Sun in the daytime sky, it appears as a blindingly bright, featureless disk. But the Sun is far from simple. Beneath its surface, invisible magnetic forces churn and twist. And now, thanks to a balloon-borne telescope soaring high above Earth, scientists have revealed a secret network of magnetic threads hidden in the Sun's seemingly quiet regions.

The mission was Sunrise-III, a NASA/ESA international collaboration that lifted off in 2024 aboard a giant high-altitude balloon. Floating approximately 35 kilometers (22 miles) above Earth in the stratosphere — well above the layer where airplanes fly — the telescope drifted above most of our atmosphere's blurring turbulence. From this clear vantage point, it captured the Sun's magnetic fields in unprecedented detail.

2. The magnetic threads above "quiet" Sun

Most of the Sun's surface falls into regions scientists call "quiet" — areas that lack the dramatic sunspots and solar flares that grab headlines. Yet "quiet" doesn't mean inactive. The Sunrise-III team, led by the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ), discovered that even these calm regions contain a surprisingly complex magnetic canopy.

Rather than smooth, uniformly expanding magnetic fields, the researchers found the chromosphere — a reddish layer of the Sun's atmosphere visible during eclipses — is woven through with countless narrow, thread-like magnetic structures. It's like discovering a hidden fishing net of invisible strings stretched across a vast expanse, each thread shaped by twisting motions on the Sun's surface that reach upward into space.

Think of it like wind gusts on a lazy summer day. From the ground, the air seems still, but looking more closely, you'd see eddies and swirls invisible to the naked eye. The Sun's quiet regions are the same — their magnetic "weather" is far more intricate than it appears.

3. How does this energy rise?

The Sun's surface (the photosphere) is where magnetic fields are strongest and most concentrated. As these fields spread outward and arch upward into the chromosphere, they create what scientists call the magnetic canopy. But Sunrise-III's new observations showed this canopy isn't smooth — it's full of twists and turns, with magnetic field lines that spiral and braid as they rise.

Computer simulations confirmed that twisting motions on the Sun's surface are the driving force. As material churns beneath the surface, it tugs and rotates these magnetic field lines, stretching them into intricate patterns high above. This process may be key to understanding a long-standing mystery: why the Sun's corona (its outer atmosphere) is millions of degrees hotter than its surface, despite being farther from the Sun's energy-generating core.

The energy generated by the Sun's nuclear fusion has to travel outward, and it appears that these magnetic threads act like invisible pathways, channeling energy into the upper atmosphere and heating it to extreme temperatures.

4. A glimpse above the edge

Sunrise-III also studied another quiet region near the Sun's edge (the solar limb), where it mapped magnetic fields inside spicules — towering jets of solar material that shoot upward like the Sun's own grass growing toward space. These spicules, some stretching 10,000 kilometers into space, carry their own magnetic signatures that shift as they rise.

The data revealed that magnetic field strength changes with height, weakening as it extends further from the surface. This gives scientists their first detailed profile of how the Sun's magnetic influence thins out as you move away from the star — information crucial for understanding how solar activity affects space weather throughout our solar system.

Fun fact: The name "Sunrise" isn't just poetic — these balloon observatories launch before dawn and track the Sun across the sky as it rises, giving them a precious few hours of observation time before the balloon drifts too far and must be recovered.

Try it yourself: Take a large bowl of water and sprinkle iron filings on a paper float. Drag a magnet slowly underneath — watch how the floating paper twists and the filings dance in invisible patterns. That's a tiny model of how magnetic fields shape the solar atmosphere, unseen but felt.
