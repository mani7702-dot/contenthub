How does a star slowly eat a brown dwarf for billions of years?

2026-10-273 min

Astronomers have found a star system where a small star is gradually pulling material from a brown dwarf companion — like a slow cosmic meal that could last billions of years instead of ending in a dramatic swallowed whole.

How does a star slowly eat a brown dwarf for billions of years?

A star that feeds instead of swallows

Most of the time, when astronomers talk about stars and their companions, the story ends in one of two ways: either the two objects stay in a calm, stable orbit around each other, or the star eventually swallows its partner in a single dramatic event — like our Sun will do to Earth in about five billion years.

But what if there is a third option? Scientists have now discovered a star system where a low-mass star is slowly, steadily eating its companion brown dwarf — a cosmic meal that could stretch on for billions of years, far slower than anyone expected.

The system, called ZTF J0440+2325, sits about 300 light-years away in our Milky Way galaxy. It is the first known example of a regular star pulling material from a brown dwarf companion in this gentle, gradual way.

What is a brown dwarf?

You might know that stars are massive balls of hot gas that glow with their own light by fusing hydrogen in their cores. Planets like Earth are too small for this. But what about objects in between?

Brown dwarfs are the cosmic outsiders: they are more massive than the biggest gas giant planets (like Jupiter) but not quite massive enough to sustain hydrogen fusion like a real star. Scientists estimate they have between 13 and 80 times the mass of Jupiter. Because they sit just below the stellar threshold, they emit only faint heat and light — usually glowing dim red or invisible infrared.

In the ZTF J0440+2325 system, the star has about 85 times Jupiter's mass, and its companion brown dwarf weighs roughly 25 Jupiter masses. For comparison, our own Sun is over 1,000 times as massive as Jupiter.

The 87-minute orbit that sparked a mystery

What makes this system so strange is how close the two objects orbit each other. The brown dwarf completes a full lap around its star in just 87 minutes — faster than the hands of a clock make a full circle. Its entire orbit would fit inside the diameter of the Sun.

This close dance was first noticed as a puzzling triangular pattern in the system's light — like a cosmic strobe. At first, researchers thought it might be a "black widow binary," where a fast-spinning neutron star strips material from a smaller companion. But something didn't match.

A new kind of stellar cannibalism

When the team led by MIT's Kevin Burdge measured how the two objects wobble around each other, the wobble was too small to be a black widow system. Instead, they found that the star itself is actively pulling matter off the brown dwarf — a process called accretion.

Unlike black holes that pull material into a swirling disk, this star is large enough that the brown dwarf's gas strikes the stellar surface directly, like an asteroid hitting the Moon — only the asteroid here is a stream of gas, and the Moon is a star.

The team estimates the star is consuming about one hundred-thousandth of Earth's mass per year — roughly 40 million dump trucks of material every second in the form of gas streaming off the brown dwarf.

But because the brown dwarf is so massive, this slow feast could continue for billions of years. Instead of one catastrophic gulp, the star is keeping a very, very long meal going.

Why this changes what we know

Scientists have long thought that close interactions between stars and their companions always ended in one of two extremes: stable orbits or rapid engulfment. This discovery shows there is a gentle middle path.

Understanding this process helps astronomers learn more about how planets and brown dwarfs evolve when they orbit very close to their host stars. It also opens a new window into the surprising variety of relationships that form among objects in our universe.

Try this at home

Take a balloon and rub it on your hair to create static electricity. Hold it near small bits of paper — the paper will jump up to the balloon. That electric pull is like gravity, the force that keeps the brown dwarf's material flowing toward the star. Now imagine if the balloon were a star and the paper were pieces slowly flaking off a brown dwarf — except it would take billions of years!

Fun Fact

The brown dwarf in this system orbits its star faster than any planet we know of in our Solar System — and it is being slowly eaten alive. Meanwhile, it is also one of the few known systems where a normal star (not a neutron star or black hole) is actively accreting matter from a companion.

For grown-ups

The system ZTF J0440+2325 was identified by the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) at Palomar Observatory, whose repeated sky surveys detected an unusual triangular light-curve pattern. Detailed follow-up by Burdge et al. (published in Nature Astronomy, October 2026) confirmed the nature of the system through radial-velocity wobble measurements showing a low-amplitude orbit between two very-low-mass objects. The 85 Jupiter-mass primary is a late M-type dwarf, and the 25 Jupiter-mass companion falls squarely into the brown-dwarf regime (13–80 MJ). Wikipedia confirms that brown dwarfs straddle the boundary between planets and stars: too low-mass for sustained hydrogen fusion but massive enough to fuse deuterium and lithium. The accretion process here differs from classic accretion disks around black holes — because the star has a large physical surface, infalling gas impacts directly rather than forming an inflowing disk, producing a persistent hotspot that rotates in and out of view every 87 minutes and creating the distinctive triangular modulation.
