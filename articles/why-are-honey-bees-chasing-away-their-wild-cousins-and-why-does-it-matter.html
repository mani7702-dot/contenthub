Why are honey bees chasing away their wild cousins — and why does it matter?

2026-10-223 min

Honey bees are pushing wild bumblebees, solitary bees, and hoverflies away from flowers — and scientists say climate change could make the pushing even worse.

Why are honey bees chasing away their wild cousins — and why does it matter?

When honey bees become flower bodyguards

Picture this: a bright patch of bramble flowers buzzing with bees, beetles, and butterflies — all quietly sipping nectar. Now picture one group showing up and saying, "This flower is ours!" That is exactly what scientists have caught honey bees doing. In a surprising twist, our fuzzy little pollinator friends can be real "flower bullies" — and it is making life harder for their wild cousins.

The discovery comes from a two-year study by researchers at Anglia Ruskin University in Cambridge, England. They spent 93 hours watching five different flower patches, each sitting within 300 meters of a busy honey bee hive. What they saw was eye-opening.

The bee bodyguards

The team watched bees visiting two kinds of flowers: bramble (those tart little berries you might pick in the wild) and ivy (the climb-y plant with tiny white flowers). They divided all the visitors into groups — honey bees, bumblebees, solitary bees, hoverflies, and a mixed "other" group of butterflies and beetles.

Then they counted every encounter. Every time one insect interrupted another's snack time, the researchers recorded who won, who lost, and who just flew away.

The results? Honey bees were the clear winners — and they were aggressive about it. In at least 20% of their showdowns, honey bees physically pushed or chased other insects away. On ivy flowers, other pollinators actively avoided honey bees in 36% of all visits. On bramble, that number was 28%.

Winners and losers at the flower party

The math was stark. Where honey bee numbers were highest, wild pollinators were fewer. Every time honey bee density doubled on bramble flowers, bumblebee numbers dropped by 14%. On ivy, more honey bees meant fewer solitary bees and hoverflies.

But the study also uncovered a tiny hero: a small, non-native bee called the ivy bee. First spotted in the UK in 2001, this little solitary bee was the only wild pollinator scientists saw fighting back — sometimes even chasing honey bees away from flowers!

Why a warming world could make things worse

Here is where it gets even more interesting (and a bit worrying). As the planet warms, scientists expect the problem to grow:

  • Flowers get stressed: When plants experience heat stress, they produce less nectar — meaning every bee has to compete harder for less food.
  • Honey bees love warmth: Honey bees become more active as temperatures rise. At one point, warming by just 12°C (about 22°F) made ten times more honey bees leave their hive!
  • Wild bees struggle in heat: Bumblebees and hoverflies are sensitive to high temperatures — they feed best in cooler weather.

Meanwhile, beekeeping is booming. The number of registered beekeepers in the UK has more than doubled since 2010 — from 19,000 to 47,000 — meaning more and more honey bees are setting up hives near wildflower patches.

Try this at home

You can observe this same "flower competition" in your garden! Plant two patches of the same flowers — one near a sunny wall (warm) and one in a shady spot. Early in the morning, sit quietly with a notebook and watch which bees visit first. Do you notice more honey bees at the warm patch? Do bumblebees seem to get pushed away? Record your observations — you are doing real science!

Fun Fact

A single honey bee colony can contain up to 60,000 bees in summer — and each one visits up to 1,000 flowers on a single foraging trip. That is a lot of flower visits to compete over!
