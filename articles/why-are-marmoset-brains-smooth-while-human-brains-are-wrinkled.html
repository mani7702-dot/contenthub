Why are marmoset brains smooth while human brains are wrinkled?

2026-10-223 min

Tiny brain-building cells grow and divide at different speeds in marmosets vs. humans — and those slow changes add up to a smooth brain instead of a heavily folded one.

Why are marmoset brains smooth while human brains are wrinkled?

Report filed at 02:59 IST from the Our World news desk.

The monkey with a tiny, smooth brain

Take a look inside your head and you will find something magical — your brain looks a bit like a walnut, all crinkled and folded. Those ripples and ridges are called gyri (the folds) and sulci (the grooves), and they pack billions of nerve cells into a small space.

But not all primates have such a wrinkly brain. Meet the common marmoset — a tiny monkey no bigger than a loaf of bread. Its brain is smooth as glass, with almost no folds at all. How can two primate brains be so different? A closer look at their baby cells gives us a surprising answer.

The brain's construction crew slows down

Your brain grows from special cells called neural progenitor cells. Think of them as the brain's construction workers — they divide and divide, making more and more nerve cells. The more workers you have and the faster they work, the more neurons they build. More neurons means more room to fold.

Scientists at the German Primate Center grew tiny "brain organoids" — miniature 3D brains in a dish — from both human and marmoset cells. What they found was remarkable: in marmosets these construction crews work by a slower schedule.

1. The cells divide more slowly than in humans.

2. Some cells have simpler shapes — fewer branches to grab onto, so they build fewer new cells.

3. The whole busy building window is shorter than it is in us.

Fewer bricks, fewer folds

It is a bit like building a brick wall. If you have fewer bricks and the workers move more slowly, the wall comes out smaller and smoother. The marmoset brain ends up with fewer neurons packed inside — and without enough neurons pushing for space, the surface stays flat instead of folding into that familiar walnut pattern.

Human brains, by contrast, keep their construction crews multiplying for longer and at a faster pace. That builds a much bigger cortex — the outer brain layer that handles thinking, remembering, and imagining — and the extra growth pleats itself into folds, like a stretchy sweater that wrinkles when it is too big for its frame.

Why does this matter?

The same cells that build the brain when we are babies can go wrong in development. By comparing marmosets and humans, scientists hope to learn how brain size and folding go so wrong in certain conditions. Their discovery also reveals a deep truth: our big, wrinkly brains did not appear out of nowhere. They grew one tiny, slow cell division at a time.

Try this at home

Make your own "brain fold" with a balloon and a rubber band. Blow up a balloon just a little — that is your small marmoset brain, smooth and tight. Now blow it up more and stretch a rubber band around it — the rubber creates a wrinkle, just like your larger, folding brain. The bigger the balloon, the more folds appear!

Fun Fact

The common marmoset is so important to science that labs around the world study it to learn about human health. Their smooth little brains teach us that sometimes the best way to understand something big — like our own wrinkly heads — is to start by studying something small and simple.
