Could a tiny crustacean really become a fish's living tongue?

2026-10-233 min

Scientists have confirmed that the tongue-eating louse — a tiny crustacean called Cymothoa exigua — is the only known parasite that can completely replace an entire organ in its host, stepping in as a living replacement for a fish's tongue.

Could a tiny crustacean really become a fish's living tongue?

What is the tongue-eating louse?

Imagine a creature so sneaky that it can replace an entire organ in another animal. That is not fantasy — it is real biology, and the star of the show is a tiny crustacean called Cymothoa exigua, better known as the tongue-eating louse.

This little isopod is related to pill bugs and woodlice, but instead of rolling into a ball, it does something far more surprising. It lives inside fish, and when it is done with its work, it becomes the fish's new tongue.

How does it take over?

The story starts in the ocean, where a juvenile louse latches onto a fish and sneaks in through its gills. From there, it makes a tiny journey into the fish's mouth, crawling across the tongue like an underwater explorer.

Once it finds the perfect spot, the louse uses its sharp front claws to bite down on the tongue. It does something remarkable: it severs the blood vessels that feed the tongue. Without blood, the tongue begins to wither and die — a process called necrosis. Eventually, the tongue falls off entirely.

But here is the twist — the louse does not leave. It cements itself onto the remaining tongue stub, anchoring in place with its five pairs of legs. From that moment on, the fish has a living, breathing louse where its tongue used to be.

Why is this so special?

You might think a parasite taking over a fish sounds pretty bad. But scientists are amazed because no other parasite in the world is known to replace an entire organ. Most parasites live off their host, but they do not become part of it.

The tongue-eating louse does not just live there — it works there. The fish can still open and close its mouth, grab food, and swallow, all thanks to its new crustacean tongue. The fish adapts and keeps going, as if nothing unusual happened.

The louse is also a hermaphrodite — every one starts life as a male, then later changes sex to become female. They can live up to three years, growing right alongside their host. These lice live in the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans, from the Gulf of California down to Ecuador, often hanging out at depths between 6 and 200 feet.

Make a "living prosthetic" with clay! Roll two different-colored pieces of modeling clay — one soft and squishy, one firmer. Press the soft piece onto a flat surface to represent the fish's tongue. Now take the firmer piece and press it gently over the soft one, molding it so it sticks. You have just made your own model of how the louse replaces the tongue! Can you still "tongue" food around? What does this tell you about how clever nature can be?

Fun Fact

The tongue-eating louse is so good at its job that the fish's brain actually learns to use it. Scientists have watched fish chew and swallow normally even after the louse takes over — the fish does not seem to notice the difference at all!
