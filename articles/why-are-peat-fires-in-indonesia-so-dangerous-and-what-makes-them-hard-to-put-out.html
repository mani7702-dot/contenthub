Why are peat fires in Indonesia so dangerous — and what makes them hard to put out?

2026-10-223 min

Peat fires in Indonesia burn underground and create toxic haze that crosses borders, but the special spongy ground where they start makes them incredibly hard to extinguish.

Why are peat fires in Indonesia so dangerous — and what makes them hard to put out?

What are peat lands?

Imagine a giant, waterlogged sponge made of half-decayed plants stretching across the ground. That is peat — nature's compost pile that builds up slowly over hundreds of years in swampy areas. Indonesia has some of the world's largest peat lands, especially on the island of Borneo (also called Kalimantan). When this spongy ground is dry, it catches fire easily — and that is exactly what is happening now in Indonesia's worst wildfire season in a decade.

Report filed at 15:41 IST from the Our World news desk.

Why peat fires are unlike campfires

A campfire burns above ground — you can see the flames and pour water on them. But peat fires are sneaky: they burn below ground, smoldering through the spongy layer like a slow, underground barbecue that never turns off. Because the fire is hidden, it can keep glowing for weeks or even months.

The haze that crosses borders

As peat burns, it releases thick, toxic smoke — a dangerous mix of carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and fine particles. This smoke doesn't stop at Indonesia's borders. It drifts across Southeast Asia, creating a choking haze that has reached neighboring Singapore and Malaysia. The smoke blankets cities, schools, and homes, turning sunny skies into a murky orange.

What makes peat so dangerous?

Peat stores an incredible amount of carbon — centuries of plant matter packed tightly together. When it burns, that stored carbon is released all at once, adding extra carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Peat fires also produce more carbon per area than almost any other type of wildfire. Wikipedia reports that over 200,000 hectares of forests and land have already burned this year in Indonesia, with fire-prevention efforts focused on provinces like West Kalimantan, Central Kalimantan, and South Kalimantan.

Fun Fact

Peat fires can keep smoldering underground for years after the surface flames are gone. In some parts of Indonesia, people have walked across ground that felt warm underfoot — not knowing that a hidden fire was burning beneath their feet!

Try this at home

Fill a clear cup halfway with water, then add a few drops of food coloring. Drop in a small piece of paper and watch how the color spreads through the water — like smoke spreading through the air. Just like the peat fire's smoke drifts and spreads, your colored water shows how invisible pollution can move through space without you seeing the source. Ask a grown-up to help you imagine where that "smoke" might travel if it could float above the cup!
