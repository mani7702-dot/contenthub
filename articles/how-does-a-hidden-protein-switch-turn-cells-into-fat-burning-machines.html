How does a hidden protein switch turn cells into fat-burning machines?

2026-10-253 min

Scientists found a protein called Mitch that acts like a switch — flip it off and cells burn more fat and stop making new fat cells. The discovery could lead to new ways to fight obesity that don't just suppress appetite.

How does a hidden protein switch turn cells into fat-burning machines?

The mitochondria powerhouse trick

Every cell in your body is like a small factory, and the mitochondria are its power plants. These tiny, bean-shaped structures take the food you eat and turn it into a fuel called ATP — the battery that keeps everything running.

But mitochondria are also shape-shifters. They constantly merge into big networks or split into smaller pieces. This matters because shape changes how efficiently they produce energy. Scientists at the Weizmann Institute of Science discovered that a single protein — nicknamed "Mitch" — sits at the center of this shape-shifting and acts like a hidden switch.

Flipping the switch to burn more fat

In a surprising discovery, when researchers turned off the Mitch protein, cells started burning more fuel — especially fatty acids. Instead of storing energy efficiently, the cells swung into a higher-energy mode, consuming more nutrients and releasing more energy.

This happened because without Mitch, mitochondria became fragmented and less efficient at conserving energy. The cells, always hungry for fuel, began devouring more fats and carbohydrates to keep up.

The most exciting part? Mitch doesn't just affect energy use — it also stops new fat cells from forming. When the protein is missing, immature cells struggle to mature into fat-storing cells. In experiments, only 5–10% became fat cells (compared to 80–90% normally). That means the body could burn more fat and make fewer new fat cells at the same time.

Why this matters for healthy bodies

In earlier mouse experiments, switching off Mitch in muscle tissue made the animals run farther, resist obesity, and stay healthier — without eating less. A later study confirmed that mice with Mitch removed from their fat tissue burned extra energy naturally, even at rest.

This discovery points toward a future where staying healthy might mean flipping the right cellular switches — not just counting calories. Established habits like resistance exercise and eating enough protein still matter most today, but understanding the body's own fat-burning buttons gives scientists powerful new tools for the future.

Try this at home

Build a mitochondria model: Grab some modeling clay or play-dough in two colors. Roll one color into long snake shapes — that's your mitochondrion. Now pinch and split it into smaller pieces. Notice how the "network" changes? Big connected networks (with Mitch ON) are good at conserving energy, while many small pieces (with Mitch OFF) burn more energy. You just modeled how scientists think cells decide whether to store fuel or burn it!

Fun Fact

The protein's nickname — Mitch — comes from "MTCH2," which stands for mitochondrial carrier homolog 2. It was first discovered as a protein that helps move molecules across mitochondrial membranes, and researchers later found it also plays a role in programmed cell death (apoptosis). Finding it also controls metabolism was a complete surprise!

for-grownups: The original study, "MTCH2 controls energy demand and expenditure to fuel anabolism during adipogenesis," was published in The EMBO Journal on January 3, 2025. The follow-up, "MTCH2 modulates CPT1 activity to regulate lipid metabolism of adipocytes," appeared in Nature Communications on October 3, 2025. Both were led by Professor Atan Gross and Dr. Sabita Chourasia at the Weizmann Institute of Science.
