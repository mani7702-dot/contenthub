What Are Bethe Strings, the Quantum Knots Predicted Nearly a Century Ago?

Imagine a knot that holds itself together with nothing to tie it — no string, no glue, nothing but the way its pieces push and pull on one another. Back in 1931, the physicist Hans Bethe worked out that such a thing should be possible, but only under one strict rule: the particles had to live in a single line. He called his mathematical prediction what we now know as Bethe strings.

For nearly a hundred years, that idea stayed on paper. Now scientists at the University of Innsbruck in Austria have built one in the lab and watched it survive a crash.

1. A Very Cold, Very Straight Line

To make a Bethe string, the team needed two things science finds hard to combine: extreme cold and perfect order. They started with a cloud of cesium atoms cooled to just a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero — colder than any place in the universe. Then they split that cloud into several thousand incredibly narrow tubes.

Inside each tube, an atom can move in only one direction, like a bead on a wire. That one-dimensional setting is the secret ingredient. Normally you can nudge past a friend in a crowd; in a single-file line, you cannot.

2. Switching the Glue On

Next, the researchers turned a dial that controls how strongly the atoms interact. When the atoms pushed each other away, they drifted as free particles. But when the scientists flipped the interaction to attractive, the atoms snuggled together — not into one big clump, but into bound clusters of many different sizes. Some held six or more atoms at once.

Strangely, no chemical bonds form. The particles stay together purely because of how they interact, the way a flock of birds holds its shape without being tied together.

3. A Knot That Refuses to Snap

How do you prove atoms are truly bound? Let the string stretch. First the team let the clusters expand while staying inside their narrow tubes — and the strings slammed into one another without falling apart. "This is a remarkable feature of the strings: they can collide without breaking apart," said lead author Milena Horvath.

Then came the twist. Because Bethe strings can exist only in one dimension, releasing them into three-dimensional space made the clusters fly apart, freeing extra energy that made the atoms spread faster. Comparing the two experiments gave a clear fingerprint of the strings.

Fun Fact

Absolute zero is −273.15 °C. The cesium cloud in this experiment was chilled to within a few billionths of a degree of it — colder than empty space, which glows with faint leftover warmth at about −270 °C.
