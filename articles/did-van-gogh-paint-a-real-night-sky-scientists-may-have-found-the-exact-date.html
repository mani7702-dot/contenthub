Did Van Gogh paint a real night sky? Scientists may have found the exact date

2026-10-224 min

One of the world's most famous paintings may have captured a real night sky — and a new study says scientists have finally figured out the exact date it was painted.

Did Van Gogh paint a real night sky? Scientists may have found the exact date

The painting that captured a real night sky — and when it happened

When you look at Vincent van Gogh's "The Starry Night," what do you see? Swirling blues and yellows, a bright crescent moon, a village tucked under rolling hills, and a sky full of stars. It is one of the most famous paintings in the world — but is it just Van Gogh's imagination, or did he paint a real night sky?

New research says it is real — and scientists think they know exactly which night Van Gogh was looking at: the morning of July 22, 1889, just before sunrise, from the window of his room at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France.

This matches what Wikipedia confirms: Van Gogh painted The Starry Night from the east-facing window of his asylum room, capturing what he saw just before sunrise. Wikipedia also notes that Venus was visible in the sky at the time — and it appears in the painting as the bright "morning star."

How one astronomer cracked the code of Van Gogh's stars

For decades, astronomers tried to figure out which part of the sky Van Gogh painted — but no one agreed. Art historian Albert Boime and astronomer George Abell tried in 1984, and Harvard's Charles Whitney gave it a shot in later years. None reached the same answer.

Then came Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim and his team. Instead of guessing, they used a method called Procrustes analysis — a mathematical way to compare the "shape" of two groups of points, even if one is rotated, resized, or moved. They matched the five brightest stars in the painting's central cluster to the stars in the Hyades cluster (in the constellation Taurus) as they appeared on July 22, 1889. The match was so precise that, mathematically, it could not be a coincidence.

They also checked the waning crescent moon and Venus in the painting — both appearing in the right place in the pre-dawn sky of that date. According to the researchers, the Hyades cluster was the key piece that confirmed the date, because it satisfied every condition: position, orientation, and visibility.

What a letter tells us about the night Van Gogh painted

Here is the twist: art historians have long believed Van Gogh painted The Starry Night in June 1889. But the sky that matches is from late July — about a month later. Does that mean the history books are wrong?

Van Gogh's own letters offer a clue. On April 12, 1888, he wrote to his brother Theo: "A starry sky, for example — well, it's a thing that I'd like to try to do, just as in the daytime I'll try to paint a green meadow studded with dandelions." He was already dreaming of painting the night sky long before he did.

Then, around late May to early June 1889, Van Gogh wrote: "This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big." This was Venus — the same bright object that appears in The Starry Night.

The researchers say this could mean Van Gogh conceived the idea in June but finished the painting after July 22, when the sky truly matched what he had in mind. Van Gogh's own words show he kept watching and waiting for the right night — and when it came, he painted it forever.

Try this at home

On a clear night this month, step outside about 45 minutes before sunrise and face east. Look for Venus — bright and low in the sky. That is the "morning star" Van Gogh painted! Now find the bright cluster of stars that looks a little like a small dipper or tiny crown. That is the Hyades in Taurus — the same cluster Van Gogh painted over 135 years ago.

You can also use a free app like Stellarium (on a phone or computer) to see what the sky looked like on any date in history. Set it to July 22, 1889, point it at the pre-dawn sky over Saint-Rémys, and you'll see exactly what inspired one of the most beloved paintings ever made.

Fun fact: The swirling patterns in The Starry Night are NOT how stars actually move. But Van Gogh painted them to show how the wind and atmosphere affect starlight — a creative choice that modern astronomers now know is surprisingly accurate. The cypress tree in the foreground was real too — Van Gogh grew up around these dark, flame-shaped trees in southern France.

For grown-ups: The scientific study was published on October 22, 2026, in the journal Scientific Reports (DOI 10.1038/s41598-026-73025-5), led by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim of Kyung Hee University. Earlier analyses by Albert Boime and George Abell (UCLA, 1984) and Charles Whitney (Harvard) had not reached consensus. Van Gogh painted The Starry Night in June 1889 while voluntarily staying at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum — he had admitted himself after cutting off part of his own ear in December 1888. The painting has been part of MoMA's collection in New York since 1941.
