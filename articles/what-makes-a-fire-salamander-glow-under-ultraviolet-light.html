What Makes a Fire Salamander Glow Under Ultraviolet Light?

2026-10-293 min

Fire salamanders are famous for their bright warning colors, but scientists just discovered they do something magical under ultraviolet light: their skin and toxic secretions glow green and cyan, like nature's own neon sign.

What makes a fire salamander glow under ultraviolet light?

Walk through a dark European forest on a moonlit night, and you might spot a flash of yellow and black slinking between the trees. That is a fire salamander, one of nature's most distinctive creatures. But what scientists recently discovered is that this familiar amphibian has a hidden trick — one that only shows up when you shine the right kind of light on it.

1. A glow that no one saw coming

Fire salamanders do not produce their own light. Instead, when ultraviolet light hits their skin, it is absorbed and then reemitted as a soft green and cyan glow. This is called biofluorescence — and until now, no one knew that salamanders could do it. The glow is strongest along the yellow underside of the body and on the salamander's toxic skin secretions, which can keep glowing for more than a full day after being released.

The discovery came from researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology in Germany, working with biologists in Barcelona and other European institutions. They noticed something unexpected while studying the salamander's famous poison glands.

2. Toxic skin that keeps on glowing

Fire salamanders protect themselves with samandarines — powerful toxins stored in special glands concentrated around the head and back. These chemicals make the salamander so poisonous that few predators dare to bite it. The same glands produce the glowing compounds, meaning the most dangerous part of the salamander is also its brightest.

The researchers believe the fluorescence might help salamanders find each other in the dark, since European forests are much dimmer than daylight. Even weak green and cyan highlights could act like tiny lanterns, helping a mate or a mother guide her babies across the forest floor. Or it could make their warning colors even more visible to would-be predators.

3. Still more secrets to uncover

The exact molecule responsible for the glow has not been identified yet. The team thinks it is a new compound, one that has been hiding in plain sight in a creature that humans have studied for centuries. That is what makes science exciting — even the most familiar neighbors can surprise us with secrets we never saw coming.

Fun fact: Fire salamanders can live for over 50 years, making them one of the longest-living amphibians. One salamander at a German natural history museum reportedly outlived every human caretaker who looked after it during its lifetime!

For the grown-ups

Fire salamanders (Salamandra salamandra) are found across southern and central Europe and are classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Their toxicity comes from steroidal alkaloids called samandarines, stored in dorsal skin glands. The biofluorescence discovery was made by Bernat Burriel-Carranza, Andrés E. Brunetti, Martin Kaltenpoth, and Salvador Carranza at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology and collaborators in Barcelona. The study — "Glandular biofluorescence in fire salamanders (Salamandra salamandra): first evidence and ecological implications" — was published in Royal Society Open Science (2026, 13(5), DOI: 10.1098/rsos.251991). Wikipedia's "Fire salamander" article independently confirms: the species is black with yellow markings (aposematic coloration), produces samandarine toxins, is found in European forests, and was uplisted to Vulnerable by the IUCN in 2023 due to the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans.
