Why cleaning up the air from power plants is unmasking faster global warming

2026-10-213 min

For over a century, burning coal and oil filled the sky with sulphur pollution — tiny bright particles that reflected sunlight and hid some of our warming. As clean-air laws sweep that pollution away, the hidden side of climate change is showing through.

Why does cleaning the air sometimes make the world warmer?

The two pollution problems from one smokestack

When coal and oil burn, they send two very different things up into the sky at the same time.

One is carbon dioxide — an invisible gas that traps heat like the glass roof of a greenhouse. That is the main driver of the warming we feel.

The other is sulphur pollution. It rises and forms vast clouds of microscopic particles called sulphate aerosols. These tiny specks are bright, and they float high above the planet — acting like a faint, planet-sized umbrella.

Dust on the greenhouse glass

Think of Earth's climate like a greenhouse. Carbon dioxide is the glass that keeps the heat in.

Sulphate aerosols are like dust gathering on that glass. The more dust there is, the less sunlight slips through — and the slower the greenhouse warms up in the first place.

For decades this invisible veil did quiet, behind-the-scenes work. Coal-fired power plants pumped out huge amounts of sulphur, and that sulphur became an accidental sunscreen over huge regions of North America, Europe, and Asia.

The day the umbrella came down

Beginning in the early 1990s, countries began passing clean-air laws — laws meant to clear the smog people could see and breathe. The United States' Acid Rain Program, Europe's push on power-plant emissions, and similar rules elsewhere did exactly what they aimed for: they scrubbed sulphur out of the air.

But sulphur that used for cooling the planet also disappeared with it.

As the bright aerosol veil thins, more of the Sun's energy reaches the surface. Warming that was being held back — held "under an umbrella" — now shows up a little more clearly each year.

What the scientists say

Meteorologists track this by comparing two numbers. One is the steady climb of carbon dioxide — now over 420 parts per million, the highest in millions of years. The other is the steady drop in sulphate aerosols across the developed world.

Professor Reto Knutti at ETH Zurich (who helped write the UN's IPCC climate reports) and his team found a clear pattern: once the effects of natural cycles like El Niño are separated out, the underlying rate of warming has been accelerating. And the vanishing aerosol umbrella is part of that story.

Dr Øivind Hodnebrog at the Norwegian climate institute CICERO explains it with a picture: sulphate particles are "really small but bright" — they brighten clouds and bounce sunlight away. "More aerosols mean brighter clouds and more reflection," he says. "So less warming." With fewer aerosols, that reflection fades.

Seeing is believing — from space

Satellites can measure both sides of the story. They watch carbon dioxide climb. They also watch clouds grow whiter and dimmer as the sulphur haze lifts — a visible fingerprint of clean air doing its job.

The past three years have been the three warmest ever recorded, and the trend line is tilting up. The good news? The same satellite view that lets us see the disappearing veil also lets us see the solution: every ton of carbon we keep buried slows the warming that the old sulphur umbrella used to hide.

Try this at home

Hold a torch behind a piece of wax paper and shine it onto a wall. First you'll see a bright patch. Then dab a little talcum powder lightly on the paper — suddenly the glow on the wall grows dimmer, because the powder scatters light just like fine pollution particles scatter sunlight. Wipe the powder off — brightness comes back. That's your miniature model of how bright aerosols dim sunlight, and what happens when they're washed away. (Ask an adult to help with the torch.)
