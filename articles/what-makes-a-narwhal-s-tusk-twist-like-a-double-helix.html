What makes a narwhal's tusk twist like a double helix?

2026-10-253 min

Scientists used giant X-ray lights to peer inside the narwhal's legendary tusk and discovered it is built like a double helix — two spirals twisting in opposite directions, like a natural piece of rope.

What makes a narwhal's tusk twist like a double helix?

The unicorn that was real

Deep in the icy Arctic Ocean lives a whale with a horn that looks like it swam straight out of a fairy tale. The narwhal (say it like "NOR-whal") is a stocky white whale with a long, spiraled tusk — and for hundreds of years, people thought those tusks were real unicorn horns! In the Middle Ages, narwhal tusks were even sold as unicorn horns and believed to have magical powers. But today, scientists are using giant X-ray machines to unlock the real, amazing secret of how this horn grows.

Two spirals, one super-strong tooth

An international team of scientists wanted to know: how does a narwhal grow a tusk that is perfectly straight, yet twisted like a corkscrew? To find out, they used the world's largest X-ray machines — giant particle accelerators called synchrotrons — in Sweden, Switzerland, and France. These super-powered X-rays can see right through the tooth and map its insides in 3D.

Here is what they discovered: the tusk is not just one spiral — it is two spirals twisted in opposite directions, like a DNA double helix made of bone! The outer layer spirals to the left, while the inner layer spirals to the right. Where they meet, they form a biological "counterbalance" that makes the tusk incredibly strong — so strong that it can handle huge forces without cracking.

What is the tusk for?

The tusk is the narwhal's left upper canine tooth — basically, a giant tooth that grows for the whale's entire life, reaching up to 10 feet (3 meters) long. Only male narwhals usually grow tusks, which tells us it may be a "badge of beauty" used to attract mates or show status, like a peacock's tail. But the tusk is also packed with millions of nerve endings, letting the narwhal "taste" changes in the water, like shifts in temperature and salt. Because those growth rings record the water's chemistry, scientists can read a narwhal's tusk like a history book of Arctic change — telling us how the planet's coldest ocean has warmed over the whale's lifetime.

Try this at home

  • Make a double helix out of two pieces of thin wire or pipe cleaners. Twist one clockwise and one counterclockwise, then gently wrap them around each other. Notice how the two opposing twists lock together into a strong, stiff shape — just like the narwhal's tusk!
  • Build a tusk from air-dry clay: roll one coil left-handed and another right-handed, then let them meet in the middle. Paint it white and silver to look like a real narwhal tusk.
  • Draw a comic strip showing a narwhal in the Arctic, its tusk glowing as it "tastes" the temperature of the seawater.

Fun Fact

A baby narwhal is called a calf, just like a baby whale or dolphin! And even though the tusk can be longer than a grown person is tall, it has no enamel — instead it is built like a tiny house with strong dentin inside and cementum outside, a design no other animal uses quite this way.
