How did Indonesia protect 4 million acres of rainforest in one big decision?

2026-10-223 min

Indonesia decided to stop clearing 4.4 million acres of rainforest, swamps, and peatlands for rice and sugar farms — saving a habitat home to orangutans, proboscis monkeys, and clouded leopards instead.

How did Indonesia protect 4 million acres of rainforest in one big decision?

Imagine an area of forest so big that it would take you 30 minutes to drive across — all at once, that whole forest was going to be cut down to grow rice and sugar. Then, one day, the government simply decided to stop. That is the story of how Indonesia protected what scientists call "the lungs of the planet."

1. The rice dream that changed course

Indonesia set a goal: grow enough rice for everyone, using land that was still wild forest. The plan covered about 4.4 million acres across two very special places — Kalimantan (the Indonesian part of the island of Borneo) and South Papua on the island of New Guinea.

But here is the surprising part. By 2026, Indonesia had already reached rice self-sufficiency — about three years earlier than planned. With the goal met ahead of schedule, the agriculture and forestry ministries agreed: there was no need to keep clearing more forest. The rice program would pause, and four million acres of trees would be saved.

2. Who lives there? (Hint: it is a whole lot of unique animals)

Kalimantan's rainforests are like a living library of animals that exist nowhere else on Earth:

  • Orangutans swing through the treetops, our closest cousin that still lives wild in the trees
  • Proboscis monkeys with their big noses bounce from branch to branch
  • Clouded leopards — big cats with the friendliest eyes — slip silently between trunks

South Papua is home to its own wonders, including emerald-green birds so bright they look like they were painted, and quiet swamp forests that store more carbon than almost any place on the planet. These peatlands act like giant sponges, holding water and keeping the land stable.

3. A clever swap: use old farmland instead

Instead of clearing wild nature, Indonesia said future farms would go on already-degraded land — dirt that was once farmed or damaged, not fresh forest. The country has over 28 million acres of this kind of land waiting to be revived. That means rice can grow, wildlife can stay, and the forest keeps breathing.

Try this at home

Draw a big rectangle on a sheet of paper — this is your 4.4 million acres! Now color half of it green with trees and little animal shapes (orangutans, monkeys). Color the other half brown for farms. Which half supports more life? Now draw tiny dots on the farm side to show "already-used land." You just recreated Indonesia's smart swap — farms on brown land, forests stay green!

Fun Fact

Borneo (shared by Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei) is home to about 10,000 plant species. Scientists estimate that 1 in 10 of all plant species in the world can be found in Borneo's forests — and a single hectare (about 2 football fields) of this rainforest can hold over 400 different tree species. A hectare of young tropical rainforest even produces enough oxygen for about 30 people each day. That means every square mile saved helps dozens of families breathe easier.
