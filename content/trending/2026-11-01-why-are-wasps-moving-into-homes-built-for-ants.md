# Why Are Wasps Moving Into Homes Built for Ants?

Deep in the rainforests of Malaysian Borneo, a partnership older than humanity is quietly coming apart — and the newcomer moving in is a predator with a taste for house-sitting.

## A 10-Million-Year Handshake

For at least **10 million years**, a group of tropical trees called *Macaranga* has struck a clever bargain with ants. The trees grow **hollow chambers** inside their stems — ready-made apartments — and even produce little food parcels packed with nutrients. In return, the resident ants patrol the leaves and attack anything that tries to nibble them, such as hungry caterpillars. It is one of nature's classic "you scratch my back" deals, known to scientists as a **mutualism**.

## The Uninvited Guest

Researchers from Queen Mary University of London, the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and the Czech Academy of Sciences expected to find ants when they cut open young *Macaranga pearsonii* trees. Instead, many of the hollow chambers were stuffed with **flies — being eaten alive by wasp larvae**.

The story unfolds in three acts. Adult predatory wasps hunt flies. They paralyse them. Then they stuff the helpless insects into the plant's chambers as a larder for their growing young. What used to be an ant colony's fortress has become a **wasp nursery**.

## Where the Trouble Grows

The takeover was not random. Trees growing in **oil palm plantations** — land heavily reshaped by people — were far more likely to host wasps than trees in logged forest. And trees occupied by wasps had noticeably **smaller ant colonies**, hinting the wasps may be pushing the ants out entirely, though the team says more experiments are needed to be sure.

Why does it matter? *Macaranga* trees are among the first to sprout on cleared or damaged land, helping forests heal. If they lose their ant bodyguards, they may grow weaker — and that could slow the recovery of the whole forest around them.

## A Warning Written in Wood

The study, published in the journal *PeerJ*, is a reminder that ecosystems can shift in subtle ways long before any species disappears. As senior author Dr. Tom Fayle explains, when a helpful partnership breaks down, it can even drive long-term evolution: the trees might eventually stop investing in chambers that no longer protect them. The first sign of change, it turns out, is hiding inside a hollow stem.

## Fun Fact

Ant-plants like *Macaranga* are so committed to their tiny tenants that some species grow their hollow rooms to fit one exact ant species — and the ants, in turn, cannot survive without the plant. It is one of the tightest friendships in the living world.
