At the very centre of Stonehenge lies a flat slab known as the Altar Stone, a six-tonne block of sandstone roughly 4.9 metres long. It is the monument's most mysterious stone — because its owner is not the rolling chalk of southern England at all.

The Altar Stone came from northeastern Scotland, around 700 kilometres (435 miles) away. How a six-tonne rock travelled that distance about 4,500 years ago has puzzled researchers for a century. A new study offers a surprising twist: part of the journey may have been made not by people, but by a glacier.

## A Daring New Theory

A team from Sheffield Hallam University and Curtin University in Australia combined mineral grain dating with computer models of ancient ice sheets. Their results suggest that during Britain's last ice age, glaciers could have carried the stone from Scotland's Orcadian Basin as far as Dogger Bank, a shallow rise in the North Sea.

Dogger Bank was then part of Doggerland — a grassy, low-lying landscape connecting Britain to Europe that is now drowned beneath the waves. Because that region had no rocky outcrops of its own, any large stone found there must have arrived from somewhere else.

## But the Glacier Couldn't Finish the Job

Here is the catch. Even if ice managed the first stretch, the glacier could not have delivered the stone to Salisbury Plain. People would still have had to move it roughly 400 kilometres more, and to rescue it before rising seas swallowed Doggerland entirely.

Co-lead author Dr Remy Veness said the idea hints that the stone may have mattered to people long before Stonehenge was built. "The evidence points to a deliberate, carefully planned movement across a challenging and varied landscape," added co-lead author Dr Anthony Clarke.

The team thinks the stone may have travelled along the Ridgeway, often called Europe's oldest road, on its way south.

## How Do Scientists Know Where It Came From?

They read a stone's address the way a detective reads a fingerprint. Each sandstone holds tiny zircon crystals, and the uranium inside them decays at a steady, predictable rate. In 2024, researchers matched those crystal "clocks" to the Orcadian Basin. The new work narrowed it further — the closest match is rock from Caithness, in the far north of Scotland.

Not everyone is convinced. Other scientists argue that glaciers are an unlikely explanation and that people moved the stone entirely by muscle, rope and raft. The debate is far from settled — which, for a monument this old, is half the fun.

## Fun Fact

Stonehenge's giant outer sarsens came from just 25 kilometres away, and many of its smaller "bluestones" travelled from western Wales. The Altar Stone's Scottish origin makes it the monument's most far-flung traveller of all.
