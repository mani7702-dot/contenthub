# How Did Magma Store a Hidden Oxygen Tank Deep Inside Earth — And Could It Have Filled Our Atmosphere?

## 1. What did scientists discover about magma deep inside Earth?

Here's a surprising thought: right now, as you read this, there may be a **hidden tank of oxygen** the size of a small ocean, locked away deep beneath our feet.

Scientists have discovered that molten rock — called **magma** — can store far more oxygen than anyone expected. Not the oxygen that's just part of its normal chemistry, but something extra they're calling **"excess oxygen."** At the crushing pressures found dozens of miles underground, magma soaks up additional oxygen almost like a sponge, holding onto it until conditions change.

The finding challenges a big assumption. For a long time, scientists thought the amount of oxygen in magma was simply dictated by what other elements were already there — silicon, magnesium, iron, and so on. Oxygen was always counted as part of those chemical "ingredients," not as something magma could independently absorb and release. But it turns out that under the right pressure, it behaves more like water or carbon dioxide — dissolving in when squeezed, and letting go when the pressure drops.

## 2. How did they test it in the lab?

To find out, researchers recreated Earth's deep interior in the laboratory. They heated a synthetic version of volcanic rock — called **mid-ocean ridge basalt** — to temperatures between 1,200 and 1,800 °C (that's hotter than the surface of the Sun). They squeezed it under pressures up to **7 gigapascals**, roughly 70,000 times the air pressure at sea level.

To create an oxygen-rich environment, they added a pinch of platinum oxide. After running the experiments, they rapidly cooled the molten rock into glass and measured how much "extra" oxygen remained beyond what the chemistry alone would predict.

The results were striking:
- At normal surface pressure: almost **no excess oxygen**
- At 7 gigapascals: **over 5% of the sample's weight** was excess oxygen

For context, control experiments without the added platinum oxide yielded only 0.2% to 0.5% excess oxygen under the same conditions. The pressure made all the difference.

Using Raman spectroscopy — a technique that identifies materials by how they interact with light — the team confirmed the oxygen had truly dissolved into the glass structure, not just bubbled up as gas.

## 3. How does this connect to our atmosphere?

Here's where it gets really interesting. As magma rises from deep underground toward the surface, the pressure around it drops. If that magma was carrying excess oxygen, some of it could be released along the way — bubbling out, so to speak.

This raises a fascinating possibility: **ancient volcanic eruptions may have helped fill Earth's early sky with oxygen.**

Around 2.5 to 2.0 billion years ago, Earth's atmosphere underwent a dramatic transformation, shifting from nearly oxygen-free to the oxygen-rich air we breathe today. This change overlapped with the formation of some of the planet's largest volcanic provinces — vast regions of rock created by enormous outpourings of magma.

The researchers estimate that if magma erupted during this period released just **0.27% of its weight as excess oxygen**, the total amount could theoretically match all the oxygen currently in Earth's atmosphere today.

That doesn't prove volcanoes were the sole cause, but it shows how even a tiny percentage from an enormous volume of molten rock could have had outsized planetary consequences.

## 4. What does it tell us about other worlds?

The discovery doesn't just rewrite Earth's story — it could reshape how we think about other rocky planets, too.

If magma can carry oxygen as an independent ingredient, then models of how atmospheres develop on planets like Mars or distant exoplanets may need a revision. The process of volcanic outgassing — when gases and other volatile materials escape from molten rock into the air — could work very differently than scientists assumed.

It also offers a new clue for understanding the early "magma oceans" that likely covered other young planets, including some of the worlds orbiting distant stars today.

The bottom line: some of the processes that helped give our planet its breathable air may have started far beneath the surface, in the hidden depths of a restless, rocky world.

## Fun fact

The word **"oxygen"** comes from the Greek words *oxys* (sharp, acid) and *genes* (producer) — coined by the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier in the late 1700s, who believed it was a component of all "acids." He was wrong about that last part, but right about the element itself being a fundamental part of our world.

The experiments that revealed this new discovery were conducted at pressures you'd only find 200 miles underground — yet they may explain one of the most important changes in Earth's entire history.

## Why should I care?

Because the next time you take a breath, remember: the oxygen in that breath may have traveled a journey of thousands of miles — from deep inside the Earth, through ancient volcanoes, and finally into the sky that surrounds us today.
