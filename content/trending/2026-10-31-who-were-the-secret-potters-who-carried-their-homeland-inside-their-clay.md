## A puzzle shelved for a century

About a hundred years ago, archaeologists digging near Tampa Bay in Florida kept pulling up pottery fragments that looked out of place. The decorations matched pottery made hundreds of miles to the north by the Mississippian culture, a society that rose around the year 1000 and spread across much of what is now the eastern United States.

On looks alone, the pieces seemed to belong to that northern world. But maps of Mississippian territory usually left peninsular Florida out. Nobody could say whether the pots had been traded south, copied locally, or made by newcomers. Without the right instruments, museum curators set the fragments on a shelf and the debate stayed open.

## Reading a pot's hidden fingerprint

A team at the Florida Museum of Natural History has now answered the question. The researchers gathered 58 plain and decorated sherds and studied details invisible to the eye.

First, they used neutron activation analysis to compare the pottery's chemistry with local clay deposits. The chemical profiles matched — the pots were made right there on the bay, not shipped in from the north.

Next came petrography. Scientists slice a sherd and polish it until it is about half the width of a human hair, then shine polarised light through it. Tiny grains and voids reveal exactly what a potter mixed into the clay. "It's a sort of fingerprint of the people who made it," said curator Neill Wallis.

## Grog within grog within grog

That fingerprint told a striking story. Four out of five decorated vessels contained grog — crushed pieces of older pottery stirred into the clay to make it stronger. Not one of the plain everyday pots did. The decorated pots were also built by stacking coils of clay, following careful recipes, while everyday vessels were simply moulded.

Inside the grog sat glittering grains of mica, a mineral common in the Florida Panhandle but rare on the peninsula. The clues point to skilled potters who travelled south from the Mississippian heartland and kept working the way they always had.

Most remarkable of all, the grog often contained even older grog — pottery from one generation baked into the pots of the next. "It's sort of like Russian nesting dolls," Wallis said. Every time the newcomers added a pinch of their homeland to the clay, they carried a piece of home forward.

**Fun fact:** The Mississippian potters were such careful builders that they mixed crushed shell or broken pots into their clay — a trick that made their vessels survive the fierce heat of cooking fires.
