# How Did Astronauts Give the ISS a Powerful Solar Upgrade?

**What happened during the 100th NASA spacewalk, and why does it matter for the future of the International Space Station?**

## 1. A Record-Breaking Spacewalk

On October 30, 2026, NASA astronaut Anil Menon and Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk suited up for what would become a historic outing. For seven hours and eleven minutes — longer than a typical workday — they floated outside the International Space Station, working in the silent darkness of space while Earth spun below them.

This wasn't just any spacewalk. It was officially designated U.S. Spacewalk 100, the 100th time NASA astronauts had ventured outside their orbital home for maintenance and upgrades. It was also the 285th spacewalk in the station's history, stretching across decades of international cooperation in space.

## 2. Installing the Seventh Solar Array

During their long shift, Menon and Kutryk installed the seventh ISS Roll-Out Solar Array, or IROSA, on the station's 2A electrical power channel. These new arrays are a modern upgrade to the station's original solar wings — they're lighter, more flexible, and roll out like a measuring tape instead of using heavy rigid panels with mechanical hinges.

Each IROSA is about half the width of the original solar array wings, but they generate significantly more power per square foot thanks to advanced photovoltaic cells. They're installed along the central part of the existing wings, canted at a 10-degree angle to catch the sun more efficiently.

## 3. Powering Up for the Future

Once the final eighth array is installed on November 5, the station's solar power expansion will be complete. The upgrade will raise the station's available electricity from about 160 kilowatts to roughly 215 kilowatts — a boost of up to 30%. That extra power supports critical operations, scientific experiments, and life-support systems for the crew.

The upgrade is especially important because the station's original solar arrays, launched between 2000 and 2009, have been gradually degrading after more than 15 years of service in the harsh space environment. The new IROSA arrays help make up the difference, ensuring the station stays powered strong until its planned decommissioning around 2030.

## 4. What This Means for Space Discovery

More power means more science. With 30% additional electricity, the station can run more experiments, support more crew members, and continue serving as humanity's laboratory in space for years to come. It also paves the way for future deep-space missions, proving that flexible solar array technology can reliably power long-duration missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond.

The IROSA technology isn't just for the ISS — it's already been used on NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) mission and will power future spacecraft, including the Lunar Gateway station planned for lunar orbit.

**Fun fact:** The name "iROSA" stands for "ISS Roll-Out Solar Array," but the underlying technology was first tested on the ISS back in 2017. The flexible, rollable design was inspired by a simple everyday object: a measuring tape! Just like a tape measure unfurls from its case, these solar arrays unroll in space to generate power. And just like a tape measure, they can be rolled back up again when not needed.
