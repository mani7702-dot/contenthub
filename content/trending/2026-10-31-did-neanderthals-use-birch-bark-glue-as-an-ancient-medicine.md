# Did Neanderthals Use Birch Bark Glue as an Ancient Medicine?

Imagine a Neanderthal on a cool autumn morning, peeling strips of white bark from a silver birch tree, then tucking the material into a fire-heated pit to coax out a dark, sticky liquid. For decades archaeologists assumed that goo was simply **glue** — a clever adhesive for hafting stone tools onto wooden spears. But what if it was also the world's earliest **antibiotic ointment**?

A new experiment by researchers in Europe suggests that birch tar, the material our ancient cousins made thousands of years before antibiotics existed, can naturally keep dangerous wound bacteria in check. The discovery hints that Neanderthals understood far more about healing than we have given them credit for.

## The Sticky Survival Trick

Birch tar is a thick, black substance produced when birch bark is heated without oxygen — a process called **dry distillation**. Archaeologists have found traces of it stuck to stone tools at **dozens of Neanderthal sites across Europe**, from caves in Belgium to rock shelters in Germany. Some of those tools are over **50,000 years old**, meaning Neanderthals were crafting this material long before written history. For years the leading theory was practical: tar made an excellent adhesive for attaching sharp stone points to spears. But a team from the University of Cologne, Oxford, and the University of Liège set out to test whether the tar did more than stick.

## A Laboratory in the Paleolithic

The researchers recreated ancient birch-tar production using the very techniques Neanderthals would have had. One method sealed birch bark inside an underground pit and heated it with smoldering embers — exactly the kind of hearth-side chemistry an Ice Age group might have stumbled upon. Another simply burned bark near a cool stone and let the vapors condense onto the surface.

When they tested the resulting tars against **Staphylococcus aureus** — the bacterium behind painful skin infections and stubborn wound sepsis — something remarkable happened. **Every sample inhibited the bacteria's growth**, no matter which traditional technique had produced it. That consistency matters: if only one method had worked, it might have been a lucky accident. Because all the tar samples showed antibacterial activity, the property seems baked into the material itself, not a quirk of a single recipe.

## Medicine in the Shadow of the Ice

So did Neanderthals actually treat their wounds with birch tar? The experiments can't prove it directly — they show the material was capable of fighting infection, but not that it was deliberately applied as medicine. The evidence is still tantalizing. The tar's antibacterial properties appear regardless of how it was made, and Neanderthals lived in a world where even a small cut could turn deadly. As study co-author Tjaark Siemssen puts it, *“Our findings suggest that antimicrobial properties may have played a role as far back as the time of the early Neanderthals.”* The discovery adds another brushstroke to our growing portrait of Neanderthals as resourceful people who understood the natural materials around them.

## Fun Fact

Birch bark is still used in traditional medicine today across northern Europe and parts of Asia, applied to burns, rashes, and insect bites. The compound thought to give it antibacterial power is **betulin**, which modern labs are now studying for its potential against drug-resistant bacteria. The next time you reach for antibiotic ointment, you're carrying on a tradition that began beside a Neanderthal fire.
