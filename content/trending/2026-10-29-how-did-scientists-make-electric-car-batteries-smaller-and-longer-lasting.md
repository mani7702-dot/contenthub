# How did scientists make electric car batteries smaller and longer-lasting?

## 1. The challenge with tomorrow's batteries

Every electric car on the road today relies on lithium-ion batteries — the same kind of battery in your phone or tablet. Inside each one, lithium ions zip back and forth between two electrodes: a cathode on one side and an anode on the other. The anode is usually made of graphite, a form of carbon that is good at storing lithium but also adds weight and takes up space.

What if we could simply **remove the anode entirely**? Without it, batteries could store more energy in a smaller, lighter package — meaning electric cars could drive farther on a single charge without needing bigger, heavier battery packs. That is the promise of **anode-free batteries**, a technology that South Korean scientists say is now closer to reality.

## 2. A microscopic parking lot on copper foil

The problem with anode-free batteries has always been the same: without graphite to catch lithium ions, the metal tends to build up unevenly on the battery's copper surface. Over time, this creates tiny needle-like spikes called **dendrites** that can damage the battery and shorten its life.

Professor Jinwoo Lee and his team at **KAIST** (the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology) had an idea. Instead of letting lithium gather wherever it wants, they turned the copper foil into a microscopic parking lot. Using a semiconductor manufacturing technique called **secondary sputtering lithography**, they etched a grid of tiny tubes — each about **300 nanometers wide** and **150 nanometers tall** — across the copper surface.

Think of it like dividing a big parking lot into hundreds of small spaces so every car has its own spot. With four times more surface area to work with, the lithium distributes itself evenly instead of clumping into dangerous spikes.

## 3. A 10-nanometer-thick invisible shield

But evenly distributing lithium was only half the solution. The protective layer that forms around the battery during charging — called the **solid electrolyte interphase** — also needs to be stable. If it cracks or breaks down, the battery deteriorates faster.

The KAIST team added a second trick: a coating of **MXene**, a two-dimensional material just **10 nanometers thick** (a few thousandths the width of a human hair). This ultra-thin layer acts like primer before paint — it helps a strong, durable protective layer form naturally during the battery's first charge, one rich in **lithium fluoride** that keeps harmful chemical reactions at bay.

Combined, these two innovations — microscopic tubes and a nanoscale primer — dramatically extend the battery's lifespan without adding bulk or weight.

## 4. What this could mean for electric cars

Today's electric vehicle batteries are heavy and take up most of the floor space in the car. Anode-free batteries that last longer and deliver more energy per pound could let manufacturers build **smaller battery packs that go just as far** — or give existing cars a major range boost.

The research team, which also included scientists from Kyungpook National University and the National Nano Fab Center, published their findings in the journal *Advanced Functional Materials*. Their approach borrows techniques straight from computer-chip production, where manufacturing precision reaches the sub-microscopic level.

## Fun Fact

A human hair is roughly 80,000–100,000 nanometers thick. The MXene coating on this battery is only 10 nanometers — meaning you could line up **8,000 of them side by side** and still be thinner than a single strand of hair!

## For the grown-ups

Anode-free batteries operate by allowing lithium to plate directly onto a bare copper current collector during the first charge, rather than storing it in a pre-existing graphite anode. Prior research has shown that the lack of a structured anode leads to uneven lithium deposition, dendrite formation, and rapid capacity fade. The KAIST-led team (Professors Jinwoo Lee and Hee-Tae Jung, along with Eunji Kim, Hyunju Jung, and Jinuk Kim as co-first authors) addressed this by (1) patterning the copper foil with a grid of ~300 nm × 150 nm microscopic tubes using secondary sputtering lithography (SSL), increasing effective surface area ~4×, and (2) applying a ~10 nm MXene coating that promotes formation of a stable solid-electrolyte interphase rich in lithium fluoride (LiF) when cycled in a LiPF₆-based electrolyte. The study was published in *Advanced Functional Materials* (DOI: 10.1002/adfm.77931) on September 1, 2026, with funding from the National Research Foundation of Korea. Wikipedia's "Anode-free battery" article confirms the general technology and trade-offs of AFBs; this paper's surface-engineering approach is a novel advancement not previously reported.
