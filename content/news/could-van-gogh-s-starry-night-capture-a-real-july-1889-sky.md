# Could Van Gogh's Starry Night Capture a Real July 1889 Sky?

## 1. A painting that might be a real photograph of the sky

Few images are as instantly recognizable as Vincent van Gogh's *The Starry Night*. Its swirling blues and yellows, bright crescent moon, and dazzling star cluster have inspired generations of art lovers. For over a century, scholars have debated whether the sky in the painting is pure imagination — or a faithful sketch of a real night. Now, a new study suggests it might be the latter, and the date written in history books may be wrong.

## 2. Why July 22, 1889, matters

Art historians have long dated the painting to **June 1889**, based on a letter Van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo around June 18th, where he mentioned working on a "new study of a starry sky." But there's a problem: around that time, the Moon appeared nearly full — not the thin crescent visible in the painting. And June's pre-dawn sky didn't feature the bright stars Van Gogh painted.

The new research, led by astronomer Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim from SLLAB and Kyung Hee University in South Korea, points to **July 22, 1889** instead. Around that morning, the sky over Saint-Rémy, France — where Van Gogh stayed at an asylum — showed exactly what we see on canvas: a waning crescent Moon, the bright planet Venus, and a cluster of stars arranged in a distinctive V-shape.

## 3. The Hyades cluster and the science of matching stars

That V-shaped cluster is the **Hyades**, the nearest open star cluster to our Solar System and the brightest cluster representing the constellation **Taurus**. Five bright stars form its recognizable pattern, clearly visible without a telescope on a dark night.

The researchers used a technique called **Procrustes shape analysis** — a statistical method that aligns shapes by removing differences in position, size, and rotation — to compare the star patterns in the painting with astronomical data. The match between the five central stars in *The Starry Night* and the Hyades was striking.

> "The relative positions of the stars preserve the skeleton of the Hyades," the researchers concluded, indicating that "the work did not invent a sky that was not there but transferred an observed arrangement onto canvas through stylistic amplification."

The team also confirmed their method by first testing it on two of Van Gogh's earlier 1888 night paintings — *Café Terrace at Night* and another — where the vantage points are well documented. The Hyades appear to match the Taurus constellation as it would have been seen that July morning over 130 years ago.

## 4. What this tells us about art and science

While Van Gogh may have mixed observation with imagination — as any artist does — the study suggests the famous swirls are rooted in a real celestial moment, painted from his window at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum. The research, published in *Scientific Reports*, also revisits Van Gogh's documented seizure history, proposing it may have occurred in late July rather than mid-July, which would have still left him able to observe the sky.

Whether the sky is a perfect astronomical portrait or a beautifully stylized interpretation, the study reminds us that art and astronomy have been gazing at the same stars for centuries — each seeing something different, yet both finding wonder in the night.

## Fun Fact

Van Gogh painted *The Starry Night* while staying at the asylum in Saint-Rémy — but the view from his window didn't include the cypress tree that towers prominently in the foreground. Many scholars believe he added that dark, flame-like tree from memory or imagination, making the painting a blend of real observation and personal expression.
