# How does the brain follow a story while you sleep through surgery?

Imagine drifting off for surgery. To you, no time passes at all. Yet a new study suggests that deep inside your head, part of your brain might still be wide awake — listening.

Researchers at Baylor College of Medicine found that people under full general anesthesia can still process language. Their brains told nouns from verbs, followed the plot of a short story, and even predicted which word would come next.

## Why the brain was studied this way

The team worked with patients undergoing epilepsy surgery, who already had tiny sensors placed in their brains. This gave doctors a rare chance to watch brain cells in action.

They used hair-thin recording devices called Neuropixels probes to listen in on hundreds of single neurons in the **hippocampus** — a seahorse-shaped region key to learning and memory. It was the first time this tool had been used on that part of the brain.

## What the sleeping brain did

In one test, patients heard a repeated beep, sometimes broken by a different tone. Even while unconscious, their brain cells noticed the odd sound — and their responses sharpened over time. That is a sign of **neuroplasticity**, the brain's ability to learn from experience.

Next came something trickier: short spoken stories. The hippocampus tracked the words in real time, sorting them by type and building up a sense of where the sentence was heading.

"The brain appears to anticipate what comes next in a story, even without conscious awareness," said Dr. Sameer Sheth, the neurosurgeon who led the work published in the journal *Nature*.

## What it means for the rest of us

So if the brain can understand and predict without us, what is consciousness actually for? One idea is that awareness depends on many brain regions working together — not just one small part going quiet or waking up. The findings could also help researchers build better tools to help people communicate after brain injuries.

## Fun fact

Your hippocampus is named after the ancient Greek word for seahorse, because early anatomists thought its curved shape looked just like one.
