# How Did Insects Conquer Land? Meet the 324-Million-Year-Old Swimmer With 24 Legs

Today, insects are the most species-rich animal group on Earth. Beetles, bees, ants and butterflies live almost everywhere on dry ground. So how did their ancestors get out of the ocean in the first place?

A newly identified fossil from western Texas suggests the answer is stranger — and slower — than anyone expected.

## A creature stuck between two worlds

Scientists named the new species *Chosha praecursor*. It lived about 324 million years ago, during a time called the Late Mississippian, in a shallow delta near an ancient coastline.

The animal had the makings of a true insect: a segmented body, a pair of antennae, an egg-laying organ called an ovipositor and six walking legs on its chest. No living insect has that arrangement.

But its belly told a different story. Segments 1 through 9 still carried jointed appendages, and the rear ones had widened into broad, paddle-like limbs — perfect for pushing through water. All told, its body carried about two dozen legs. No living insect keeps anything like them.

## Neither swimmer nor walker, but both

That mix is the exciting part. It means early insects didn't simply crawl out of the sea and stay there. They spent millions of years in the wet space between water and dry land — marshes, deltas and damp shorelines — where paddles and walking legs were both useful at once.

The team, led by Prof. Chenyang Cai of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Erik Tihelka of the University of Cambridge, published their findings in the journal *Nature*. As insect ancestors leaned less on swimming, those paddle limbs shrank away. Losing them likely made walking easier and freed the abdomen for other jobs, like digestion and reproduction.

## Filling an 80-million-year gap

The fossil record was famously patchy here. The oldest clear hexapod fossils are about 405 million years old, and recognizable insects don't turn up in numbers until much later — leaving a gap of roughly 80 million years.

By comparing the Texas find with other puzzling fossils from Scotland and Illinois, the researchers showed these creatures belong to one ancient stem lineage. Together, they form the oldest known insect group and help close that missing chapter.

## Fun fact

The fossils were long mistaken for crustacean larvae and sat mislabelled in museum collections for years. It took special cross-polarized light to reveal they were really adult female insects — a reminder that sometimes the answer has been sitting in a drawer all along.
