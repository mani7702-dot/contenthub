# What Is the 'Cheshire Cat's Grin' Hiding Inside a Superconductor?

Remember the Cheshire Cat from *Alice in Wonderland*? It vanishes — but its grin stays behind, floating in the air. Physicists at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign have just found a real-world version of that grin, hiding inside a special metal called uranium ditelluride.

## The cold magic

Superconductivity is one of the strangest tricks in nature. Cool a suitable metal far below freezing and something wonderful happens: its electrons pair up and glide through the material with zero resistance — no lost energy, no wasted heat. This is already used in MRI scanners and is hoped to one day power cleaner grids and faster computers.

Those paired electrons are called Cooper pairs, named after physicist Leon Cooper, who helped explain them in 1957.

## A hidden ripple

For decades, scientists assumed these pairs spread out evenly. But back in 2007, a team led by Illinois professor Eduardo Fradkin predicted something odd: the pairs could organise themselves into repeating ripples, like patterns in sand. They called this a "pair density wave."

For nearly twenty years, those ripples were hard to pin down. Thanks to new ways of growing purer samples, the team could finally watch them directly and see that they matched the theory exactly.

## The grin that stays

The real surprise is what happens when the sample warms up. Normally, the superconducting state disappears above a certain temperature. Yet the rippled pattern kept going — faintly, but clearly there.

That lingering signal means the electron pairs may begin forming *before* the full magic kicks in. "Pair density waves are the Cheshire Cat's grin of superconductivity," Fradkin said — the leftover smile after the cat has gone.

Published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, the work helps scientists understand a puzzling family of "unconventional" superconductors discovered back in 1986. Understanding them better could bring us closer to materials that carry electricity perfectly at higher, more practical temperatures.

**Fun Fact**

If you could keep a superconducting ring looped forever, its electric current would keep circling for years without ever running down — because there is nothing to slow it.
