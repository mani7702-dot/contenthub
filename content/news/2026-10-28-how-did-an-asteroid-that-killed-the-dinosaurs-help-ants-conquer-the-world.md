# How Did an Asteroid That Killed the Dinosaurs Help Ants Conquer the World?

## 1. When the Sky Fell 66 Million Years Ago

Picture this: 66 million years ago, a massive asteroid — about 10 kilometers across — slammed into what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. The impact was so powerful it threw dust and debris high into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight for months. The age of the dinosaurs came to an end, and a whole new chapter for life on Earth began.

But here's a surprising twist: that same cosmic collision may have set the stage for one of Earth's tiniest — yet most triumphant — creatures to take center stage. Scientists now believe the asteroid's aftermath gave ants the genetic tools they needed to conquer the world.

## 2. Jumping Genes: Nature's Surprise Engineers

Inside every living thing's DNA are mysterious segments called "jumping genes" — officially known as **transposable elements** or **transposons**. These are pieces of genetic code that can move around, copy and paste themselves into new locations, and sometimes even wake up ancient dormant sequences.

For decades, scientists thought these mobile elements were just "genomic parasites" — like viruses that replicate without helping their host. But more recently, researchers have discovered that jumping genes can also be powerful drivers of evolution, creating new genetic variations that natural selection can act upon.

## 3. The Genetic Surge That Coincided with Ant Success

An international team led by **Dr. Lukas Schrader** from the University of Münster in Germany studied the genomes of **163 ant species** — representing twelve of the sixteen ant subfamilies that exist today. By comparing these genomes, they traced how jumping gene activity changed over the past 100 million years.

What they found was striking: several major ant lineages experienced separate surges in jumping gene activity during the **early Paleogene period** — roughly 66 million years ago, right when the asteroid hit. These genetic bursts happened just before these ant groups began rapidly expanding into the thousands of species we see today.

"The asteroid impact had dramatic consequences for the environment," explains Schrader. "We have now found the genomic mechanism that connects these ecological upheavals to the subsequent rapid diversification of the ants."

## 4. Better Communication, More Species

The researchers discovered something else fascinating: lineages with the greatest numbers of jumping genes tended to have the most ant species alive today. It seems these mobile DNA segments didn't just shuffle genetic code randomly — they may have helped sharpen ants' most vital skill: **chemical communication**.

Jumping genes appear to have contributed to the expansion of gene families responsible for **odorant receptors** — the molecular antennas that let ants detect smells. This is essential for nearly every aspect of ant social behavior: finding food trails, recognizing nest-mates, coordinating colony activities, and even waging quiet wars between rival colonies.

With better smell sensors, ants became better at cooperating, organizing, and exploiting new ecological niches left empty after the dinosaurs' departure. From that point on, they were unstoppable.

## Fun Fact

Jumping genes were first discovered by **Barbara McClintock** in the 1940s, studying corn. At first, her peers were skeptical — the idea that genes could move around was so radical that it took decades to be accepted. She was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1983 for her discovery, proving that sometimes the most revolutionary ideas seem impossible at first.
