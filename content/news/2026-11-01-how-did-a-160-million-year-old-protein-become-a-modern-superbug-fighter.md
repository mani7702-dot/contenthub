# How Did a 160-Million-Year-Old Protein Become a Modern Superbug Fighter?

Imagine a tiny weapon that has been handed down through your family for 160 million years. Long before the first dinosaur movies, long before the first mammal ever walked the Earth, an immune molecule began guarding bodies just like yours. Now, biologists at the University of Oregon have brought that ancient molecule back to life — and discovered it still knows how to fight.

## A Bodyguard Older Than the Dinosaurs' Goodbye

About 160 million years ago, near the end of the Jurassic Period, the shared ancestor of nearly all mammals — from mice to whales to us — first appeared. Around the very same time, a protein called **lactoferrin** showed up too. You carry it today in almost every body fluid except blood: breast milk, tears, saliva, and even the mucus that lines your gut. Its main job is simple and clever: it grabs iron and holds on tight. Bacteria need iron to grow, so by hiding the iron, lactoferrin quietly starves them ahead of time.

But lactoferrin hides a second trick. Tucked inside it is a short chain of amino acids — a **peptide** — that can poke holes in bacterial membranes and burst the cells.

## Reading an Ancient Recipe Book

To find out how that weapon changed over time, lead author Titas Sil compared the lactoferrin genes of living animals, such as humans and cows. Using a method called **ancestral sequence reconstruction**, the team worked backwards to estimate the genetic recipes carried by long-extinct ancestors.

Then they did something remarkable: they rebuilt those vanished proteins in the lab and pitted them against real disease-causing bacteria.

## The Surprise: A Single Tiny Change

Here is what stunned the researchers. The oldest reconstructed peptides could still damage bacteria — but the germs patched themselves up and survived. Versions rebuilt from more recent ancestors, however, grew steadily stronger. Some fought better than the versions found in humans today.

And the reason was astonishingly small: a **single mutation** in the amino-acid chain was enough to make the molecule dramatically more powerful.

"What was surprising and unexpected was how small changes in these domains could have such large effects," said senior author Matt Barber.

## Why It Matters

The team is careful to say these ancient peptides are not medicines yet — inside the body, they break down quickly. But they offer a treasure map. As bacteria keep evolving around our current antibiotics, understanding how these natural defenses changed across millions of years could help scientists design cleverer treatments.

"Evolution is essentially a billions-year-old science experiment," Barber said. "We're seeing the results of what worked and what didn't work."

**Fun fact:** Your tears contain lactoferrin, which is part of why crying can help keep your eyes clean — an ancient defense that may have been protecting mammal eyes since the age of the dinosaurs.
