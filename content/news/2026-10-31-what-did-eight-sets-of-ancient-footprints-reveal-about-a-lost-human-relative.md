# What Did Eight Sets of Ancient Footprints Reveal About a Lost Human Relative?

More than a million years ago, a group of early human relatives trudged along the muddy edge of a lake in what is now northern Kenya. The ground was soft, the water was near, and the moment should have been forgotten forever. Instead, it left a set of fossil footprints that scientists are only now learning to read.

A new study describes **eight sets of tracks** pressed into an ancient lakeshore about **1.4 million years ago** — and the walkers turn out to be surprising in more ways than one.

## A Snapshot of a Single Moment

Bones can pile up over thousands of years, but footprints are different. A walking surface forms and hardens in just hours or days, so a set of tracks can capture a real moment in time — who was there, and what they were doing.

The international team, made up of researchers from the United States and Kenya, concluded that all eight trackmakers were likely adults. Most appear to have been travelling together as a group.

"They may have lived in large groups," says co-author Neil Roach of Harvard University, hinting at a complex social life for a species that vanished long ago.

## Bigger Than Anyone Expected

The tracks belonged to ***Paranthropus boisei***, a stocky, big-jawed relative nicknamed "Nutcracker Man" for its powerful chewing teeth. For decades, almost everything scientists knew about this species came from skulls — fossils of the rest of the body are rare.

So the size of the footprints came as a genuine shock. Lead author Kevin Hatala, of Chatham University and the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, reports that the tracks point to **body sizes up to about 1.8 metres tall and 75 kilograms** — remarkably human-like for a relative long assumed to be small.

The find challenges earlier ideas that *Paranthropus boisei* was much smaller than *Homo erectus*, the species that lived alongside it and is considered a possible direct ancestor of modern humans.

## Reading Steps Like a Story

The researchers identified the footprints using analytical techniques their team first developed in 2024, reading clues from foot anatomy and the way the walkers moved. The discovery is part of a much larger collection of hominin footprints found around ancient lakeshores near Lake Turkana — a region that keeps giving up rare glimpses of our deep past.

**Fun fact:** Footprints are one of the only kinds of evidence that can show *behaviour* rather than just anatomy. At the famous site of Laetoli in Tanzania, a set of 3.7-million-year-old tracks even preserves the moment one individual paused and turned to look back.
