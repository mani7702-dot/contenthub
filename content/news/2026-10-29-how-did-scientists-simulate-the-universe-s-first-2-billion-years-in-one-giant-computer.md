# How Did Scientists Simulate the Universe's First 2 Billion Years in One Giant Computer?

## 1. A time machine made of math

Imagine trying to replay the entire universe from its earliest days — every swirl of gas, every flicker of light, every invisible dance of dark matter. That is exactly what a team of astronomers has done with **Lumina**, one of the largest and most detailed computer simulations of the early universe ever created.

Built by researchers at the **Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian**, Lumina models the universe's first **2 billion years** after the Big Bang. That covers everything from the "dark ages" when the cosmos was still filling with light, to the birth of the very first galaxies and the moment the universe truly began to take shape.

## 2. Powering the cosmic simulation

Recreating 2 billion years of cosmic history is not a job for an ordinary laptop. Lumina ran on **Frontier**, one of the world's fastest supercomputers, using over **15,000 graphics processing units** (GPUs) working together at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee.

The virtual universe Lumina created is enormous — a cube roughly **1.5 billion light-years** on each side. That is big enough to capture entire regions of space that today's most powerful telescopes, like the **James Webb Space Telescope** (JWST) and the upcoming **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope**, are just beginning to peer into.

## 3. Helium, darkness, and the first light

One of Lumina's biggest breakthroughs is how it handles **helium reionization** — a process where radiation strips electrons from helium atoms across the early universe. For the first time, a simulation this large follows that transformation unfold while simultaneously tracking how it affects the surrounding cosmos.

This matters because helium reionization changed how light traveled through space, which in turn influenced how the first stars and galaxies formed. By comparing Lumina's predictions with JWST observations, scientists can check whether their theories match reality — or discover something entirely new.

## 4. Watching the universe wake up

With Lumina, astronomers can watch the universe evolve second by second in the simulation. They can see how the earliest galaxies lit up pockets of darkness, how gas flowed between them, and how radiation shaped the space between all the structures.

Any surprises in the simulated cosmos — spots where the virtual universe behaves differently from the real one — could point to physics that scientists don't yet understand. That is the exciting part: Lumina does not just confirm what we already know. It might reveal what we are missing.

## Try this at home

Think of Lumina like a gigantic game of cosmic Sims — but instead of people, you are tracking billions of particles of gas and dark matter across 2 billion years of time. If the universe were a song, Lumina has replayed the first few notes over and over, letting scientists hear exactly how the cosmic symphony began.

## Fun Fact

The name **"Lumina"** is fitting — it comes from the Latin word for "light." These scientists did not just simulate the early universe; they simulated the moment when the universe first lit up.

## for-grown-ups

The Lumina simulation was developed by researchers at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian, led by Oliver Zier. The simulation models the universe's first 2 billion years after the Big Bang, covering a virtual volume of approximately 1.5 billion light-years per side. It was run on the Frontier supercomputer using over 15,000 GPUs at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Lumina's novel treatment of helium reionization — following both hydrogen and helium reionization across the full volume — is a major advance over previous simulations. The results were published in the *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society* (DOI: 10.1093/mnras/stag1469). The simulation is designed to help interpret observations from the James Webb Space Telescope and the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.
