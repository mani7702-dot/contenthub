Every morning, millions of people open Wordle, type a five-letter word, and wait for the little squares to turn green, yellow, or grey. Six guesses. One hidden word. Most of us play by feel. But researchers at Binghamton University, State University of New York, decided to play it with mathematics instead — and their method solved 99% of puzzles in simulations.

## The Counterintuitive Trick

Here is the surprising part: the best guess is often not the word most likely to be right. It is the word that will teach you the most.

The team leaned on Shannon entropy, a way of measuring uncertainty developed in the 1940s. Instead of asking "Which word is probably the answer?", they asked "Which word will shrink my list of possibilities the fastest?" Sometimes a strange-looking word with unusual letters splits the remaining options into tiny groups — even though it could never be the final answer itself.

"The objective shifts to maximizing the expected reduction in uncertainty rather than the probability of being right," explained doctoral student Donald Stephens.

## How Well Did It Work?

The numbers speak for themselves. Compared with a sensible everyday strategy built around common letters like A, E, and R, the information-theory approach pulled far ahead:

- Shared-letter strategy in simulations: 90% solved
- Information-theory strategy in simulations: 99% solved

The project wasn't meant to be serious research at all. It began as a class assignment from Assistant Professor Congyu "Peter" Wu, who challenged students to show how information theory could solve a real problem. It is now published work.

## What It Means for Your Morning Game

To use the strategy yourself, you would need a small computer program: after each guess, you would type in the colours Wordle gave you, and the software would pick the next most informative word. That is a lot of effort for a coffee-break habit — but the underlying lesson is easy. When you are stuck, a bold, information-rich guess beats a safe one.

## Fun Fact

The idea of "entropy" was popularised by engineer Claude Shannon in 1948 to measure how much information a message carries. Seven decades later, the same mathematics turns out to be very good at beating a word game played by millions of people a day.
