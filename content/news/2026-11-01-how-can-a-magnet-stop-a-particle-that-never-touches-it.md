# How Can a Magnet Stop a Particle That Never Touches It?

## 1. A Strange Prediction From 1959

Imagine a racetrack loop with a magnet hidden in the middle, sealed inside a box. A runner laps the track but never crosses the box. Common sense says the magnet cannot possibly affect the runner. Yet in the quantum world, that is exactly what can happen.

Back in 1959, two physicists, **Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm**, predicted something remarkable: a charged particle travelling *around* a region containing a magnetic field still picks up a measurable change in its quantum state — even though it never travels through the field itself. For decades this idea, now called the **Aharonov–Bohm effect**, stood as one of the clearest proofs that the quantum world follows rules that would seem impossible in everyday life.

## 2. Rebuilding the Puzzle in a Quantum Computer

Researchers at the **University of Oxford** have now recreated this effect in a brand-new setting. Reporting in the journal *Nature Physics*, the team used a *hybrid quantum computer* — a machine that blends two kinds of quantum components working side by side.

- **Qubits**, the basic units of quantum information, were encoded in the internal states of trapped ions and played the role of magnetic fields.
- **Quantum oscillators**, the gentle vibrations of those same ions, represented the particles of matter.

By linking them into a loop, the scientists let a simulated particle travel around a region of magnetic flux. As it went, the particle's movement ground to a complete stop — slowed by an invisible influence from a field it never once crossed.

## 3. Why It Matters

These "lattice" models describe how matter interacts with the fundamental forces of the universe. As the systems grow larger, the maths becomes so demanding that even the most powerful supercomputers struggle. Quantum computers offer a shortcut: instead of *calculating* how a complicated system behaves, you build a physical system that behaves the same way and simply watch it.

The Oxford team, working with a colleague in Madrid, built this experiment over several years. Their success hints that quantum machines could one day tackle some of the hardest problems in particle and high-energy physics.

## Fun fact

The Aharonov–Bohm effect is famous in physics classrooms because it reminds us that in quantum mechanics, *how* a particle gets somewhere matters — the invisible paths it does not take can still change its destiny.
