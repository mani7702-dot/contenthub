# How Did a 23-Foot Crocodilian Rule Ancient South America's Food Chain?

More than ten million years ago, much of what is now South America lay under a vast lake. Its warm waters and green shores were crowded with life — giant turtles, armadillos the size of small cars, and herds of strange hoofed animals that tipped the scales at more than a tonne each.

With so much prey around, you might expect mammals to have been in charge. A new study says otherwise: the region's reigning predator was a colossal crocodylian.

## Meet Purussaurus

The star of the story is *Purussaurus neivensis*, an ancient relative of today's caimans. It grew to about seven metres (23 feet) long and weighed roughly 1,800 kilograms — about as heavy as a small car.

At that size, there was little in its world it could not tackle. Researchers think it may have been powerful enough to shape entire populations of plant-eating animals, not just pick off stragglers one by one.

## Reading the Bite Marks

How do you tell who was eating whom millions of years ago? You look at the bones.

The team, led by Oscar Wilson at the University of Helsinki, studied fossils held in museum collections, ranging from 10.5 to 16 million years old. Bite and tooth marks left on the bones of herbivores are some of the only direct clues about real hunts — proof that an animal actually fed, rather than just looking the part.

The marks told a clear story. Crocodylians, and Purussaurus especially, left the most tells of all.

## A Crowded World of Predators

South America's Miocene was no quiet place. Sharing the shores were anacondas, saber-toothed mammals, huge flightless "terror birds," and sebecids — land-dwelling cousins of crocodylians.

Even against that competition, the study suggests crocodylians were the most important hunters, particularly when it came to bringing down big prey. As Wilson put it, there was simply so much prey that mammalian predators could not have eaten it all.

**Fun fact:** Today's caimans are the much smaller living relatives of Purussaurus — a reminder that the crocodilian family tree once included true giants.
