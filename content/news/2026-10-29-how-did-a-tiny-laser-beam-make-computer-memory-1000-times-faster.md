# How Did a Tiny Laser Beam Make Computer Memory 1,000 Times Faster?

## 1. Can Light Replace Magnets Inside Your Computer’s Brain?

Every time you save a photo, send a text, or stream a song, a microscopic part of your device flips tiny magnetic switches — over and over — to record that information as 1s and 0s. Those switches live inside what we call computer memory, and they are normally controlled with magnetic fields.

But what if, instead of magnets, scientists could use beams of light? Researchers at the University of California, San Diego have shown that a carefully shaped laser pulse can flip those magnetic switches — and it could happen more than **1000 times faster** than today’s methods. That means tomorrow’s phones, tablets, and laptops might wake up from sleep in a blink.

The study was published on September 15, 2026 in *Nature Communications* (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-77572-3).

## 2. The Trick: Shaping Light Like a Sculptor

Modern hard drives and memory chips store data in layers of magnetic material — think platinum and cobalt stacked in ultra-thin sheets. To write new information, tiny electromagnets flip the direction of each microscopic magnet. That process works, but it takes energy and limits how fast data can be saved.

The UC San Diego team tried something different. Instead of fighting with magnetic fields, they turned a **specialized laser** into a precision tool. By reshaping and shrinking the beam — making it tens of orders of magnitude smaller than anything used before — they concentrated laser energy into an incredibly tiny spot.

Here is how it works:

1. The first laser pulses briefly heat a small patch of the magnetic material, causing part of it to flip its magnetic orientation.
2. Additional pulses gradually enlarge that flipped region until the new magnetic state becomes stable.
3. Because the light is so precisely engineered, the whole process works **without needing a particular polarization** — a headache that tripped up earlier experiments.

“We've optically engineered the light to change the physics that's happening in the material at the micro- and nanoscale,” said Muhammad Waleed Khalid, a Ph.D. student and first author on the paper.

## 3. Why This Could Change How We Store Everything

The researchers demonstrated their technique on a magnetic structure containing **nine alternating layers** of platinum and cobalt — substantially thicker than the three layers that worked in earlier experiments. That extra thickness matters because thicker magnetic stacks can hold data longer, which is important for reliable, long-term memory.

And because the beam can be focused onto remarkably small areas, future devices could pack **more data into less space** — imagine storing an entire library on a chip the size of a fingernail.

The “All-optical switching” approach the team used is known in the scientific world, but previous attempts were stuck on two big problems: they required materials with very specific magnetic properties, and they needed the light’s polarization to be set in one exact direction. This new method sidesteps both limitations by letting the **shape of the light beam** do the work instead.

## 4. The Roadblocks Still Ahead

Of course, today’s experiments use a large, specialized ultrafast laser — the kind that does not fit on a computer chip. Bringing this technology into everyday devices will take more work. The team is exploring new magnetic materials that respond to simpler laser systems, and trying to shrink the laser beam down to just a few hundred nanometers.

But the breakthrough shows a promising path forward: a future where your devices write data with light, not magnets — making them faster, smaller, and far more energy-efficient.

## Fun Fact

The word **“laser”** stands for *Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation*. It was first coined in 1960 by physicist Theodore Maiman, who built the very first working laser just a year earlier.

The UC San Diego team used a platinum-cobalt multilayer — the same kind of “sandwich” material used in every modern hard drive — as the canvas for their light-shaping experiment.
