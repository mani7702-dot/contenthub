# How Can One Beam of Light Steer Another in 74 Quadrillionths of a Second?

### 1. Light is fast — so what's the holdup?

Light travels so quickly that it can cross the width of a human hair in about 74 femtoseconds (that's 74 *quadrillionths* of a second). Yet the tools we use to control light have always been surprisingly sluggish by comparison. Today's beam-steering devices must physically tilt mirrors, twist liquid crystals, or push electrons into new energy states before they snap back. Each of those steps — even when it sounds speedy — operates on nanosecond or picosecond timescales, which is thousands of times slower than the blink we are talking about. For photonic computers and ultra-sensitive cameras, that lag is a real bottleneck.

### 2. Steering light with light

A research team at the California Institute of Technology has found a way to skip the mechanical middleman entirely. Their device lets one beam of light redirect another, using an ultrathin silicon surface patterned with features smaller than the wavelength of light. The approach leans on the **optical Kerr effect**: when an intense "control" pulse briefly changes a material's refractive index, a second "probe" beam passing through the same spot has its direction altered. Because no electrons are promoted to long-lasting excited states, the change can appear and vanish almost as fast as the light pulse itself.

### 3. The metasurface amplifier

On its own, the Kerr effect is too feeble to turn a beam by a useful amount. The Caltech group solved that by engineering a **metasurface** — a flat sheet studded with a forest of nanoscale silicon pillars. By tuning the size and spacing of those pillars, the surface traps incoming light for just a little while longer, letting it "circulate" instead of passing straight through. That extra interaction time amplifies the tiny refractive-index shift until it becomes strong enough to steer the probe beam — by up to 13 degrees on demand.

> **Fun fact:** The speed limit of the device is set by the laser pulses that run it (also 74 femtoseconds), not by the fundamental physics of the metasurface itself — so even faster tricks may be possible as the lasers improve.

### 4. Why you might notice it

Because controlling light at light-speed scales would matter everywhere light already does. Faster beam steering could sharpen the cameras in self-driving cars, speed up the data links between computer chips, and enable sensors that catch events too brief for today's instruments. It could also point toward emerging photonic ideas — like time crystals and synthetic time-varying materials — that rely on reshaping light faster than any electronic switch can manage.

The same idea, the researchers say, could one day let a single chip reprogram how light travels across its surface hundreds of times before a single conventional clock tick finishes.
