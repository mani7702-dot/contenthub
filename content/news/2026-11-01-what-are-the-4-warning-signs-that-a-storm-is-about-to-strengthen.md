# What Are the 4 Warning Signs That a Storm Is About to Strengthen?

## 1. Why Some Storms Stammer

A hurricane can look enormous and dramatic from a satellite and still struggle to grow. The reason often hides in how the storm is built — not how big it is.

Meteorologists describe storms as having a "tilt." Picture a spinning top that leans sideways as it rises through the air. The storm's turning center near the ocean and its turning center higher up are not lined up. Strong upper-level winds can push the top of the storm away from its base, leaving the whole thing leaning.

And a leaning storm usually cannot intensify much — until it stands up straight.

## 2. Thirty Years of Storm Chasing

To find out what tips a leaning storm upright, researchers at the University of Miami's Rosenstiel School and NOAA's Atlantic Oceanographic and Meteorological Laboratory turned to a remarkable treasure trove. They used nearly three decades of radar readings gathered by NOAA's Hurricane Hunter aircraft — the planes that fly straight into storms.

Their database holds 1,510 radar analyses collected across 28 hurricane seasons, from 1997 through 2024. By comparing storms that eventually straightened with ones that stayed tilted, clear patterns emerged.

Lead author Michael S. Fischer put it simply: "A tropical cyclone has to stand up straight before it can intensify."

## 3. The Four Clues

The team identified four signs that a tilted storm is likely to line up and strengthen:

1. **A tight, compact spin** close to the ocean surface.
2. **A favourable tilt** — the lean points in a helpful direction compared with the surrounding winds.
3. **Vigorous rising air and heavy rain** near the storm's lower center.
4. **A friendly environment** — warm ocean water, moist air, and gentle mid-level winds.

The storms that aligned "already looked different about a day beforehand," Fischer added. Those powerful thunderstorms near the center may do more than signal organization — they may actively help pull the storm upright.

## Fun fact

NOAA's Hurricane Hunters fly directly into the eye of storms to drop sensors and take radar readings — so some of the most important storm science comes from sending a plane where everyone else is flying out.
