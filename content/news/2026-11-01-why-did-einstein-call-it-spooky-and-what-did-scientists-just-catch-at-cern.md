## A rule that makes no sense — but works anyway

Imagine two coins that always land on the same side, no matter how far apart you carry them. Flip one here in a lab, and the other instantly "knows" what happened thousands of kilometres away. That impossible-sounding idea is real, and it has a name: **quantum entanglement**.

Albert Einstein didn't like it one bit. He called the idea **"spooky action at a distance."** Yet more than a century later, scientists keep finding it hiding in the most surprising places — including, now, inside the most powerful machine humans have ever built.

## Catching spooky action at record energies

Physicists working at **CERN's Large Hadron Collider** near Geneva have found strong evidence of entanglement between two extremely short-lived particles called **Z bosons**, produced when Higgs bosons decay. The findings were published in the journal *Physical Review Letters*.

Here's the tricky part. Z bosons vanish in a tiny fraction of a second. So the team studied the **electrons and muons** they leave behind, carefully measuring the angles at which those particles fly out. Those angles let researchers rebuild what the original Z bosons were doing — and reveal whether the pair was entangled.

They were. And it happened during proton collisions at **13 trillion electron volts**, with particles racing along at 99.99% the speed of light. That makes it one of the highest-energy confirmations of entanglement ever achieved.

## Why this is a big deal

Entanglement is already the secret ingredient behind quantum computers, ultra-secure communication, and super-precise sensors. But it's usually tested on gentle, delicate systems like single photons or trapped ions.

Finding it alive and well in particles this heavy, created in collisions this violent, tells us something profound: this strange rule of quantum mechanics isn't fragile at all. It's woven into the fabric of reality — from a quiet laboratory bench to the heart of a particle collider.

"This shows just how fundamental and robust this quantum effect really is," said University of Oxford's **Professor Alan Barr**, a study co-author who was among the first to suggest colliders could probe entanglement. He was involved in building the LHC itself.

## Fun fact

The word "quark" — the name for the tiny building blocks inside protons and neutrons — was borrowed by physicist Murray Gell-Mann from a line of nonsense poetry in James Joyce's novel *Finnegans Wake*: "Three quarks for Muster Mark!" Sometimes science names things in the most playful ways.
