# Could Your Favorite Diet Soda Be Aging Your Brain Faster?

## The sweetener puzzle

Maria always reaches for the diet soda at lunch. It's sugar-free, so what could be the harm, right? But what if that daily can of "zero sugar" is quietly speeding up her brain's aging? A major new study suggests that for people who drink diet sodas, chew sugar-free gum, or snack on "zero calorie" treats every day, the sweet choice might cost the brain more than scientists once thought.

## What the study found

Researchers at the University of São Paulo in Brazil followed **12,772 adults** for about **eight years**. When the study began, participants were an average of 52 years old and filled out detailed food diaries describing everything they ate and drank in the previous year.

The team then divided everyone into three groups based on how much artificial sweetener they consumed each day:
- **Lowest group:** 20 milligrams per day (about 1/19th of a diet soda)
- **Middle group:** moderate consumption  
- **Highest group:** 191 milligrams per day (about one full diet soda)

At the start, middle, and end of the study, all participants took memory and thinking tests three times. These measured how quickly they could recall words, how fast they processed information, and how well they held onto thoughts in their working memory.

## Six sweeteners are under suspicion

The researchers tracked **seven** common sweeteners: aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol, and tagatose. You have probably seen these listed on ingredient labels — they show up in diet sodas, sugar-free gums, "zero sugar" snacks, protein bars, fiber supplements, and even some medicines.

After crunching the numbers, the results were clear: **six out of seven** sweeteners were linked to faster cognitive decline. Only tagatose showed no connection.

## The 62% gap

Even after adjusting for age, blood pressure, diabetes, and other health factors, people in the highest-consuming group experienced a stark difference. Their memory and thinking skills declined at a rate **62% faster** than those in the lowest group.

That difference translated to roughly **1.6 extra years of brain aging** over the eight-year study period — meaning the brains of high sweetener consumers were functioning as if they were nearly two years older than their actual age.

Even people in the middle group weren't immune. They showed a **35% faster decline**, equivalent to about 1.3 additional years of cognitive aging.

## Younger adults may be at higher risk

When the researchers broke the results down by age, they found something surprising: adults **under 60** showed stronger links between sweetener consumption and cognitive decline. Among people over 60, no significant connection appeared.

The study also found a stronger association in people with **diabetes** — a group that often turns to artificial sweeteners as a sugar substitute.

## Why it matters

Artificial sweeteners were designed to give people the pleasure of sweetness without the calories or blood sugar spikes that sugar brings. For decades, government health agencies have approved them as safe.

But this is one of the largest studies yet to look at how these sweeteners affect the brain over time. As the lead researcher, Dr. Claudia Kimie Suemoto, noted: "Low- and no-calorie sweeteners are often seen as a healthy alternative to sugar; however, our findings suggest certain sweeteners may have negative effects on brain health over time."

## Important fine prints

The researchers were quick to point out what their study did **not** prove. It was an **observational study**, which means it can show a link but cannot prove that sweeteners literally *cause* brain aging. Other habits that often go along with heavy diet drink use — like less physical activity or different eating patterns — could also play a role.

Additionally, the study only looked at seven sweeteners. Many more exist on grocery store shelves, and each may behave differently in the body.

## What you can do

For now, health experts say the best approach is moderation. If you drink several cans of diet soda a day or rely heavily on sugar-free snacks, you might consider swapping some of those for naturally flavored sparkling water or whole fruits, which bring their own natural sweetness.

The study's authors recommend that people — especially those with diabetes or anyone under 60 who consumes a lot of artificial sweeteners — talk with their doctors about their habits.

What this really comes down to is a reminder that there are rarely "free" choices in nutrition, and the newest research suggests our brains may pay attention to what we drink even when it contains no sugar at all.

## Fun Fact

The sweetest substance known to science is **neotame** — a lab-made sweetener that is **7,000 to 13,000 times sweeter** than table sugar. A single drop could sweeten 1,000 gallons of tea! (It isn't one of the six flagged in this study, but it illustrates just how intensely sweet modern alternatives can be.)

## for-grownups

The University of São Paulo study (Suemoto et al., *Neurology*, 2025; PMID: 39961384; DOI: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000214023) examined 12,772 Brazilian adults tracked for ~8 years. Participants were grouped by daily sweetener intake (20 mg/d lowest, 191 mg/d highest — ~1 diet soda). Six of seven tracked sweeteners (aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol) were associated with cognitive decline; tagatose was the exception. Highest consumers showed 62% faster decline (~1.6 extra years of aging); middle group showed 35% faster (~1.3 extra years). Associations were stronger in adults under 60 and in people with diabetes. Cognitive assessments measured verbal fluency, working memory, word recall, and processing speed. The observational design precludes causal inference.
