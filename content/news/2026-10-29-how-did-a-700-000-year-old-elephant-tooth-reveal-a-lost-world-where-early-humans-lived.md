# How Did a 700,000-Year-Old Elephant Tooth Reveal a Lost World Where Early Humans Lived?

## 1. One Tooth, Three Hundred Thousand Years of Secrets

Deep in the sediment of northern Israel, buried beside ancient stone tools, a single elephant tooth waited silently for nearly seven hundred thousand years. When scientists finally uncovered it at a place called **Gesher Benot Ya'aqov** — whose name means "Daughters of Jacob Bridge" — they realized they were holding a time machine, not in the shape of a device, but in the form of a fossilized molar.

The tooth was enormous, belonging to a **straight-tusked elephant** (*Palaeoloxodon antiquus*), one of the largest land mammals that ever lived. Rather than simply cataloging the animal, the research team decided to read the tooth like a history book — each groove, each chemical trace, each tiny wear pattern a sentence telling the story of an ancient world.

## 2. Forests, Rivers, and Wetlands — Reconstructed from a Molar

Using CT scans, 3D reconstruction, microscopic wear analysis, isotope geochemistry, and pollen extracted from nearby sediments, the scientists painted a picture of a landscape that would have looked nothing like the dry hills of today.

Where the **Hula Valley** sits now — a quiet stretch of wetlands just south of the Sea of Galilee — there was once a thriving mosaic of **woodlands, open grasslands, marshy wetlands, and slow-flowing rivers**. Pollen grains trapped in the soil revealed willows, sedges, reeds, and tamarisk — plants that love water. Carbon isotopes from the tooth enamel told the team that the local climate held a **dependable supply of fresh water**, creating a reliable haven in an otherwise changing world.

This lush corridor sat inside the **Levantine Corridor** — the natural land bridge that has connected Africa and Eurasia for millions of years. For early humans making their first great journeys out of Africa, a green, well-watered passage would have been more than helpful — it could have been essential.

## 3. Stone Tools Beside Giant Bones

The elephant tooth was not alone. Beside it, archaeologists found **basalt handaxes and cleavers** — the distinctive teardrop-shaped tools of the **Acheulian tradition**, one of humanity's earliest and most widespread tool-making cultures. These implements, some of the oldest known to human ancestors, required skill to craft: striking one stone against another to shape a sharp, lasting edge.

For scientists, the pairing is deeply significant. It shows that **early humans and giant elephants shared this same landscape** — not as hunters in a dramatic chase, but as neighbors in a fertile valley. The elephants browsed the riverbanks and woodlands; the humans shaped their tools and moved through the same scenery, likely returning to the same reliable water source generation after generation.

## 4. A Migration Highway Written in Stone and Enamel

Why does this matter today? Because the Hula Valley sits at the crossroads of one of Earth's most important migration routes. Over hundreds of thousands of years, waves of climate change repeatedly reshaped the region — sometimes green and welcoming, sometimes dry and harsh. Finding evidence of a stable, productive oasis during a cool phase helps researchers understand how our ancestors survived and spread.

As **Professor Gonen Sharon** of Tel-Hai University, who led the excavation, explains: *"The wetlands, rivers, and woodlands of the Hula Valley helped sustain life along one of the world's earliest migration corridors, offering a rare opportunity to understand the ecological conditions that supported both animals and human populations nearly 700,000 years ago."*

The study — published in **Quaternary Science Reviews** (Volume 392, November 2026) — was led by **Hannah Farrell**, a PhD candidate at the University of Haifa, working with colleagues including specialists in isotope analysis, pollen studies, and archaeozoology from universities across Israel.

By reading a single tooth, they have reminded us that the landscapes we pass through today have been gathering places for both mighty beasts and curious humans for longer than imagination easily stretches — and that water, more than almost anything else, has been the quiet thread weaving life's great journey across the continents.

## Fun Fact

The straight-tusked elephant — *Palaeoloxodon antiquus* — was so massive that its closest living relative is the much smaller African forest elephant. Its tusks could stretch **over two meters (six and a half feet) long**, and the animal itself may have weighed as much as a small car.
