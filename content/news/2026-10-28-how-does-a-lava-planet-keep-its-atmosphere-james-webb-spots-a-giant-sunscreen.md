# How does a lava planet keep its atmosphere? James Webb spots a giant sunscreen

## A world that should not still have air

Imagine a planet so close to its star that the daylight side is a permanent, roiling ocean of molten rock — surface temperatures soaring past 2,400° Celsius (about 4,400° Fahrenheit). By all the rules of planetary science, that kind of radiation should have blown a thin atmosphere away into space billions of years ago. Yet the James Webb Space Telescope has found a rocky world named **TOI-561 b**, located roughly 280 light-years away in the constellation Sculptor, that still appears to be holding on to a substantial atmosphere — and reflecting about three-quarters of the starlight that reaches it.

## Why the planet is special

TOI-561 b is a **super-Earth**, about 1.4 times Earth's radius. Its orbit is so close to its star that it completes a full lap in just over **10 hours** — a blink of an eye in planetary terms. The same side always faces its star, meaning one hemisphere lives in permanent, blistering daylight while the other sits in perpetual night.

That makes the planet a natural laboratory for testing how worlds behave under extreme heat. Researchers led by Dr. Anjali Piette from the University of Birmingham and Professor Lisa Dang from the University of Waterloo used JWST's infrared eyes to watch TOI-561 b through an entire orbit, measuring how its brightness and heat changed as it rotated from dayside to nightside. What they saw was a surprise: the planet's dayside was far cooler than expected for such a dark, rocky world.

## Clouds that act like sunscreen

The explanation may be high-altitude clouds full of tiny **silicate crystals** — the same minerals that form glass and sand. These clouds contain compounds like silicon dioxide (SiO₂) and magnesium silicate (MgSiO₃), and they do something remarkable: they bounce incoming starlight back into space before it can heat the surface.

"It's like the planet is wearing a giant sunscreen," explains the research team. When they compared the telescope measurements with climate computer models, they found that a thick blanket of silicate clouds could explain the planet's unusually high reflectivity — about **75 percent of incoming light is reflected**, comparable to Venus, the most reflective planet in our own solar system.

## Where does the air keep coming from?

So how has this atmosphere survived for billions of years under such intense bombardment? The leading hypothesis is that the planet's surface is a global ocean of molten rock, and it acts like a planet-sized volcano — constantly burping new gases into the air. Each time the star's radiation strips a little atmosphere away, the lava ocean below releases more, keeping the protective cloud layer fresh.

"This raises major questions," says Dr. Piette. "Whether the planet started with an unusually large supply of gases or if the ocean of molten rock covering its surface acts as a planet-wide volcano that is constantly feeding its atmosphere, we don't yet know."

The next step is for JWST to observe TOI-561 b at longer wavelengths, which could reveal the exact mix of gases in that atmosphere and confirm whether it really resembles a superheated Venus or an even more extreme version of Jupiter's volcanic moon Io.

## Fun Fact

TOI-561 is also home to two other confirmed planets — TOI-561 c and TOI-561 d. All three orbit in a gravitational resonance, like a cosmic dance that has stayed in rhythm for billions of years.
