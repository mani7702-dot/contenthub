Every year, power plants, mines and factories leave behind enormous piles of waste — mountains of coal ash, red mud and leftover rock that most people would call rubbish. But look closer, and those piles are hiding something valuable.

Locked inside all that material are **rare earth elements**, the special metals used in phones, electric cars, wind turbines and medical scanners. One estimate suggests that American coal-ash landfills alone hold around 11 million tons of them, worth about $8.4 billion — nearly eight times the country's known raw reserves.

The trouble is getting them out. Traditional mining and refining use huge amounts of energy and harsh chemicals. So a research team led by Worcester Polytechnic Institute (WPI) is asking a very different question: what if we copied nature instead?

## The world's tiniest builders

Some living things are masters of building with minerals. **Diatoms** — microscopic algae with glassy shells — and **sea sponges** pull dissolved silicon out of water and assemble delicate silica structures, all at ordinary temperatures and pressures.

The team wants to learn those tricks. The five-year project has received a $3.3 million award from the National Science Foundation's Growing Convergence Research program. It is led by Mingjiang Tao, an associate professor at WPI, with colleagues Carrick Eggleston and Yan Wang, and researchers from four other universities.

## Waste into two useful things

Rather than just grabbing the rare earths, the plan is to use as much of each waste stream as possible. The silica itself could be turned into valuable products, while the rare earths and other critical minerals are separated out.

"Recovering critical minerals is only part of the opportunity," Tao explained. "We want to develop a process that uses as much of each waste stream as possible... That whole-material approach could fundamentally change how industries manage waste and obtain essential resources."

To speed up the search for the right molecules, the team will use advanced computer modelling to predict how they interact with silicon-rich waste.

## Fun fact

Rare earth elements aren't actually rare — they're just spread very thinly through the ground. That is why old industrial waste, where they have already been gathered together, can be such a promising place to find them.

## Why it matters

If the idea works, it could shrink waste piles, cut pollution from mining, and give countries a steadier supply of the metals that modern life depends on — all by borrowing a recipe that tiny sea creatures have been perfecting for hundreds of millions of years.
