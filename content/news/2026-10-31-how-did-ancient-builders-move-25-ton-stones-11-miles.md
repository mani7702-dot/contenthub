Standing in a flat stretch of countryside near Boroughbridge in northern England are three enormous stones. The tallest rises about seven metres — taller than most of the stones at Stonehenge. Together they form Britain's tallest surviving prehistoric stone row, and for centuries nobody knew where they came from.

The stones are known as the Devil's Arrows. A story once claimed the devil himself threw them at nearby towns and missed — a tale that hints at just how out of place these giant rocks seem in a quiet lowland landscape.

Now a team led by Curtin University, working with the University of York, has finally traced their origin. The answer is surprising.

## A much longer journey than expected

The researchers matched the stones to **Brimham Rocks**, a rugged outcrop in North Yorkshire about 18 kilometres (11 miles) away. Each stone weighs more than 25 tonnes — roughly as much as five large elephants. Hauling them that distance would have taken serious planning and teamwork.

What makes the finding so striking is that a closer source existed. Plumpton Rocks, just a few kilometres away, had long been assumed to be the quarry. But the new evidence rules that out.

"They were deliberately chosen from a challenging landscape 18 kilometres away," said Dr Anthony Clarke, the study's lead author.

The stones were raised sometime around 2000 BC, during the Late Neolithic or Early Bronze Age — long before wheels, cranes or metal tools were common in the region.

## How do you trace an ancient rock?

The team used a clever, gentle technique. Rather than slicing samples from the monument, they pressed adhesive tape against the stones to lift tiny mineral grains. Those grains carry age "fingerprints" that can be compared with the geology of possible source sites. The match pointed clearly to Brimham Rocks, and also ruled out glaciers, which might otherwise have carried the stones naturally.

## Why carry them so far?

If the builders could have grabbed nearby rocks, why trek 11 miles instead? The researchers think the answer lies in meaning, not convenience. The landscape around Brimham Rocks may have held cultural or spiritual importance, making the stones worth the effort.

"It wasn't just about convenience — the location the stones came from likely held meaning for them," Dr Clarke said.

## Fun fact

The Devil's Arrows were never part of a full circle. They form a straight row, and there may once have been a fourth and fifth stone. One of the surviving stones is thought to have toppled centuries ago and now rests on its side.
