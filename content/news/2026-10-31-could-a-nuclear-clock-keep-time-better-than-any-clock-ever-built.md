# Could a Nuclear Clock Keep Time Better Than Any Clock Ever Built?

What if a clock could tick so steadily that it would not lose a single second in 30 million years? That is not a thought experiment anymore — a team in Vienna has just built one, and it may change how we measure the world around us.

## 1. A Clock That Pulls Its Own Strings

For most of history, humans kept time with swinging pendulums, then with the tiny vibrations of quartz crystals. Today's best clocks watch electrons inside atoms leap between energy levels, using that flicker as a steady beat.

The new device, built at **TU Wien** in Austria, works a little differently. Instead of electrons, it uses the **nucleus** of an atom of **thorium-229**. In a special crystal made in Vienna, a laser shines onto the thorium, nudging its nuclei between two energy states. The nuclei absorb the laser light only when the frequency is exactly right — and when the light drifts, the absorption fades. The clock senses that change and gently adjusts the laser back on course, all by itself.

"What you really want is a self-stabilizing nuclear clock," explains **Prof. Thorsten Schumm**. "You have a laser and you have thorium. The laser changes the energy state of the thorium nuclei, and the thorium nuclei are used to stabilize the frequency of the laser."

## 2. Why Nuclei Make Better Timekeepers

The trick to a great clock is finding a beat that nothing can disturb. Atomic nuclei are more than **10,000 times smaller** than whole atoms, which makes them far less sensitive to temperature changes, magnetic fields, and other outside noise.

Over a full day of testing, the Vienna clock reached a precision of roughly **10 to the power of minus 15** — about **one second of error in 30 million years**. That is not yet better than the world's finest optical atomic clocks, but the team is thrilled: "For a first prototype it is a fantastic result," Schumm says.

## 3. Two Clocks, One Big Week

The milestone arrived twice over. In the very same week, a separate team in Beijing reported its own thorium-229 nuclear clock, and researchers around the world described the moment as a turning point in precision measurement. The work was reported in journals including **Nature**, and covered by outlets from Reuters to The New York Times.

## 4. What Better Timekeeping Unlocks

Why chase such extreme accuracy? Because better clocks sharpen our view of everything. Ultra-precise timekeeping helps satellites navigate, lets scientists watch the tiniest shifts in Earth's gravity, and could one day reveal whether the constants of nature are truly constant. Next up: stronger lasers and purer thorium crystals, which the team hopes will push nuclear clocks past today's atomic clocks entirely.

## Fun Fact

The second itself is defined by atomic clocks — specifically, the vibration of a **caesium-133** atom, which ticks 9,192,631,770 times per second. If nuclear clocks become the new standard, that number could change for the first time in more than half a century.
