# How Did a 240-Million-Year-Old Prehistoric 'Pig' Stay Hidden in a Museum for 60 Years?

Sometimes the most exciting discoveries aren't found with a hammer and chisel on a dusty hillside. This one was found in a museum drawer.

Scientists have identified a brand-new species of prehistoric animal that lived in what is now Tanzania around **240 million years ago**, during the Triassic period. The catch? Its fossils were dug up back in 1963 and had been sitting in the Natural History Museum in London, only partly studied, ever since.

## A Huge Plant-Eater With a Turtle's Beak

The new animal is called ***Dinodontosaurus isiyavamanda***. Its second name honours the land of the Wamanda people, who live in the region of Tanzania where the fossils were found.

Dinodontosaurus belonged to a group called **dicynodonts** — sturdy, plant-eating relatives of mammals that roamed the Earth long before dinosaurs took over. Picture something the size of a large pig, with a beak like a turtle's for cropping plants and tusks jutting from its jaw. These barrel-bodied herbivores were the gentle giants of their time, munching their way across ancient floodplains.

## Why a Museum Find Matters So Much

What makes this fossil truly special is *where* its rocks come from. The same Tanzanian deposits have also produced fossils thought to be among the **oldest potential dinosaurs ever found**.

Here's the puzzle. Scientists had assumed those Tanzanian rocks matched rocks in South Africa, and dated them accordingly. But the new Dinodontosaurus changes the picture. Until now, this genus was known **only from South America** — so its appearance in Tanzania links the two continents' fossil beds and suggests they are the same age. Fresh dating from South America hints that those rock layers are as much as **10 million years younger** than the South African ones.

The result: Tanzania's "oldest dinosaur" candidates probably aren't quite that old after all, nudging scientists to rethink exactly when and where dinosaurs first appeared.

## Thirty Years in the Making

For palaeontologist Nigel Larkin, the story is personal. He studied the skeleton for his master's thesis back in 1994 and suspected it was a new species — but it took nearly 30 years to write it up. "What's 30 years?" he joked. "It's the blink of an eye compared to the age of this prehistoric specimen."

**Fun fact:** A large portion of the Tanzanian Dinodontosaurus material still hasn't been studied — so more surprises may be waiting in the same museum cabinets.

The work, led by the University of Bristol, was published in the *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology*.
