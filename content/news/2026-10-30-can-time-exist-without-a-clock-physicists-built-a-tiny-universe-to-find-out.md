We treat time as the most reliable thing in life. Seconds tick, birthdays arrive, and no one has to wind the universe each morning. But what if time isn't a built-in feature of reality at all — what if it simply *emerges* from the changes happening around us? A physicist at the University of Birmingham has now built a tiny laboratory universe to test that strange idea.

## A cosmos made of 24,000 atoms

Professor Giovanni Barontini cooled about 24,000 atoms to just a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero — colder than deep space. At that chill, atoms blur into one shared quantum state and behave in ways ordinary matter never does. Two laser beams formed a thin barrier, splitting the cloud into a "bright" region and an unseen "dark" region.

Trapped inside, the bright region began to swell and shrink over and over. That rhythm echoes two grand stories from cosmology: the Big Bang, when the universe expanded, and a possible "Big Crunch," when expansion could one day reverse. Barontini wasn't recreating those events — he was building a simple, controllable stand-in where he could ask a deep question.

## Time from change, not clocks

Here's the puzzle. Some theories that try to merge quantum mechanics with gravity describe the universe as a single, unchanging quantum state — no external clock ticking away. So how can anything happen in order?

Barontini's answer was to measure progress using the atoms themselves. As particles shuffled between the bright and dark sides, their spread — a measure called entropy — shifted. That shift became a kind of internal clock. When the spread changed, this "entropic time" moved forward. When nothing changed, the measure stood still.

Most striking of all, this inner time behaved like the real thing. It ran in one direction, giving a clear arrow between past and future, and it could even speed up or slow down.

## What it means

The findings, published in *Physical Review Research*, show a quantum system can keep a meaningful sense of time without any outside clock. This doesn't prove time is an illusion — it gives scientists a hands-on tool to probe one of physics' oldest questions. Follow-up experiments might even model black holes or the universe's earliest moments.

## Fun fact

Colder than empty space itself — the coldest spot in the known universe is often a lab, not a corner of deep space. Some atom clouds dip to a few billionths of a degree above absolute zero, which is far frostier than the cosmic background at a balmy 2.7 degrees above absolute zero.
