# How Did Japan Launch a Spacecraft to Bring Home the First-Ever Samples From a Martian Moon?

In a stellar step forward for planetary science, Japan successfully launched a spacecraft on October 19th, 2026, headed for the mysterious moons of Mars. The mission aims to collect soil samples and bring them back to Earth for the first time in history — offering our best yet chance to solve a puzzle that has fascinated astronomers for centuries: how did Mars's tiny, potato-shaped moons come to exist?

## 1. A Five-Year Journey to the Red Planet's Moons

The Martian Moons eXploration mission, known as MMX, lifted off from Tanegashima Space Center aboard Japan's new H3 rocket at 3:41 p.m. EDT (4:41 a.m. local time the next day). Led by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), this roughly 5-ton, $578 million international effort brings together partners from NASA, France's CNES, Germany's DLR, and the European Space Agency in a shared quest to unlock secrets of our solar system. The spacecraft is expected to arrive at Mars by late summer 2027, where it will study the planet's two small moons — Phobos (about 22 km or 14 miles across) and the even smaller Deimos.

## 2. Landing on Phobos to Solve an Ancient Mystery

The big moment comes when MMX attempts something never done before: landing on Phobos to gather samples of its surface. Scientists are eager to understand how these curious moons formed. Were they created from debris after a giant impact on Mars, or are they asteroids captured long ago? The returned samples could finally settle the debate. Even better, material from Phobos may contain grains blasted off Mars itself over billions of years, offering fresh clues about the Red Planet's history and perhaps even the conditions that helped life take root on Earth.

To help navigate Phobos's challenging terrain and extremely low gravity, the mission carries a small French-German rover named Idefix (after the loyal white dog in the Asterix comics). This microwave-sized, 4-wheeled scout will be released first to explore ahead, bouncing carefully across the surface at speeds measured in millimeters per second to identify a safe spot for the main spacecraft to touch down. The lander is designed to spend only about two hours on the surface, using both Japanese and NASA-contributed tools to drill and vacuum up at least 10 grams of material before returning to orbit.

After additional observations — including flybys of Deimos — the MMX probe is scheduled to depart the Martian system in 2030 and deliver its sample capsule to the Australian desert in 2031. If successful, it will mark the first time humanity has brought home material from any moon other than our own. The mission will also gather data that could support future human journeys to Mars — JAXA has noted that Phobos may one day serve as a natural outpost or staging point.

## Fun Fact

Phobos is slowly spiraling inward toward Mars — about 13 feet (4 meters) closer each century — and is doomed to either crash into the planet or break apart into a ring of debris in roughly 30 to 50 million years. So MMX is racing against deep time to study a moon that won't exist forever.
