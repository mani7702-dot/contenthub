When NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft arrived at asteroid Bennu in 2018, it expected a fairly smooth parking lot where it could grab a few handfuls of dust. Instead it found a nightmare landscape of enormous boulders, some as big as office buildings, scattered so densely that not a single safe landing spot could be found.

That alone was odd. But a second, stranger mystery soon appeared: Bennu warms up and cools down far too quickly. Beach sand does that — it heats in sunlight and chills in shadow. Solid rock does not. An asteroid that looks like a pile of bricks should behave like rock. Yet Bennu acted like sand.

## The crack inside every rock

The answer finally came from the samples themselves. After three years grinding Bennu’s boulders back on Earth, scientists led by Andrew Ryan of the University of Arizona measured the thermal behavior of the rock up close. They discovered that nearly every boulder is riddled with a hidden network of microscopic cracks.

Heat travels slowly through solid stone, but zip through narrow gaps in the air. Those tiny cracks — thinner than a human hair but running like spiderwebs through the whole rock — let warmth race along exposed surfaces, exactly the way it does in loose sand. That is why the asteroid still cools fast even though its surface is made of big rocks.

## A sample the size of a sugar cube

The amount of Bennu material returned is remarkably small — only about 10 grams (a bit less than a sugar cube) — yet it carried enough information to solve a puzzle that had baffled astronomers since 2018. Each grain was like a tiny letter from the asteroid’s past, telling the story of how it was assembled long before Earth even existed.

## Why it matters for the whole solar system

The crack-filled boulders do more than explain a curious heat trick. They also nudge Bennu on a slow walk around the Sun. As sunlight streams off the warm rock and cracks, it gives the asteroid a tiny push — a drift called the Yarkovsky effect. Knowing the cracks means scientists can now predict Bennu’s path decades into the future, and that same rule likely applies to every boulder-strewn asteroid in the sky.

**Try it yourself:** shine a flashlight on two spoons taped together — one on a solid block, one loose. The loose one (air gaps everywhere) heats and cools faster, just like Bennu’s cracked boulders.

## Sources

NASA’s OSIRIS-REx team first noticed the boulder problem in 2019, but it took the returned samples — studied by over 175 scientists worldwide — to show why the whole asteroid stays thermally "leaky." The peer-reviewed findings appear in Nature Communications.
