# How Did 128,000-Year-Old Ice in Tibet Reveal What Earth Looked Like in the Last Ice Age?

## 1. A Frozen Secret Hidden High in the Mountains

Way up on the Guliya Plateau in northwestern Tibet, at an elevation of about 6,200 meters (20,000 feet), lies an ice cap so old it may have been quietly holding Earth's climate secrets for more than 128,000 years. That's older than the entire time modern humans have existed as a species!

Scientists were surprised to discover that ice drilled from this spot during two separate expeditions — one in 1992 and another in 2015 — tells a remarkably consistent story of Earth's ancient weather. The deeper layers, they found, extend far beyond the Holocene, the warm geological epoch that began about 12,000 years ago after the last great ice age melted away.

## 2. How Do You Date Ice That Old?

Figuring out the age of ice that has stayed frozen through countless seasons is no simple task. You can't just count the yearly snow layers — they get compressed and squished over time. Instead, an international team of researchers turned to nature's own clocks: radioactive isotopes of beryllium and chlorine.

These naturally occurring "isotopes" decay at steady, predictable rates, leaving behind chemical signatures that act like timestamps. The team used these markers to detect evidence of a dramatic event called the Leschamp Geomagnetic Excursion — a time more than 41,000 years ago when Earth's magnetic field wobbled and shifted. Finding that signature locked inside the ice gave them a reliable anchor point to build a timeline.

They then compared their results with oxygen-isotope records preserved in nearby Tibetan cave formations, which confirmed the same long, deep history written in the ice.

## 3. Why This Matters for Understanding Our Planet

Ice cores are among the most valuable tools scientists have for reading Earth's climate diary. As snow piles up year after year and slowly compresses into ice, it traps tiny bubbles and chemical clues from the atmosphere at the time. By studying different layers, researchers can piece together how temperature, air makeup, and even volcanic activity have changed over thousands of years.

Earlier studies had suggested the Guliya ice cap might only contain ice from the relatively recent Holocene. But this new evidence shows that at least some of the ice survived the transition out of the last ice age — proving that large mountain glaciers can persist through major climate shifts.

## 4. A Record Worth Protecting

What makes Guliya special is its location. While polar ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica cover vast areas, mountain ice cores from this extreme altitude are far rarer — especially ones that reach back so far. The high elevation and sheltered terrain of the plateau likely created the perfect conditions for ancient ice to remain intact.

Going forward, researchers hope to study additional samples from the same cores. With a more accurate timeline now in hand, they can re-examine clues they may have previously misread. As lead researcher Lonnie Thompson of Ohio State University put it, the results tell "a very consistent story" — and now it's time for our understanding to catch up.

The findings were published in the journal *Science Advances*, opening a new chapter in how we study long-term climate change and the resilience of Earth's great ice archives.

## Fun fact

The Guliya ice cap sits so high that its surface is actually a frozen desert — it receives very little snowfall compared to polar ice sheets, yet the ice has managed to survive for over 100,000 years, making it one of the oldest continuous climate records from a mountain setting on Earth.
