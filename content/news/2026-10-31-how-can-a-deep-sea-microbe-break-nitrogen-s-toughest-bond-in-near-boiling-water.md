Every breath you take is mostly nitrogen — about 78 percent of the air around us is made of nitrogen gas. Yet for most living things, that gas is like food sealed inside an unbreakable jar. The two nitrogen atoms in each molecule are locked together by one of the strongest bonds found in nature.

Plants and animals simply cannot open that jar. Certain microbes can. The trick is called **nitrogen fixation**, and it turns nitrogen gas into ammonia, a form that living things can actually build with.

## The microbe that works in a furnace

One champion of this skill is *Methanocaldococcus infernus*, a single-celled microbe that lives in deep-sea volcanic vents, where the water can be hotter than its own boiling point. Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for Marine Microbiology in Bremen wanted to know how it manages such heavy chemistry in such a punishing place — so they "tamed" the microbe in the lab and nudged it to keep fixing nitrogen above 90 °C.

The star of the show is an enzyme called **nitrogenase**. It holds one of the most complicated metal-based helpers known in biology, and it comes in three main flavors — built around molybdenum, vanadium, or iron alone.

## A protein that shrugs off the heat

When the team pulled the enzyme out and tested it, the protein only began to come apart at 90 °C, and some of it was still intact at 98 °C.

"This proves that this enzyme is designed to function under conditions in which most proteins would rapidly decay, like egg white cooked in hot water," said first author Nevena Maslać.

Curiously, it does almost nothing at room temperature. Only when things get hot does it start turning nitrogen into ammonia.

## A family reunion, seen for the first time

Using a powerful synchrotron in Grenoble, France, the researchers mapped the enzyme at near-atomic detail. It turned out to be the simplest nitrogenase ever studied — yet it carries structural features from all three flavors at once, hinting that it may resemble the ancient ancestor they all came from.

The team also caught it in a fleeting reaction step that had only ever been seen in two of the three types. Finding it here suggests all nitrogenases may share a common way of cracking nitrogen open.

## Fun fact

These deep-sea nitrogen-fixers do more than feed themselves: they are responsible for producing about half of the methane in Earth's atmosphere.
