# How Did a Hidden Ocean Chain Reaction Drive Earth's Great Ice Ages?

## 1. The mystery of two hemispheres

Long before humans ever walked the Earth, vast sheets of ice crept across the Northern Hemisphere, sometimes kilometers thick. We call these periods "ice ages." But a puzzling question has long stumped scientists: how could ice sheets growing in the north — thousands of miles away from Antarctica — help trigger changes in the far southern oceans that pulled carbon dioxide out of the air?

It's like asking how a pebble dropped in a pond in New York could create ripples that reach the opposite side of the world and change the tide. For decades, researchers knew that the Southern Ocean around Antarctica plays a crucial role in storing atmospheric CO2, but the missing piece was understanding how northern ice alone could set that process in motion.

## 2. The cooling ripple effect

Scientists at the University of St Andrews, led by Dr. Madison Shankle, used powerful computer simulations of Earth's ocean currents to test a bold idea. Instead of cooling the whole planet at once, they cooled only the far northern regions — mimicking what happens when massive ice sheets spread across the north.

The results were remarkable. The cold water in the North Atlantic began sinking and flowing southward, like an underwater river. As this chilly current traveled thousands of miles toward Antarctica, it gradually changed how the entire Southern Ocean circulated. The modeling showed that this northern-only cooling produced 74% as much CO2 drawdown as a simulation in which the entire planet was cooled — proving that temperature changes in one part of the world can have a surprisingly powerful influence on the other side of the planet.

## 3. Why CO2 matters for ice ages

The greenhouse gas carbon dioxide acts like an invisible blanket around Earth, trapping heat. When the atmosphere holds less CO2, the planet cools. During past ice ages, a significant portion of that CO2 was absorbed by the oceans, particularly the frigid waters around Antarctica.

The Southern Ocean's ability to pull CO2 from the air depends heavily on how its waters mix and circulate. When cold northern water arrives and alters those currents, it can create conditions that let the ocean "breathe" differently — drawing down more carbon from the atmosphere and locking it away in the deep sea for centuries.

This discovery helps solve one of climate science's biggest puzzles: how local changes at one pole can trigger global cooling. It shows that Earth's climate system is connected in ways we are still learning to understand.

## 4. Lessons for today

Understanding how past ice ages developed gives scientists a natural laboratory for studying Earth's carbon cycle. These ancient periods reveal how carbon dioxide moves between the atmosphere and oceans, and how shifts in that balance can tip the planet's temperature.

As today's researchers work to predict how Earth's oceans and other natural systems will respond to the extra carbon dioxide now entering the atmosphere, knowing more about these ancient feedback loops becomes increasingly valuable.

**Fun fact:** The last great ice age peaked about 20,000 years ago, when so much water was locked up in ice that sea levels were 120 meters (394 feet) lower than they are today. If all that ice melted now, the coastline maps of every continent would look completely different!

**Try it yourself:** Fill a clear bowl with warm water and sprinkle a pinch of glitter on top. Gently drizzle cold water around the edges — the denser cold water sinks and pushes the glitter into swirling patterns that can travel across the whole bowl. That's a tiny model of how cold currents can carry changes from one ocean region to another.
