# Why Did the First Diplodocus Outside North America Turn Up in Spain?

Ask anyone to picture a dinosaur and they will probably describe *Diplodocus*: a long-necked giant with a whip-like tail and a surprisingly small head. For almost 150 years, every confirmed *Diplodocus* skeleton on Earth had come from one place — the Morrison Formation of the western United States. Now that has changed.

Palaeontologists working in eastern Spain have identified the first *Diplodocus* ever found outside North America. The find doesn't just add a new dot to the map. It hints that these enormous animals were roaming between continents millions of years before anyone thought they could.

## Fourteen Bones in a Spanish Hillside

The fossils were unearthed at the La Tejería site near El Castellar, in the province of Teruel. A team from the Fundación Conjunto Paleontológico de Teruel-Dinópolis recovered fourteen exceptionally well-preserved tail vertebrae, along with several chevron bones that once supported the underside of the tail.

Those bones turned out to be a near-perfect match for *Diplodocus*. By comparing anatomy and running detailed evolutionary analyses, the researchers placed the specimen firmly inside the familiar genus — as a close relative of *Diplodocus hallorum*. The animal would have measured about 25 metres from snout to tail tip, roughly the length of two city buses parked end to end.

Lead author Sergio Sánchez Fenollosa described the work as pieces of a puzzle slowly falling into place. As the team studied the vertebrae, telltale *Diplodocus* features began to emerge.

## A Bridge Across the Ocean

The Spanish *Diplodocus* lived around 150 million years ago, near the end of the Jurassic Period. Its presence in Europe is powerful evidence that dinosaurs could move between North America and Europe at the time.

So how did they get there? The researchers think the answer lies in the sea itself. As the young Atlantic Ocean rose and fell, temporary land bridges sometimes appeared between the continents. During those windows, dinosaurs could wander from one landmass to the other — a slow, generations-long crossing rather than a single dramatic journey.

## A Whole World of Giants

Spain's Jurassic was already famous for giant sauropods, including the distinctive *Turiasaurus*. Adding *Diplodocus* to the list shows just how varied and crowded those ancient landscapes were.

The fossils now have a second life on public display in the dinosaur hall of the Museo Aragonés de Paleontología at Dinópolis in Teruel. Museum casts of *Diplodocus* have charmed visitors for a century; this time, the real thing is staying put.

**Fun fact:** *Diplodocus* was first discovered in the United States in 1878. Its museum casts became so popular in the early 1900s that the dinosaur turned into a worldwide symbol of palaeontology — long before most people had ever seen a fossil in person.
