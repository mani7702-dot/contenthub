Some people refuse to disappear from history. Shadrack Byfield is one of them — and thanks to a lucky find in an American library, we now know far more about him than we did a year ago.

## A soldier who kept his own record

Byfield was born in 1789 near Bradford-on-Avon, a textile town in Wiltshire, England. At eighteen he joined the local militia, and by 1809 he had sailed to Canada with the 41st Regiment of Foot. When the United States declared war in June 1812, he was stationed at Fort George on the Niagara River and fought through several of the conflict's major battles.

In 1814 he was badly wounded and lost his left arm below the elbow. As it was carried away, he asked for it back, built a small wooden box with his own hands, and gave the arm a proper burial — a quiet act of dignity that says a great deal about the man.

## The invention that brought him back to work

Returning to England, Byfield could no longer work as a weaver, so he sketched an idea for a device that would let him operate a loom one-handed. A local blacksmith built it from his design. He kept it — and kept going. He married, raised a family, and later wrote a memoir called *The History and Conversion of a British Soldier*, printed in London in 1851.

For years, historians assumed Byfield had died around 1850. His experiences had appeared in books, documentaries and even a 1985 children's novel, but the man behind them stayed shadowy.

## Found 3,700 miles from home

That changed when Dr Eamonn O'Keeffe, then a historian at the University of Cambridge, tracked down what appears to be the only surviving copy of Byfield's book — at the Western Reserve Historical Society library in Cleveland, Ohio, some 3,700 miles from where it was printed. He reported the find in the *Journal of British Studies*.

The memoir, plus newspaper records and other archives, revealed a man who lived more than two decades longer than anyone knew, and who pushed back hard for the pension and independence he felt he had earned.

**Fun fact:** The soldier who once buried his own arm has a permanent display in his honour at the Fort Erie Visitor Centre in Ontario, Canada.
