# How Did a Lost Shark Become a City's Mascot and Win a Rescue Operation?

## 1 — How did a shark end up in a concrete canal?

In mid-September 2026, residents of Busan, South Korea, got a surprise: a large shark had been spotted swimming in a narrow concrete canal right next to the world's seventh-busiest port. This wasn't a small shark — it was a grown adult, and it was far from home.

Marine biologist Kim Hyun-woo, one of South Korea's leading shark experts, was called in to help. He had never been asked to rescue a shark before. "I thought I'd seen everything in my career," he said, "but never a shark stranded in a city canal."

The canal was built for boats, not wildlife. It was shallow, lined with concrete walls, and full of human activity. No wild shark should have been there. Officials believe the shark may have followed schools of fish into the canal, drawn by warming ocean temperatures. The South Korean oceans ministry noted that roughly **four times** as many large sharks were recorded off the country's east coast in the first half of 2026 compared to the same period the previous year — and climate change may be bringing more sharks closer to shore.

## 2 — How did the whole city fall in love with a shark?

When the shark first appeared, local coastguards sent out an emergency text alert warning residents to "exercise caution for their safety." The message had the opposite of its intended effect — within hours, thousands of people flooded to the canal to catch a glimpse of their unexpected visitor.

Someone gave her the nickname **Bukangi** — Korean for "harbor entrance" — and the city embraced her completely. Local businesses started selling Bukangi merchandise: plush toys, T-shirts, even pins. A city council banner invited people to sign a petition "on behalf of Bukangi" to support funding for a new baseball stadium. The local government began livestreaming the shark 24/7, and schoolchildren were given front-row viewing spots to "meet" her.

"People just fell in love," said one visitor from Busan. "It's really rare these days for everyone to come together like this."

But love alone couldn't keep the shark safe. As autumn temperatures dropped, the shallow canal became increasingly dangerous. Kim explained: "This canal is far from something we can call a habitat. A wild animal cannot stay in a space made strictly for humans."

## 3 — How did 100+ people organize a shark rescue?

Saving a shark is no small task. The oceans ministry took over the rescue from the fisheries institute, mobilizing roughly **40 personnel**, including divers from Busan's aquarium, a veterinarian on standby, and rescue boats standing by in case the shark became aggressive.

Their plan: install two layers of netting across the width of the canal to gently guide Bukangi out into open water. But the clever shark kept finding ways to slip through or swim back. A first rescue attempt using electrical deterrent devices failed after five hours — she was too large and fast.

Then came the protests. As the rescue efforts intensified, some citizens began demanding that Bukangi be left alone. Signs appeared: "Leave Bukangi alone!" and "Protect Bukangi's right to self-determination." One tearful fan shouted, "I love you, Bukangi!" as guards tried to clear the area.

Finally, on Thursday, divers managed to install the double-net system correctly. For the first time, the shark was guided into deeper water. She circled back once more — brushing against the outer net — but then was last seen swimming steadily toward the open sea. No sightings have been reported in the days since, which experts say is a positive sign that she is adjusting well.

## Fun Fact

Dusky sharks are incredible long-distance travelers! Satellite tracking has shown that individual dusky sharks migrate **thousands of kilometers** each year — some swim from South Africa to Australia and back again, following warm water currents and schools of fish. They can grow up to 4.5 meters (15 feet) long and are built for speed, with powerful tails that can propel them through the ocean like torpedoes.

## For parents and teachers

This story demonstrates how wildlife can unexpectedly appear in urban environments due to climate change and warming ocean temperatures. The dusky shark (Carcharhinus obscurus) is classified as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List — its population has declined by over 95% in some regions due to overfishing for fins soup, liver oil, and meat. Conservation efforts like marine protected areas and sustainable fishing quotas are critical for the species' survival. The rescue of Bukangi also highlights the importance of collaboration between scientists, government agencies, and local communities in wildlife emergencies. - *time: 11:52 IST*
