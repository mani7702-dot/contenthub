# Did Magnetism Help Build the Solar System 4.6 Billion Years Ago?

About 4.6 billion years ago, our corner of space was nothing but an enormous cloud of gas and dust. Over a few million years that cloud collapsed and flattened into a spinning disk, and from it the Sun and planets were born. Ask most astronomers what did the heavy lifting, and you will hear one word: gravity. A new study suggests that answer may be incomplete.

## 1. A Cloud That Learned to Spin

Picture a giant, slowly turning pancake of dust. As it swirls, electrically charged particles move with it — and moving charges are exactly what creates a magnetic field. Researchers at MIT wondered whether the infant solar system whipped up one of its own. If it did, that field should have left a fingerprint on the very first solid specks to form.

## 2. Clues Inside an Icy Meteorite

Their evidence came from **DOM 08006**, a meteorite found in Antarctica's Dominion Range in 2008. It is one of the most unaltered space rocks ever recovered — "somehow, DOM has experienced less alteration than any other meteorite," said Benjamin Weiss of MIT. Inside it sit microscopic grains called **calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions**, or CAIs, which condensed during the solar system's first 200,000 years. They are the oldest known material from that era.

## 3. A Field Stronger Than Earth's

By teasing out the faint magnetism still trapped in those grains, the team estimated the early solar system's field at roughly **150 to 600 microteslas — three to twelve times stronger than Earth's magnetic field today**. That is strong enough to have helped drag gas inward toward the newborn Sun, working alongside gravity rather than replacing it.

## 4. Rewriting the First Chapter

The finding, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, pushes the story of magnetism back to a time before the Sun had fully formed. "If you want to fully understand how the Sun and planets formed, you should include magnetic fields in the ingredients that make them," said lead author Cauê Borlina, who began the work at MIT and is now at Purdue University.

**Fun fact:** CAIs are so tiny that several different kinds can be found inside a single one-millimetre piece of the meteorite — a reminder that the oldest chapters of our story were written in grains smaller than a pinhead.
