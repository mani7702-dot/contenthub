# How Did One Small Hub Transform the Space Station from a Cramped Shed into a Real Home?

## The day the station grew up

On **October 23, 2007**, the space shuttle **Discovery** roared off Launch Pad 39A at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida. Its cargo was not a person, not a telescope — it was a **connecting hub** no bigger than a small house, but one that would change everything for the International Space Station.

That hub was **Harmony** — also known as **Node 2**. At just **14 feet (4.3 meters) wide** and **24 feet (7.3 meters) long** (about the size of a minibus), Harmony is the kind of room that *connects* everything else. It links the American, European, and Japanese laboratory modules together, carries electrical power and data through the station, and even has **sleeping cabins for four astronauts**.

## From three-bedroom to five-bedroom

Before Harmony arrived, the International Space Station was only about the size of a **three-bedroom house**. Once astronauts installed the module, the station grew into something more like a **five-bedroom house** — with new room to live, work, and dream.

During the two-week STS-120 mission, the crew first left Harmony temporarily attached to the **Unity node**. Then, using the station's big robotic arm, they moved Harmony to its permanent spot next to the **Destiny laboratory**. From there, it became the connection point for Europe's **Columbus** lab and Japan's massive **Kibo** lab — each docking on either side, just like adding rooms to a growing home.

## Why Harmony matters today

Harmony has **five "common berthing mechanisms"** — that is a fancy name for strong, airtight connection ports. Every one of them lets a new module, cargo ship, or visiting spacecraft attach to the station. Today, Harmony is still the central hub where international partners meet — a symbol of how countries can work together in space.

Inside Harmony are **eight payload racks**: four hold the station's avionics (computers and wiring), and four others hold either supplies or **crew sleeping quarters**. When astronauts need a nap 250 miles above Earth, they float into one of Harmony's private sleeping bags, tethered so they do not drift away!

## Try this at home

**Build a space station hub!** Take a paper towel roll or a short piece of cardboard tube to be your "Harmony module." Use smaller tubes or straws taped to it as the "modules" (American lab, European lab, Japanese lab). Can you connect them all? How many "berthing ports" does your hub have?

## Fun Fact

The name **Harmony** was chosen by the public! NASA held a naming contest, and out of thousands of entries, "Harmony" won because it perfectly describes how the module brings different nations and their space labs together in peaceful cooperation.
