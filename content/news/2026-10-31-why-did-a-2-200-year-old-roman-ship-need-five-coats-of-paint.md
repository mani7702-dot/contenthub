When a wooden ship sinks, the sea usually makes short work of it. But a Roman trading vessel that went down off the coast of what is now Croatia about 2,200 years ago kept something remarkable: the sticky, waterproof coating that once protected its hull.

Now a team of researchers from France and Croatia has finally decoded that coating, and the recipe is surprisingly clever.

## A coat that kept the sea out

The wreck, called Ilovik–Paržine 1, was discovered in 2016. Since then, scientists have studied both the ship and its cargo, but the protective layers painted onto the wood had never been examined in detail.

For as long as people have sailed, boats have needed shielding from salt water, wood-boring worms and the tiny organisms that munch on timber. Until the mid-1900s, though, researchers paid little attention to the non-wood materials used in ancient shipbuilding—especially the waterproofing.

The new study, published in the journal *Frontiers in Materials*, put those forgotten layers under the microscope.

## Pine tar, beeswax and a Greek trick

Analysing ten coating samples, the team found that almost every one was dominated by pine tar, also known as pitch—a thick, sticky substance made by heating coniferous resin.

One sample was different. It blended pine tar with beeswax, creating a mixture Greek shipbuilders called *zopissa*. This version is more flexible and easier to spread once warmed, making it handy for patching awkward curves of a hull.

To identify the ingredients, the researchers used mass spectrometry, a technique that can spot and measure unknown substances hiding inside complex organic mixtures.

## Pollen turned the coating into a time capsule

Because pine tar is sticky, it traps pollen drifting through the air. By studying those microscopic grains, the scientists could work out where the tar was made—and where it was reapplied during repairs.

The pollen painted a picture of a whole Mediterranean world. It pointed to coastal areas and inland valleys, holly oak and pine forests, and shrubland where olive and hazel trees grow. Traces of alder and ash hinted at riversides, while fir and beech pointed toward the mountains of the northeastern Adriatic.

## Repaired again and again

Put together, the evidence suggests the ship received four to five separate coatings over its working life. The stern and middle sections shared the same material, while three distinct layers sat at the bow.

Earlier studies of the ship's ballast tied its construction to Brundisium—modern Brindisi, on Italy's southeastern coast. The pollen backs that up, but also shows that later coats were added farther north, along the coast where the ship finally came to rest.

**Fun fact:** Roman shipwrights weren't just stopping leaks—they were also, in a way, collecting a diary. Every repair brushed onto the hull carried a tiny sample of the plants growing nearby, letting scientists today trace a voyage that ended two thousand years ago.
