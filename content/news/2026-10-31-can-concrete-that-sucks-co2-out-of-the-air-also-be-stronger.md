# Can Concrete That Sucks CO2 Out of the Air Also Be Stronger?

Concrete is everywhere — in the sidewalks we walk on, the bridges we cross, and the buildings that shape our skyline. But producing all that concrete comes at a cost: the cement that binds it releases a steady stream of carbon dioxide, making it one of the world's leading industrial sources of the greenhouse gas that warms our planet.

What if one of our most common building materials could be given a gentler job? A team of engineers in India tested a simple, optimistic idea: redesign a standard concrete mix so it both gets stronger *and* quietly pulls CO₂ out of the surrounding air. The experiment, reported in the journal *Carbon Research*, shows that the right natural ingredients can do both at once.

## Two Natural Ingredients, One Stronger Mix

The researchers, led by Srinivasan Revathi at Mepco Schlenk Engineering College, started with M35-grade concrete — a common, mid-strength blend used in roads and everyday structures. They swapped in two materials that both exist in abundance on a planet that grows bamboo: **zeolite**, a porous volcanic mineral, and **bamboo biochar**, a charcoal-like substance made by heating bamboo under controlled conditions.

Like a puzzle with many pieces, they tested several combinations. Some replaced up to 50% of the sand-like fine aggregate with zeolite, and added bamboo biochar in small doses. Each mix was measured for how well it held weight (compressive strength), how it resisted pulling apart (split tensile strength), how much water it soaked up, and how tough it was against knocks.

One combination stood out. A blend of **50% zeolite and 1% bamboo biochar** — nicknamed ZB5 — delivered the best overall package. Its compressive strength reached about 38.5 MPa, roughly **7½% higher** than ordinary concrete. Its ability to grip and hold also improved by about **15%**, meaning it resists cracking and splitting too.

## A Building Block That Cleans the Air

Strength was only half the goal. In laboratory tests, ZB5 didn't just bear weight — it actively absorbed carbon dioxide. When exposed to controlled conditions, the mix captured roughly **1.2 grams of CO₂ per day**, with the gas soaking in about 1.5 cm deep over a week. That's still early, careful work: real-world weather, seasons, and decades would tell the fuller story.

The researchers think the credit belongs to the two ingredients working as a team. Zeolite's spongy network of microscopic pores acts like a welcome mat for gas molecules, while the bamboo biochar is packed with carbon that meshes well with the surrounding cement. The result is a denser, sturdier structure that also happens to be a better home for trapped carbon.

## From Lab Bench to City Street?

This is a proof of concept, not a green light for mass construction just yet. Before city sidewalks or highway barriers could be poured from this exact mix, the team wants to test more kinds of biochar, different concrete grades, and how the material holds up over time against real sun, rain, and traffic. They also want to study how best to grow and process the bamboo so the whole idea is genuinely friendly to the planet.

Still, the direction feels encouraging. Concrete has long been a quiet emitter; here's a way it might one day be part of the solution, holding up the world while also catching a little of the carbon that humans have added to the sky.

**Fun fact:** Bamboo is one of the fastest-growing plants on Earth — some species can grow several inches in a single day — which makes its charred remains a surprisingly patient partner for century-old construction projects.
