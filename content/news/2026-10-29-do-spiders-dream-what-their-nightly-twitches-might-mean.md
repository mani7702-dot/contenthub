# Do Spiders Dream? What Their Nightly Twitches Might Mean

**1. A tiny spider with a surprisingly complex sleep**

The Australian jumping spider *Portia fimbriata* is only about the size of a one-euro coin when fully grown, but its hunting skills make it anything but ordinary. Unlike most spiders that wait passively in webs, Portia spiders actively stalk other spiders — even pretending to be trapped insects to lure their prey close. This kind of clever, adaptive thinking suggests something unusual is happening inside their tiny brains.

Now, researchers from the University of Bonn in Germany have added another remarkable item to the Portia resume: these eight-legged hunters appear to experience genuine, regulated sleep with two clearly distinguishable stages. And one of those stages looks strikingly like the REM sleep that humans cycle through every night.

**2. Two stages of spider sleep — and twitches like our own**

To study the spiders' sleeping habits, the Bonn team placed individual *Portia fimbriata* spiders in small plastic dishes filled with tiny obstacles — think miniature jungle gyms. Using infrared cameras, they recorded the animals continuously over several days and nights, capturing every twitch and pause.

What they found was surprising. The spiders exhibited two clearly different sleep phases:

- **A quiet phase** — the spider stays very still, movements minimal.
- **A REM-like phase** — the spider's eyes dart around, its muscles twitch, and its breathing changes.

In humans, that second phase is called REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, the stage most closely linked to vivid dreaming and memory processing. Whether spiders are actually dreaming in any conscious sense remains an open question — but the parallels are intriguing.

**3. How scientists confirmed these spiders were truly sleeping**

Proving that a creature is genuinely asleep — and not just sitting motionless — requires more than watching and waiting. The Bonn researchers used several careful tests:

First, they played sound stimuli at regular intervals and measured how quickly the spiders responded. A truly sleeping spider reacts much more slowly than one that is simply resting. The spiders showed exactly the response patterns expected for real sleep.

Second, the team deliberately disrupted the spiders' normal rest cycles and watched what happened afterward. When spiders were kept awake during their usual sleep window, they compensated by sleeping longer and more intensely later — a phenomenon called "sleep rebound." This is the same effect humans experience after pulling an all-nighter.

One limitation: because spiders have very different nervous systems from mammals, the researchers could not measure brain waves directly using electrodes. Instead, they relied on careful behavioral documentation over thousands of hours of video.

**4. What this tells us about the evolution of sleep**

Sleep is one of the most widespread biological phenomena on Earth. Nearly every animal — from fruit flies to whales — spends a significant portion of its life asleep. Yet scientists long lacked a clear picture of how sleep evolved across species with very different brains.

Finding sleep patterns similar to our own in a creature whose nervous system evolved separately from vertebrates suggests that certain aspects of sleep may be deeply ancient — perhaps as old as the last common ancestor of all animals. Studying spider sleep could even shed light on fundamental questions about human sleep disorders.

The researchers are now expanding their work to other spider species and comparing lab-raised spiders with those living in natural environments, hoping to understand how surroundings shape sleep quality. They're also investigating whether different sleep stages actually help spiders learn and remember their complex hunting strategies — the spider equivalent of studying for a test while they tuck their eight legs in at night.

## Fun Fact

A group of spiders is called a **cluster** (or sometimes a **colony** if they're social). And while most spiders have eight eyes, not all of them work equally well — jumping spiders have some of the clearest vision in the spider world, with four pairs of eyes that can rotate independently toscan 360° without moving their heads!
