For seven years, volunteers near Hertford in eastern England worked on a quiet rescue mission: coax a rare chalk river, the River Ash, back into the winding shape it once had. They replanted foliage, rebuilt gravel beds, and let the water wander again.

Then, this past late summer, a small, shimmering visitor showed up.

## What did they find?

The **scarce blue-tailed damselfly** (*Ischnura pumilio*) was recorded at newly created wetlands along a 4.3-mile (7-kilometre) stretch of the river on the Easneye Estate. Damselflies look like delicate dragonflies that fold their wings along their backs when they rest.

The species is a fussy one. It likes shallow, temporary pools and marshy seepages — exactly the kind of soggy edges that vanish when a river is straightened and fenced in. So finding it at two monitoring spots just a short distance apart was a genuine surprise.

## Why does it matter?

The discovery arrived at the same moment the British Dragonfly Society published its latest review of Britain's dragonflies and damselflies. In that 2026 assessment, the scarce blue-tailed damselfly was moved from **Near Threatened to Endangered**.

The review found that **eight species are now classed as endangered — twice as many as in the previous check back in 2008.**

"Natural rivers, connected floodplains and healthy wetlands support biodiversity, providing places for wildlife to feed and breed," said Sarah Perry, rivers recovery manager at the Herts and Middlesex Wildlife Trust. She added that the wetlands also help water move through the landscape, building resilience against both flooding and drought.

## A river that came back to life

The River Ash is one of only about **260 chalk rivers in the world** — clear, cool waterways fed by underground springs. Since its restoration, monitors have logged a 177% and 30% rise in river invertebrates such as mayflies and stoneflies at two sites, and brown trout are already spawning on the new gravel beds.

For anyone who worries that damaged places stay damaged, the lesson is gentle but real: sometimes all a rare creature needs is for us to put the curves back.

**Fun fact:** Dragonflies and damselflies have been flying for more than 300 million years — longer than humans have existed — making them some of the earliest flying invertebrates on Earth.
