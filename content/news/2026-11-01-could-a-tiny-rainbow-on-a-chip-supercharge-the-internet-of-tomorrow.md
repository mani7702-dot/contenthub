# Could a Tiny 'Rainbow on a Chip' Supercharge the Internet of Tomorrow?

Think about how much you send and receive every day — messages, photos, videos, video calls. The world is hungry for data, and scientists are looking for clever new ways to carry even more of it, faster.

Their latest idea is surprisingly beautiful. It involves making an invisible rainbow, on a chip no bigger than a grain of rice.

## 1. A rainbow you cannot see

When sunlight passes through raindrops, it fans out into bands of colour. Every colour is simply light vibrating at a slightly different speed, or *frequency*.

Physicists at Loughborough University, working with an international team, have built a tiny device that does something similar with light you cannot see at all. It is called a **microcomb**. Instead of a smooth, blended rainbow, it produces a neat row of sharply separated light frequencies — like the rungs of a ladder, each one precisely placed.

Those separate colours matter, because a clever antenna can convert them into **millimetre waves**: high-frequency signals that can carry far more data than the radio waves used today. Each frequency could act like its own lane on a busy motorway, letting information travel several ways at once.

## 2. The trick is a simple loop

For years, microcombs could manage only a single precise millimetre-wave frequency at a time. Making many at once demanded a device that stayed extraordinarily steady.

The Loughborough team found an elegant fix. Instead of shining laser light into a tiny light-trapping ring on the chip, they connected that chip to a much longer loop of optical fibre. Light races around both, feeding itself back through the chip again and again until the rainbow of frequencies builds up on its own — and stays put.

"It's remarkably robust," said Dr. Luke Peters. "We've even had people jumping up and down next to the system and the microcomb remains stable."

## 3. Why it matters

The researchers, writing in the journal *Nature Communications*, showed they could strengthen or weaken individual frequencies too, tuning the rainbow for whatever a job needs.

Faster, higher-capacity **6G** networks are the headline dream, but the same tool could sharpen radar, chemical sensors, and the instruments astronomers use to measure the universe. The technology is still years from everyday use — but one of its biggest hurdles just got smaller.

## Fun fact

A microcomb's "rainbow" is real light at carefully spaced frequencies — but every one of them sits outside what your eyes can see. It is a rainbow you could never spot, even in the brightest sunshine.
