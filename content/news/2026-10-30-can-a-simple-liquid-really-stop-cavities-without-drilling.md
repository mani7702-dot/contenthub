# Can a Simple Liquid Really Stop Cavities Without Drilling?

## 1. The Liquid That Could Replace the Dentist's Drill

Imagine a cavity treatment that takes just a few seconds, needs no needles, no drilling, and no anxiety. A major Phase III clinical trial published in *JAMA Pediatrics* suggests that such a treatment already exists — in the form of a simple, inexpensive liquid called silver diamine fluoride, or SDF.

The study, led by researchers at the University of Michigan, enrolled 830 children under the age of six who were recruited from dental clinics, pediatric medical practices, and Head Start programs across Michigan, New York, and Iowa. The results are compelling: applied with a tiny sponge-tipped applicator directly onto a cavity, SDF successfully stopped tooth decay in more than half of the treated baby teeth when given every six months.

Unlike a traditional filling — which requires removing damaged tooth material and placing a restorative material — SDF works differently. It does not rebuild lost structure. Instead, it helps prevent existing decay from progressing, gently halting the clock on a cavity that would otherwise worsen over time.

## 2. How Does a Clear Liquid Fight Tooth Decay?

SDF is a clear, odorless solution that combines two powerful ingredients. Silver acts as a natural antibacterial agent, targeting the harmful microbes that cause cavities. Fluoride, long celebrated for strengthening tooth enamel, helps reinforce the mineral structure of the tooth and builds resistance against further damage.

In the trial, the researchers administered a 38% silver diamine fluoride solution. Children were assessed at baseline, then at three, six, and eight months. At the six-month mark, 54% of treated cavities had stopped progressing, compared to just 22.5% in the placebo group. By eight months — after a second application — the treatment group still outperformed the placebo, with 50.2% of cavities arrested versus 17.4% in the control group.

"This is a very effective and safe treatment — even in children as young as 1," said Dr. Margherita Fontana, professor of dentistry at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry and the study's lead investigator.

## 3. Why This Could Matter for Millions of Families

Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease of childhood, affecting more than 40% of American children. Left untreated, cavities can cause severe pain, infections, difficulty eating and sleeping, and missed school days. Every year, thousands of young children end up in emergency rooms because of dental problems that emergency physicians simply cannot address.

For many families — especially those facing barriers to regular dental care — SDF offers a practical alternative. The treatment can be applied quickly in medical or school-based settings, without the need for specialized dental equipment or the coordination required for traditional procedures. This could be especially valuable for older adults, people with developmental or physical disabilities, and anyone who struggles with dental anxiety.

Dentists have used SDF successfully for decades in many countries, and in the United States it has been applied off-label since 2014, when the FDA approved it as a medical device for treating tooth sensitivity. However, it has lacked the large-scale U.S. clinical evidence needed for full FDA approval as a cavity treatment. This new Phase III trial — which began in 2018 and continued through the major disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic — was designed to fill that gap.

## 4. The One Tradeoff Parents Should Know

There is one important consideration: SDF permanently darkens the treated area of the tooth. The discoloration is purely cosmetic — the treated portion turns a faint gray or brown, which is most noticeable on front teeth. For many families, avoiding pain, drilling, or more invasive procedures may outweigh this drawback, but it is worth discussing with a dental professional before treatment.

The researchers noted that in some cases, cavities treated with SDF may still eventually require a conventional filling or other procedure, particularly if the decay is very deep or near the tooth's nerve. However, for many children and families, SDF represents a gentle, effective bridge — a way to pause a cavity's progression and buy time until a more comprehensive treatment becomes feasible.

**Fun fact:** Silver diamine fluoride was first developed in Japan in the 1970s. For decades, millions of children there and across parts of Asia and Latin America have received the treatment. The liquid tastes slightly sweet and is often applied with a flavor that children find pleasant — a far cry from the stress and discomfort of a traditional dental visit.
