What if a computer wasn't built from silicon and wire, but grown in a dish?

That's the idea behind a new experiment at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where researchers have engineered bacteria to act like **transistors** — the tiny switches inside every electronic gadget that turn a signal on or off.

## 1. Bacteria that behave like switches

In an ordinary chip, a transistor decides whether electricity flows through a circuit. In this living version, engineered bacteria do the same job by controlling the movement of small signalling molecules, which carry information from one group of cells to the next.

The team built two kinds of bacterial "transistor" and three more strains that act as relays between them. Those five strains together form a flexible kit — like a box of building blocks that can be arranged into many different circuit designs.

Using them, the researchers built working logic gates and even a circuit that can add two numbers together, printed onto a gel-like growth material inside a Petri dish.

## 2. The puzzle they solved

Until now, living circuits mostly squeezed all their parts into a single cell — and cells run out of room. "We've built some initial computer architecture components that are commonly used, but any operation can be built with these five strains," said Hamid Doosthosseini, the study's lead author.

The trick was borrowing a bacterium called *Pantoea agglomerans*, which naturally grows on plant surfaces, and splitting the work across separate colonies instead of one crowded cell.

## 3. Where a living circuit might help

The researchers aren't trying to replace your laptop. Their hope is subtly different: to place living circuits on plant leaves or roots, where they could sense a drought or a pest attack and help the plant respond.

Biology, after all, is already brilliant at detecting tiny changes in the world around it.

## Fun fact

These circuits are slow by computer standards — signals crawl along over minutes or hours. But they can repair themselves, grow, and even make copies of themselves, things no silicon chip has ever managed.
