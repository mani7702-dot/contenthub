# What Is the Quantum 'Energy Ladder' That Took 40 Years to Measure?

Sometimes very different things start behaving in exactly the same way. Water boils, a magnet loses its pull, a material suddenly stops carrying electricity — and yet, at the moment each change happens, the mathematics behind them looks remarkably alike. Physicists call this **universality**: the messy, tiny details wash out, and only a few essential features survive.

Now, in a study published in the journal *Nature*, a team at Caltech has taken one of the clearest looks yet at that shared behaviour — by building the phenomenon from scratch and measuring it directly.

## A Tipping Point Near Absolute Zero

Ordinary changes, like ice melting into water, are driven by temperature. But the change the Caltech team studied is different: it arises purely from quantum effects, at temperatures close to absolute zero (about −273°C). At that "tipping point," the system can absorb energy only in specific, separated amounts — like the rungs of a ladder.

Theory has told physicists what those rungs should look like for roughly 40 years: they should appear at precise ratios. But nobody had ever measured them in an experiment, until now.

## Atoms Held by Tweezers of Light

To do it, the researchers used a setup borrowed from quantum computing research. They trapped a line of **strontium atoms** using tightly focused lasers known as *optical tweezers*. Then more lasers nudged the atoms into highly excited states called Rydberg states, where each atom strongly feels its neighbours. The whole chain began to act like one single object rather than a crowd of separate particles.

The team then gently "shook" the chain by wobbling the lasers at chosen frequencies and watched how strongly the atoms responded. Sweeping through those frequencies revealed the rungs of the ladder. Working with chains of up to 35 atoms, the measured rungs landed exactly where the theory said they should land.

## Why It Matters

"The energy levels predicted by these theories are important because they encode profound information about the theories themselves," explains Caltech's Jason Alicea. His colleague Manuel Endres adds that the real excitement is what comes next: pointing the technique at systems where nobody knows the answer in advance — including ones that ordinary computers cannot reach.

**Fun fact:** The same "optical tweezer" arrays the team used were originally built to develop quantum computers. Here, they were pointed at a fundamental physics question instead — proving a good tool often finds a second life somewhere unexpected.
