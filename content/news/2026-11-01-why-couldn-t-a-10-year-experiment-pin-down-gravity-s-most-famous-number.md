# Why Couldn't a 10-Year Experiment Pin Down Gravity's Most Famous Number?

Gravity is the force that keeps your feet on the floor, holds the Moon in orbit, and gathers whole galaxies together. You would think we know exactly how strong it is by now. We don't — and a ten-year experiment has just reminded scientists why.

## The number they call "big G"

Every fundamental force has a strength that scientists can write down. The strength of gravity has its own famous symbol: **big G**, the gravitational constant.

Every other basic constant of nature is pinned down to six or more digits. Big G is the odd one out — the least precisely known of them all. Researchers have chased its exact value for more than 225 years, ever since Isaac Newton described how gravity works.

Part of the problem is that gravity is astonishingly weak. A magnet the size of a pinhead can lift a paper clip — beating the gravitational pull of the entire Earth on that same clip. So experiments have to measure the gentle tug between small metal weights instead of whole planets.

## An answer sealed in an envelope

At the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in Maryland, physicist Stephan Schlamminger led a team that spent a decade repeating a delicate French measurement of big G.

To avoid fooling themselves, they tried something clever. A colleague secretly altered part of the data, so no one could guess the result in advance. The correction needed to unlock the true answer was locked inside a **sealed envelope** — a bit like a scientist's version of a sealed vote.

Schlamminger almost opened it in 2022, then waited two more years to be sure every tiny effect had been checked. When he finally revealed the hidden number in 2024, the answer wasn't quite what the earlier experiment had found.

## Why scientists aren't worried — yet

The two results differ by only about **0.02%**. In everyday life that is nothing: it wouldn't budge the reading on a bathroom scale.

But for physics, that gap is a genuine puzzle. The team's careful new value, reported in the journal *Metrologia*, simply doesn't match the older one, and the difference is bigger than the usual measurement wobble.

The most likely explanation is some subtle flaw no one has spotted. The more exciting — and far less likely — idea is that we might be missing something about gravity itself.

## A 1798 trick that still works

Both experiments share a design from 1798, when Henry Cavendish twisted a hanging wire to feel gravity's faint pull. Today's version uses eight polished metal cylinders and a ribbon about as thin as a human hair.

**Fun fact:** Because gravity is so weak, a small refrigerator magnet can out-pull the entire Earth on a paper clip — which is exactly why measuring big G is one of the hardest jobs in all of science.
