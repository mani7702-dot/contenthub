# Why Do Quarks Leave Ripples in the Universe's Very First Soup?

Imagine dropping a stone into a pond and watching rings spread across the surface. Now imagine something far stranger: a single tiny particle, travelling at nearly the speed of light, leaving ripples behind it in the very first soup the universe ever made.

That is exactly what scientists have just seen — not in a pond, but inside the world's largest atom smasher.

## 1. The Universe's First Drink

In its earliest moments, the universe was almost unimaginably hot — trillions of degrees. At those temperatures, matter could not form atoms, or even protons. Instead, it was a furious mix of **quarks** and **gluons**, the building blocks that normally hide inside protons and neutrons.

This state is called **quark-gluon plasma**, and it is thought to have existed for only a few millionths of a second before the universe cooled and those particles locked together into the matter we know today.

Because it vanished so long ago, nobody expected to see it again. But at **CERN's Large Hadron Collider** in Switzerland, physicists can recreate slivers of it by smashing heavy lead ions into each other at close to the speed of light.

## 2. Catching a Single Quark's Wake

The tricky part has always been the noise. A quark never travels alone — its partner flies off in the opposite direction and muddies the picture.

A team led by MIT physicist **Yen-Jie Lee** found a clever workaround. They searched for events where a quark shot out in one direction and a **Z boson** — a shy, neutral particle that barely touches the surrounding matter — flew the other way. The Z boson acts as a clean marker, so any ripples on the quark's side must come from the quark itself.

Sifting through about **13 billion collisions**, the researchers found roughly **2,000** Z-boson events. Again and again, they spotted splashes and swirls trailing behind the lone quark — like a duck paddling across a pond.

## 3. A Liquid, Not a Crowd

These wakes match a prediction made more than 20 years ago: that quark-gluon plasma should behave like a fluid, not like a cloud of separate particles bouncing around. Lee puts it simply: "The plasma is incredibly dense, such that it is able to slow down a quark, and produces splashes and swirls like a liquid. So quark-gluon plasma really is a primordial soup."

Studying how those wakes ripple and fade could reveal the plasma's deepest properties — and offer a rare snapshot of the universe's first microseconds.

## Fun Fact

Quark-gluon plasma is the **hottest liquid ever known** — more than 200,000 times hotter than the centre of the Sun — yet it flows with almost no friction at all, making it the nearest thing to a "perfect" liquid in the universe.
