To most of us, a vacuum means emptiness — a space with nothing in it at all.

Quantum physics disagrees. Even in the emptiest void, tiny particles are constantly flickering in and out of existence, filling space with a faint, restless hum. Scientists call these **quantum fluctuations**, and they are real enough to have been measured many times over.

Now, for the first time, a team of researchers has shown they can put that hidden hum to work — using "empty space" to make a superconductor perform better.

## What is a superconductor?

A superconductor is a material that carries electricity with zero resistance, meaning no energy is lost along the way. The catch is that most superconductors only work at extremely cold temperatures. Warm them up even slightly and the special behaviour vanishes.

The temperature at which a material flips into its superconducting state is called its **critical temperature**. Raise that number, even a little, and the material becomes more useful.

## The experiment

The team, led by Changgan Zeng and Guanghui Cheng at the University of Science and Technology of China, placed a thin flake of the superconductor **NbSe₂** inside a tiny engineered cavity — a kind of mirrored box that reshapes the electromagnetic environment around it. The work also involved researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and MIT, including Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek, and was published in the journal *Nature*.

Inside the cavity, the vacuum's fluctuations grew stronger. And so did the superconductor: its critical temperature rose by up to **5.4%**, while its ability to carry current and withstand magnetic fields also improved.

The researchers ran careful control tests to rule out simpler explanations like strain or wear. When they tuned the cavity to a particular frequency, the boost peaked — a strong sign that the vacuum itself was doing the work.

## Why it matters

It points to a strikingly gentle idea: perhaps we can influence quantum materials **without touching them or driving them with external energy**. As Wilczek put it, the background itself can become an actor, not just a stage.

The team hopes that better-designed cavities could one day control quantum states more powerfully — nudging superconductivity not with brute force, but with the quiet hum of nothing.

**Fun fact:** Superconductors can also levitate magnets, because they lock magnetic fields out of their interior. That's why a magnet can float, frozen in mid-air, above a cooled superconductor.
