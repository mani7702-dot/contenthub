Zoom in far enough and the world stops behaving itself. Particles can sit in a blend of possibilities — a bit of "here" and a bit of "there" at the same time. Scientists call this **superposition**, and it is the idea behind the famous thought experiment of Schrödinger's cat: a cat sealed in a box that is somehow both alive and dead until someone looks.

Oddly, nothing in the everyday world works that way. Your coffee mug is on the table or it is not. The strange, blurry behaviour of the quantum world seems to fade away as objects get bigger. Physicists call that fading **decoherence**, and for decades nobody has been quite sure what causes it.

## The gravity suspect

One leading idea pointed at gravity itself. Back in the 1960s, the Hungarian physicist Frigyes Károlyházy suggested that the fabric of space and time might never be perfectly smooth — that it would always carry tiny, unavoidable "jitters." Those jitters, he proposed, could gradually shake quantum superpositions apart, which would explain why big objects never stay in two states at once.

If the idea were true, the jitters should give charged particles a gentle random nudge, and nudged particles should glow with an extremely faint burst of radiation. That faint glow would be the fingerprint of gravity quietly ending quantum weirdness.

## A quiet room under a mountain

To hunt for such a whisper, researchers turned to the Gran Sasso National Laboratory in Italy — the world's largest underground science lab, buried beneath about 1.4 kilometres of solid rock. All that stone blocks cosmic rays and other background noise, making it one of the calmest places on Earth for spotting something rare.

Inside sat a detector built around a lump of ultra-pure germanium crystal about the size of a coffee mug, wrapped in extra layers of copper and lead. The team gathered data for 62 days, carefully subtracted the radiation they expected to see anyway, and compared what was left with the pattern the old theory predicted.

## The verdict: silence

Nothing showed up. No whisper, no glow, no telltale jitter. The result, published in the *New Journal of Physics*, does not prove that gravity has nothing to do with decoherence — but it does rule out one of the oldest and most natural versions of the idea.

"This absence of a signal is itself a major scientific result," said Catalina Curceanu, a research director at Italy's National Institute for Nuclear Physics, who led the work. By closing off one possibility, the experiment helps sharpen where the real answer might be hiding, including in ambitious attempts to unite gravity and quantum physics such as string theory.

**Fun fact:** because roughly 1.4 kilometres of rock sit overhead, the Gran Sasso lab is shielded from most of the cosmic radiation that constantly rains down at the surface — making it a favourite spot for experiments that need to hear the faintest possible signals.
