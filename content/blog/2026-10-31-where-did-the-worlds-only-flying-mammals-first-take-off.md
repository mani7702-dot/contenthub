# Where Did the World's Only Flying Mammals First Take Off?

Bats are the only mammals on Earth that can truly fly. They hover around us at dusk, fill our skies at night, and make up roughly **one in five of all mammal species** — yet for decades scientists could not agree where the very first bat took wing. Africa, Asia and North America were all proposed as possible birthplaces.

Now the largest study of its kind has a new answer, and it points to **Europe**.

## 1. The biggest bat family tree ever built

An international team of **137 researchers from 64 countries** combined genetic data from nearly every living bat family with evidence from ancient fossils. Working through a project called **Bat1K**, they analysed **103 bat genomes** — including 42 brand-new, chromosome-level assemblies — and added clues from **44 fossil bats**.

That mountain of evidence let them rebuild the bat family tree with far more confidence than ever before. The results suggest bats most likely originated in **Europe during the late Paleocene, around 65 to 60 million years ago**, before their descendants spread into Africa, the Americas, Asia and Australia.

## 2. Flight and "seeing" with sound arrived early

The study also tackled two of bats' most famous superpowers: **powered flight** and **echolocation** — the ability to "see" in the dark by listening to the echoes of their own calls.

By studying a fossil bat named *Vielasia*, which sits on the oldest branch of the family tree, the team found that echolocation had already appeared very close to the beginning of bat evolution. That suggests both flight and echolocation emerged **before** modern bat groups began to diversify — and likely helped bats become one of the planet's most successful mammal groups.

## 3. Why this was so hard to work out

Rebuilding the bat tree was tricky because early bat lineages swapped genes with one another, so different parts of the genome can tell conflicting stories. But the researchers found that part of the **X chromosome** preserved a particularly clear record, helping settle disputes that had lingered for decades.

"Bats constantly surprise us. They are one of evolution's greatest experiments," said Professor Sonja Vernes of the University of St Andrews.

## Fun Fact

Most bats navigate using echolocation, but some fruit bats rely mainly on their **large eyes and a strong sense of smell** instead. And a single small bat can catch **more than 1,000 mosquito-sized insects in just one hour** — a very welcome natural pest control.
