# How Did a Teacher's Daily Positivity Practice Spread Through an Elementary School?

## A tiny habit that grew into a school-wide movement

Every afternoon, as the final bell rings at **Rex Elementary School** in Haysville, Kansas, first-graders gather in a circle and share two simple promises: **"I am kind!"** and **"I can do hard things!"**

These aren't just cute sayings — they are **positive affirmations**, short sentences that help children end their day feeling confident and safe. The idea belongs to teacher **Tyler Richards**, who started the practice in his own classroom last spring. Something wonderful happened: the habit spread.

## How one classroom became a movement

Principal **Shane Paulus** noticed the change almost immediately. "It's a great way for them to feel loved, feel appreciated, know they're welcomed here, and to end their day on a good note," he told local reporters.

Teachers across every grade at Rex Elementary began adapting Tyler's idea in their own way — some added morning affirmations, others started "kindness walls" where students post notes about classmates who helped them. The children themselves took ownership quickly, inventing new positive statements and sharing them with friends.

## Why small habits matter

Psychologists call this a **positive psychology** approach — the scientific study of what makes life worth living. When children practice short, positive statements daily, their brains start building patterns of optimism and self-confidence that last far beyond the classroom.

The key is repetition, not grand gestures. Every morning, a child says "I can do hard things," and every afternoon, the same child leaves school remembering they are kind. These small anchors can carry a child through a tough math test, a new friendship, or a challenging day.

## Try this at home

Ask a grown-up to help you try a 30-second gratitude circle at dinner. Each person shares one small thing they appreciated that day — maybe the way sunlight came through the window, or how someone held the door. It takes less than a minute, but like Tyler's classroom, it might just become a habit that sticks.

## Fun Fact

The word "affirmation" comes from the Latin *affirmare*, meaning "to make firm" or "to strengthen." When you say something positive about yourself out loud, your brain treats it like a workout — each repetition makes the neural pathway a little stronger.

**for-grownups:** Positive psychology, founded by Martin Seligman in the 1990s, is the scientific study of well-being and positive relationships. Research shows brief daily affirmation practices can improve academic performance, reduce stress, and boost classroom engagement. These align with CASEL-endorsed social-emotional learning programs.
