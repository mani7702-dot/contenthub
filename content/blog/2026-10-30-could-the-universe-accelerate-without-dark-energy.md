## What makes the universe stretch faster and faster?

For nearly thirty years, scientists have carried an intriguing puzzle in their pockets. They noticed that the universe is not just expanding — it is *speeding up*. As if space itself were a balloon being blown up, and someone kept adding more air.

The accepted answer was **dark energy** — a mysterious, invisible force that pushes galaxies apart. But now, a team of mathematicians at the University of California, Davis, is asking a bold question: *What if dark energy is not needed at all?*

What if the answer was hiding in Einstein's own equations the whole time?

## A pencil balanced on its tip

Blake Temple, the study's corresponding author and a distinguished professor emeritus of mathematics at UC Davis, offered a simple comparison. Imagine a pencil standing perfectly upright on its tip.

"All the forces are in balance when a pencil is standing on end, so it is a 'solution of the equations,'" he said. "But it's unstable. Any breath of air and it falls away."

The standard model of the universe relies on what mathematicians call **Friedmann spacetimes** — neat, even mathematical descriptions where space expands while matter stays evenly spread out. Like that pencil on its tip, they look like valid solutions to Einstein's equations. But Temple and his colleagues — Christopher Alexander and Zeke Vogler — found something unsettling: those solutions are *unstable*.

## The wobble that changes everything

In physics, unstable solutions are considered "not physical." You will never observe them in nature, because the tiniest disturbance sends them tumbling.

The team's calculations, published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society A*, show that Friedmann spacetimes are unstable at both small and large scales around the Big Bang. That means the very foundation of the Lambda-cold dark matter model — the leading framework scientists use to describe how the universe evolved — may not hold up under mathematical scrutiny.

Instead of needing a separate, mysterious dark energy to push the universe apart, the acceleration could emerge **naturally** from Einstein's original equations of general relativity. The Einstein-Euler equations — which combine gravity with how fluids behave — already contain the seeds of cosmic acceleration.

## What about where we stand?

Einstein originally introduced a term — the cosmological constant — to keep the universe static. After Edwin Hubble discovered the universe was expanding in 1929, Einstein famously called it his "biggest blunder."

Then, in the 1990s, that same cosmological constant returned to prominence as the leading candidate for dark energy.

But Temple's work raises a deeper question — one that touches on a core assumption of modern cosmology called the **Copernican principle**. Named after Nicolaus Copernicus, who helped us understand that Earth is not the center of the solar system, this principle says our location in the universe is not special or privileged. 

The new mathematical framework suggests the universe could behave like the standard model near a central region, while showing different expansion behavior farther away. If that is true, it would mean the standard model and the alternative both require us to occupy a special spot — and by that logic, neither can claim superiority over the other.

### Fun fact: The pencil is not just a metaphor. In dynamical systems theory, balanced equilibria are *mathematically* unstable solutions. That is the same math that governs why a marble balanced on a bowl's rim will roll away with the slightest nudge — but a marble at the bottom of a valley stays put.
