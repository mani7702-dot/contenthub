# How Did Mayo Clinic Discover New Ways to Fight Zombie Cells That Drive Aging?

*Report filed at 07:03 IST from the Our World explainers desk.*

## The cells that won't retire

Imagine a worker who stops doing their job but never clocks out — they just hang around the office, leaving a mess behind. Inside our bodies, something similar happens. Scientists call these lingering troublemakers *senescent cells*, but they've earned a catchier nickname: "zombie cells." Once a cell becomes senescent, it stops dividing but refuses to die. Instead, it releases inflammatory signals that can nudge us toward age-related aches, stiffness, and forgetfulness.

A team at Mayo Clinic's Robert and Arlene Kogod Center on Aging has been peeling back the mysteries of these zombie cells. In seven new studies published across Nature journals, the researchers uncovered not just *why* these cells are harmful, but fresh ways to recognize and target them — including a compound that's already hanging out in your kitchen.

## Not all zombie cells are the same

For years, scientists treated all senescent cells as identical. But the Mayo Clinic team discovered that zombie cells are remarkably diverse. Their characteristics change depending on *where* in the body they settle and *what* triggered them to become senescent in the first place.

To make sense of this variety, the researchers created **SenMayo** — a gene-activity toolkit that identifies senescent cells more reliably across different tissues. They tested it on bone biopsies from older adults, and found that the gene signature grew weaker after senescent cells were removed in lab experiments. SenMayo has since become a widely used resource for researchers hunting age-related mysteries.

## The mitochondrial connection to constant inflammation

One of the most surprising discoveries links zombie cells to their own power plants gone rogue. Mitochondria — the tiny structures that normally generate a cell's energy — start malfunctioning in senescent cells. The team found that mitochondrial DNA and RNA leak out and trip the immune system's alarm bells, creating a chronic inflammation loop. A second pathway rewires mitochondrial metabolism to flip inflammatory genes on or off through epigenetic changes.

This matters because it opens a two-pronged strategy: instead of always trying to kill every zombie cell (which can be harsh on the body), doctors might one day calm the inflammation by targeting these mitochondrial signals — leaving the cells in place but silencing their noisy, harmful output.

## Tomatoes, pills, and a blood test

When tested in aging mice, experimental treatments produced encouraging results. Depending on the approach — whether senolytic medications, genetic techniques, or a natural compound found in tomatoes — researchers saw drops in inflammation measures plus improvements in physical frailty, brain inflammation, and memory tasks.

The team also identified **IL-23R** as a promising biomarker that could eventually be measured with a simple blood test, helping doctors track how well anti-aging therapies are working.

These findings are still early days, but they chart a clearer roadmap than ever for treatments that support healthy aging. The research feeds into Mayo Clinic's broader Precure initiative — catching harmful biological changes before they become serious disease.

## Fun fact

The word "senescent" comes from the Latin for "to be old." But zombie cells aren't exclusively a problem of old age — they can form at any time after an injury or infection, which is why the body needs cleanup systems to clear them out as we grow older.
