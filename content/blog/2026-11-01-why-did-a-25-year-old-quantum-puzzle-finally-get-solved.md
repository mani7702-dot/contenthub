# Why Did a 25-Year-Old Quantum Puzzle Finally Get Solved?

Imagine two coins that are magically linked. Flip one, and the other — no matter how far away — instantly lands the same way. That strange connection is real in the quantum world, where it goes by the name *entanglement*, and it is the engine behind some of the most exciting technologies being built today.

But there was a catch. For more than 25 years, scientists could reliably measure one famous flavour of this link, yet a second, equally important one kept slipping through their fingers. Now a team in Japan has finally caught it.

## 1. Two Ways to Be Tangled

When several particles of light — called photons — become entangled, they can lock together in different patterns. The best-known pattern is named GHZ, and back in the late 1990s scientists worked out how to *measure* it efficiently.

A second pattern, called the W state, is special because its link is more resilient: if one photon wanders off, the remaining ones stay connected. That toughness makes W states valuable. Yet no one had devised a practical way to measure them. The data needed for the usual method balloons wildly as you add photons, so a workable shortcut was essential.

## 2. Borrowing a Trick From Symmetry

Researchers at Kyoto University and Hiroshima University found their answer in a hidden rhythm called *cyclic shift symmetry*. In plain terms, the photons' arrangement can be rotated in a repeating cycle while the important underlying pattern stays the same.

Using that rhythm, the team designed a special optical circuit that performs a mathematical operation known as a quantum Fourier transform. Instead of gathering mountains of measurements, the device identifies the W state in a single clever step. "More than 25 years after the initial proposal concerning the entangled measurement for GHZ states, we have finally obtained the entangled measurement for the W state as well," said corresponding author Shigeki Takeuchi.

They built a real device and tested it with three photons, confirming it could tell different three-photon W states apart. Their method should, in principle, work for any number of photons — a path toward scaling up.

## 3. Why It Matters

One promising use is **quantum teleportation** — a technique that doesn't move matter but transfers a particle's delicate quantum *state* from one place to another using entanglement as the bridge. Being able to measure W states reliably could also boost quantum communication and a style of computing built entirely from measurements.

For now, the win is a fundamental one: a long-standing gap in our understanding has closed.

**Fun fact:** The word "teleportation" is slightly misleading — quantum teleportation never beams a physical object anywhere. It only sends the *information* describing a particle's state, and the original is always destroyed in the process, so nothing is ever duplicated.
