# How Did a Piece of Antarctica Get Named After a BBC Science Reporter?

Picture a sliver of water so narrow that two Antarctic islands almost touch through it. That channel — sitting between Rothschild Island and the icy tip of Alexander Island in western Antarctica — now has a very human name: the **Amos Channel**. It honors Jonathan Amos, the BBC science correspondent who spent 38 years bringing the mysteries of the poles, space, and climate science into homes around the world.

So how does a stretch of remote ocean end up carrying the name of a reporter who never even visited the continent?

## 1. Who Is Jonathan Amos?

Jonathan Amos joined the BBC in 1986 as a local radio reporter in Cambridge. He became one of the voices that helped launch the BBC News website in 1997, and he went on to lead the online science desk for many years. As a BBC science correspondent, he traveled the world covering everything from colossal icebergs and melting ice sheets to the drama of penguin colonies and the hunt for Ernest Shackleton's lost ship, the Endurance. He left the BBC in 2024 after a remarkable 38-year career.

## 2. What Is the Amos Channel?

The Amos Channel is a body of water in the Amundsen Sea, off the western coast of Antarctica. It sits between Rothschild Island — a thin, jagged sliver of land named after the famous Rothschild family — and the southern end of Alexander Island, which is one of the largest known islands in the Antarctic Ocean. The channel sits at roughly **71° South latitude**, far from any human settlement, reachable only by research ships that brave some of the roughest seas on Earth.

## 3. Why Was It Named After Him?

The UK Antarctic Place Names Committee — the body that decides what geographical features in the British Antarctic Territory get called what — recommended the name as a tribute to Amos's "exceptional and influential role in bringing Antarctic research to the forefront of public understanding." Athena Dinar of the British Antarctic Survey said, "Jon's passion for Antarctica and engaging journalism offers something for every reader, viewer, or listener." Fiona Fox of the Science Media Centre added that Amos "consistently highlighted the importance of Antarctic research, explaining not only what science is being done there, but why it matters for society, climate understanding and the global environment."

## 4. How Does a New Channel Appear?

Geographical features in Antarctica — even entire channels — are not always permanent fixtures on the map. The Amos Channel appeared relatively recently when a chunk of floating ice shelf that once connected Rothschild Island and Alexander Island disintegrated. As glaciers and ice shelves grow, retreat, and calve, the coastline shifts and new passages of water open up. These changes are closely monitored by satellite and by scientists on research expeditions, and new names are assigned to keep maps accurate for researchers and navigators.

### Fun Fact

Although Jonathan Amos has reported extensively on Antarctica for decades, he has never actually set foot on the continent. He joked that the naming "is quite the incentive to go and visit" — now there is literally a piece of home calling him to come see it in person.
