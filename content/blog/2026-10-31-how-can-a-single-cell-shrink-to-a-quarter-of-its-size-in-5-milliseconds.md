Take a deep breath and blink. In the time it takes you to do that once, a tiny pond creature can snap its whole body down to a quarter of its length — and then do it again, and again. Meet *Spirostomum ambiguum*, a single-celled organism that has just handed scientists a fresh puzzle about how living things move.

## A giant among single cells

*Spirostomum* is a ciliate — a one-celled creature fringed with hairlike paddles called cilia that it uses to swim. Despite being just one cell, it is enormous for its kind: some reach lengths of up to four millimetres, visible to the naked eye as a pale, worm-like thread drifting through muddy ponds and streams.

Living as a single cell, it has no muscles, no bones and no nerves. Yet it can contract at roughly 100 body lengths per second, a speed that researchers note is hundreds of times quicker than a human blink. Human muscle fibres can shorten by a similar proportion, but they take about ten times longer to do it.

## The secret is a fishnet

A team led by scientists at North Carolina State University used electron and fluorescence microscopes to look inside the organism. They found that calcium ions act as the trigger, flooding the cell and setting off the movement.

The actual contraction is carried out by unusual fibrous structures called myonemes, built from two calcium-binding proteins, centrin and Sfi1. In *Spirostomum*, these myonemes arrange themselves into a mesh that wraps around the outside of the cell like a fishnet. When calcium arrives, the net tightens inward — and the whole organism shrinks.

That fishnet geometry is clever: because the web squeezes evenly, the cell contracts uniformly, protecting the delicate internal compartments, the single cell's version of organs, while it moves at extraordinary speed.

## Gas versus electric

The comparison to our own bodies is striking. Human muscles run on ATP, a molecule that stores and releases energy by undergoing a chemical change — a bit like burning petrol. In *Spirostomum*, the calcium trigger behaves more like an electric current. It is not active at room temperature, and it keeps working without the constant refuelling that muscles need.

The discovery was published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, and it may point toward a new kind of fast-moving artificial muscle that does not depend on ATP.

## Fun fact

*Spirostomum* can repeat its lightning contraction over and over, which puzzles scientists: calcium-triggered reactions are usually one-shot. Working out how it resets after each snap could be the key to building machines that mimic the fastest twitch in nature.
