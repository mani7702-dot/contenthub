# How Did One New Skull Fragment Push Homo erectus Back 200,000 Years?

## A Quarry Full of Waiting Stories

South Africa's "Cradle of Humankind" is one of the richest places on Earth for the remains of our ancient relatives. At its Drimolen Main Quarry, scientists have been digging since 1992, and large numbers of fossils were pulled from the ground right up to 2019. The strange part? Many of those bones were never properly studied. They simply waited — carefully stored, but unknown to science.

That is where a team of archaeologists from La Trobe University, working with colleagues in South Africa and the United States, came in. They went back through the collection and described 18 hominin fossils that had never been published before. One of them changed the picture entirely.

## Meet the Oldest Adult of Its Kind

The new discovery is a skull fragment labelled DNH 127. It belonged to an adult *Homo erectus*, and it is about 2 million years old. That makes it roughly **200,000 years older** than any other adult *Homo erectus* fossil ever found — including the famous ones from Dmanisi in Georgia, which date to around 1.8 million years ago.

*Homo erectus* matters because it is the first species that researchers broadly agree belongs in our own genus, *Homo*, and it is likely directly ancestral to us. An earlier find at the same site, DNH 134, was a child aged between two and four. Nobody knew what a grown-up version looked like, until now. "This paper provides further confirmation that this species first appeared in southern Africa 2 million years ago," said lead researcher Dr. Jesse Martin.

## Two Cousins, Two Very Different Paths

The same batch of fossils also included remains of *Paranthropus robustus*, a stockier big-toothed relative that lived alongside our ancestors. Between about 2 million and 1.8 million years ago, these fossils show, *P. robustus* was slowly changing — growing larger teeth and stronger chewing muscles as the climate shifted.

For a while, it seems, *Paranthropus* was doing better than *Homo erectus*. One analysis suggests early *Homo erectus* may have been outnumbered by its cousin by as many as ten to one. Yet only one of those two lineages led to us.

The findings appear in the journal *Annals of Human Biology*.

## Fun fact

*Homo erectus* means "upright man" — the name was given in 1891, years before scientists had any idea how old the species really was. For decades, people wildly underestimated it. We are still correcting that estimate today.
