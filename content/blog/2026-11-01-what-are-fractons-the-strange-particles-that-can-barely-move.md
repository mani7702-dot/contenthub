## A particle that can't go anywhere on its own

Most particles we know are restless travellers. Light zooms, electrons race around atoms, and even the atoms in your chair are forever jiggling. Now imagine the opposite: a particle so stubborn that, all by itself, it simply cannot move.

That is a **fracton**. These are not atoms or electrons but *quasiparticles* — collective behaviours that emerge from many particles interacting inside a solid, the way a wave emerges from lots of water molecules. Fractons appear at the corners of magnetic "domain walls," the borders where regions of a material point their tiny magnetic compasses in different directions.

Their signature trait is extremely limited mobility. A lone fracton is essentially stuck; it can only shift when it interacts with another fracton. Now physicists led by Professor Johannes Reuther and Dr. Nils Niggemann at HZB in Germany have shown these shy particles can turn up in a far more realistic model of a solid, according to a study in *Nature Communications*.

## Why scientists are so curious

A particle that refuses to roam sounds useless — but that is exactly what makes fractons exciting. Because they are so hard to move, they may be able to store quantum information in a way that resists disruption, a long-sought goal for the next generation of computers.

Fractons had been predicted in **quantum spin liquids**: curious states of matter where the magnetic moments of electrons never settle into a fixed pattern, even at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature. The trouble? Those predictions leaned on highly abstract theories that were tough to test in a lab.

The new study closes part of that gap. Earlier attempts ran into a Goldilocks problem: crank up the quantum effects too much and the fractons vanished; dial them back and they survived only as ordinary, non-quantum objects. By improving how their simulations handle interactions between spins, the team found evidence the unusual phase can exist under realistic quantum conditions.

## The next step: spotting one for real

Nobody has directly observed a fracton in a quantum spin liquid yet. The team suggests one promising platform for a future experiment: **Rydberg atom simulators**, arrays of atoms that researchers can tune like tiny circuits to mimic exotic physics.

"When modeling this complex spin interaction, we benefit from personal exchanges with HZB colleagues in experimental solid-state physics," Reuther said — physics driven by conversation across chalkboards.

## Fun fact

The name "fracton" nods to *fractal* — these quasiparticles can be pinned down at points, lines or even flat planes, giving their allowed movements a strange, fractional quality unlike anything a normal particle experiences.
